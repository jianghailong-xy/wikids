Prepositions of position and movement are subtle: the same physical situation can take **different** prepositions depending on what you want to emphasise — a flat surface or a high obstacle, a line you follow or a space you push through, one thing directly covering another or simply below it. This unit sorts out the pairs that advanced learners most often confuse.

## A — Across, over

We can use **across** or **over** to talk about a position **on the other side of**, or **moving to the other side of** a road, bridge, border, river, etc.:

<SayIt text="Antonio lives in the house across / over the road from ours." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Once she was across / over the border, she knew she would be safe." />

We use **over** rather than **across** when we talk about reaching the other side of something that is **high**, or **higher than it is wide**. Compare:

<SayIt text="He jumped over the fence into the garden." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(A fence is high — you clear it vertically.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="He jumped across the stream." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(A stream is a flat surface — you cross it horizontally.)</p>

When we are talking about something we think of as a **flat surface**, or an **area** such as a country or sea, we prefer **across** rather than **over**:

<SayIt text="He suddenly saw Eva across the room." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The programme was broadcast across Canada." />

<Callout type="warning" title="All over vs all across">
  We prefer <strong>all over</strong> rather than <em>all across</em> to mean 'to or in many different parts of an area'. However, we commonly use <strong>across</strong>, or <strong>right across</strong> for emphasis:<br /><br />
  <SayIt text="The disease has now spread all over the world." />
  <span className="text-sm text-slate-500">(or <em>... (right) across the world.</em>)</span>
</Callout>

## B — Along, through

When we talk about **following a line** of some kind (a road, a river, etc.), we use **along**:

<SayIt text="They walked along the footpath until they came to a small bridge." />

We use **through** to emphasise that we are talking about movement in a **three-dimensional space**, with things all around, rather than a two-dimensional space, a flat surface or area:

<SayIt text="He pushed his way through the crowd of people to get to her." />

**Through** often suggests movement **from one side or end of the space to the other**. Compare:

<SayIt text="She walked through the forest to get to her grandmother's house." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Movement from one side of the forest to the other — a 3D space with trees all around.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="She spent a lot of her free time walking in the forest." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Movement within the forest, not necessarily from one side to the other.)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="The mental model">
  Think of <strong>along</strong> as tracing a <strong>line</strong> (a path, a corridor, a coastline) and <strong>through</strong> as moving inside a <strong>volume</strong> (a crowd, a tunnel, a forest). When in doubt, ask: is the thing around me flat and line-like, or does it surround me on all sides?
</Callout>

## C — Above, over; below, under; beneath, underneath

We can use either **above** or **over** when we say that one thing is at a **higher level** than another:

<SayIt text="Above / Over the door was a sign saying, 'Mind your head'." />

However, we prefer **above** when one thing is **not directly over** the other. Compare:

<SayIt text="They lived in a village in the mountains above the lake." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(The village is higher up but not directly over the lake.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The bird hovered just a few metres above / over the lake." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(The bird is directly over the lake — both prepositions work.)</p>

We use **over**, not **above**, when something **covers** something else and **touches** it:

<SayIt text="She put a quilt over the bed." />

And we usually use **over** when we are talking about **horizontal movement** at a higher level than something:

<SayIt text="I saw the helicopter fly out over the water, near the fishing boat." />

**Below** is the opposite of **above**; **under** is the opposite of **over**. The differences in the uses of **below** and **under** are similar to those between **above** and **over**:

<SayIt text="It's hard to believe that there is a railway line below / under the building." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(At a lower level — both work.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Her head was below the level of the table so nobody noticed her." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Not directly under the table — use below.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="She hid the presents under a blanket." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(The blanket covers and touches the presents — use under.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Zara ran under the bridge." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Horizontal movement at a lower level — use under.)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="Underneath and beneath">
  We can use <strong>underneath</strong> as an alternative to <strong>under</strong> as a preposition of place. <strong>Beneath</strong> is sometimes used as a more formal alternative to <strong>under</strong> or <strong>below</strong>.
</Callout>
