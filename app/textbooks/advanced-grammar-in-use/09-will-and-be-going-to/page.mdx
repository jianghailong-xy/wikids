<Lesson textbook="advanced-grammar-in-use" slug="09-will-and-be-going-to">

Both **will** and **be going to** point to the future, and much of the time either will do. This unit is about the places where they part company: a prediction that rests on your **opinion** versus one you can read off **present evidence**; a decision taken **as you speak** versus one **already made**; and the fine print of what happens when either turns up inside an **if-sentence**. Get these distinctions under control and your future reference stops sounding random and starts carrying meaning.

## A — When either form works

We can use **either will or be going to** to talk about something that is **planned**, or something we think is **likely to happen** in the future:

<SayIt text="We will study climate change in a later part of the course." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>We are going to study …</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Where will you stay in Berlin?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>Where are you going to stay …?</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The south of the city won't be affected by the power cuts." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>… isn't going to be affected …</em>)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="A leaning, not a rule">
  When both are genuinely possible, we <strong>often prefer be going to in informal contexts</strong> — conversation, messages, speech — and lean towards <strong>will in more formal ones</strong> (see also <strong>D</strong>). The rest of this unit is about the cases where the choice is <em>not</em> free.
</Callout>

## B — Predictions: opinion versus present evidence

We use **will** rather than be going to to make a prediction based on our own **opinion or experience** — there's nothing in front of us forcing the conclusion:

<SayIt text="Why not come over at the weekend? The children will enjoy seeing you again." />
<br />
<SayIt text="'Shall I ask Lamar?' 'No, she won't want to be disturbed.'" />

We use **be going to** rather than will when we make a prediction based on some **present evidence** — you can point to what makes you say it:

<SayIt text="The sky's gone really dark. There's going to be a storm." />
<br />
<SayIt text="'What's the matter with her?' 'It looks like she's going to faint.'" />

<Callout type="warning" title="The test for B">
  Ask yourself: <em>why do I think so?</em> If the answer is <strong>'from what I know / believe / expect'</strong> → <strong>will</strong>. If the answer is <strong>'because of something I can see right now'</strong> → <strong>be going to</strong>. Dark sky, screeching brakes, a pale face: that's evidence, so <em>going to</em>.
</Callout>

## C — Predicting with I bet, I expect, I think …

To predict the future we often use **will** with **I bet** (informal), **I expect, I hope, I imagine, I reckon** (informal), **I think, I wonder** and **I'm sure**, and in questions with **think** and **reckon**:

<SayIt text="I imagine the stadium will be full for the match on Saturday." />
<br />
<SayIt text="That cheese smells awful. I bet nobody will eat it." />
<br />
<SayIt text="When do you think you'll finish work?" />
<br />
<SayIt text="Do you reckon he'll say yes?" />

<Callout type="info" title="Going to isn't shut out here">
  <strong>Be going to</strong> can also be used with these phrases, <strong>particularly in informal contexts</strong>: <em>I reckon it's going to rain</em> is perfectly natural in speech. So <em>I think / I bet / I reckon</em> pull towards <strong>will</strong> by default, but they don't forbid <em>going to</em>.
</Callout>

## D — Deciding now versus a decision already made

We use **will** when we make a decision **at the moment of speaking**, and **be going to** for decisions about the future that have **already been made**. Compare:

<SayIt text="I'll pick him up at eight." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(an offer; making an arrangement <em>now</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I'm going to collect the children at eight." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(this was <em>previously</em> arranged)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="'Pineapples are on special offer this week.' 'In that case, I'll buy two.'" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(deciding on the spot → will)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="When I've saved up enough money, I'm going to buy a smartphone." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(the plan is already in place → going to)</p>

However, in a **formal style**, we use **will** rather than be going to to talk about future events that have been **previously arranged in some detail**. Compare:

<SayIt text="Are you going to talk at the meeting tonight?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(everyday, informal → going to)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The meeting will begin at 9 am. Refreshments will be available from 8:30 onwards." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(a formal, detailed schedule → will)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="Watch the trigger words">
  Phrases like <em>In that case …</em>, <em>Right, I'll …</em>, <em>OK, I'll …</em> almost always introduce a decision taken as you speak — <strong>will</strong>. A plan you refer back to (<em>I'm going to …, it's all arranged</em>) is already settled — <strong>be going to</strong>.
</Callout>

## E — Will and be going to in if-sentences

We can use **will or be going to** with **little difference in meaning** in the main clause of an if-sentence when we say that something (often something **negative**) is **conditional** on something else:

<SayIt text="You'll / You're going to knock that glass over if you're not careful." />

When the future event **does not depend** on the action in the if-clause, we use **be going to**, not will. This kind of sentence is mainly found in spoken English. Compare:

<SayIt text="I'm going to open a bottle of lemonade, if you want some." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= I'm going to open one anyway. Do you want some?)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I'll open a bottle of lemonade if you want some." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= If you say you want some, I'll open it.)</p>

However, we use **will**, not be going to, when the main clause is an **offer, request, promise, etc.** or refers to **ability**:

<SayIt text="If Erik phones, I'll let you know." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(an offer; <em>I'm going to let you know</em> would suggest 'I intend to tell you when Erik phones')</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="If you look to your left, you'll see the lake." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= you'll be able to see it — ability; <em>you're going to see</em> would suggest 'I know this is what's there')</p>

… and when one thing is the **logical consequence** of another:

<SayIt text="If you don't switch on the monitor first, the computer won't come on." />

<Callout type="warning" title="The if-sentence shortlist for will">
  Even inside an if-sentence, keep <strong>will</strong> for four jobs: <strong>offers/requests/promises</strong> (<em>If you ask, I'll help</em>), <strong>ability</strong> (<em>you'll be able to see</em>), <strong>logical consequences</strong> (<em>the computer won't come on</em>), and the conditional warning where <strong>both</strong> are fine (<em>you'll / you're going to knock that over</em>). Reach for <strong>be going to</strong> when the main-clause event is a plan that would happen regardless of the if-clause.
</Callout>
