You already know the basic futures — **will** for predictions and decisions, **be going to** for plans and present evidence. This unit adds three more forms that let you place yourself at a precise point on the future timeline: the **future continuous** (*will be doing*), the **future perfect** (*will have done*), and the **future perfect continuous** (*will have been doing*). Master them and you can say not just *what* will happen, but what will be **in progress**, what will be **finished**, and what will have been **going on for some time** by a given future moment.

## A — Future continuous: will be doing

We use the future continuous to talk about:

**(i) something that is predicted to start before a particular point of future time, and that may continue after this point** (often the result of a previous decision or arrangement):

<SayIt text="When it goes into orbit, the spacecraft will be carrying 30 kilos of plutonium." />
<SayIt text="Anna will be helping us to organise the party." />

**(ii) a future activity that is part of the normal course of events or that is one of a repeated or regular series of events:**

<SayIt text="Dr Lin will be giving the same talk in room 103 at ten next Thursday." />
<SayIt text="Will you be driving to work, as usual?" />

<Callout type="info" title="Future continuous or present continuous?">
  We can often use <strong>either</strong> the future continuous or the present continuous when we talk about arranged activities or events in the future (see also Unit 10). Compare:<br /><br />
  <em>We will be leaving for Istanbul at 7:00 in the evening.</em> (timetabled; or <em>… are leaving …</em>)<br /><br />
  But: <em>When the race starts later this afternoon the drivers <strong>will be hoping</strong> for drier weather than last year.</em> — <strong>not</strong> <em>… are hoping …</em>, because this is not reporting the details of a programme or timetable.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Will you be staying or will you stay?">
  When we <strong>don't want to indicate willingness, intention, invitation, etc.</strong>, we prefer to use the future continuous instead of <em>will</em>. For example, if guests have stayed longer than you wanted, and you don't know when they are leaving, you might ask:<br /><br />
  ✅ <em>Will you <strong>be staying</strong> with us again tonight?</em> (asking about their plans — neutral)<br />
  ❌ <em>Will you <strong>stay</strong> with us again tonight?</em> (they might think this is an invitation — or a plea to leave!)<br /><br />
  The future continuous strips away the overtones of willingness, intention, or invitation that plain <em>will</em> often carries. Use it whenever you want a <strong>neutral question about plans</strong>.
</Callout>

## B — Future perfect and future perfect continuous: will have done and will have been doing

We use the **future perfect** to say that something will be **ended, completed, or achieved** by a particular point in the future:

<SayIt text="By the time you get home I will have cleaned the house from top to bottom." />
<SayIt text="I'm sure his awful behaviour will soon have been forgotten." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Passive form: <em>will have been + past participle</em>.)</p>

We use the **future perfect continuous** to emphasise the **duration** of an activity in progress at a particular point in the future:

<SayIt text="Next year I will have been working in the company for 30 years." />

<Callout type="tip" title="The time marker rule">
  With both the future perfect and future perfect continuous we <strong>usually mention the future time</strong> (<em>By the time you get home …, Next year …, In two years' time …</em>). Without a time anchor, the form feels incomplete — the listener is left waiting for the <em>by when</em>.
</Callout>

## C — These forms for deduction: what we believe is happening around now

The future continuous, future perfect, and future perfect continuous can also be used to say **what we believe or imagine is happening around now** — a present deduction expressed through a future form:

<SayIt text="We could ask to borrow Joe's car. He won't be using it today — he went to work by bike." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Future continuous — deduction about a present situation.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Most people will have forgotten the fire by now." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Future perfect — deduction that the forgetting is complete by now.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Tennis fans will have been queuing at Wimbledon all day to buy tickets." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Future perfect continuous — deduction about an ongoing activity, emphasising its duration.)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="Why the future form for the present?">
  These are sometimes called <strong>modals of deduction</strong>. The <em>will</em> here expresses confidence in your belief about the present, not a future time reference. The continuous or perfect shape (<em>be using</em>, <em>have forgotten</em>) then positions the action relative to <strong>now</strong> — ongoing or completed — exactly as the present continuous and present perfect do.
</Callout>

## D — Future perfect continuous for past deduction

We can use the future perfect continuous to say what we **think was happening at a point in the past**:

<SayIt text="Motorist Vicky Hesketh will have been asking herself whether speed cameras are a good idea after she was fined £100 last week for driving at 33 mph in a 30 mph zone." />

<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">Here the speaker is <strong>not</strong> talking about the future at all. The form expresses a confident inference about what must have been going on in the past — <em>she must have been asking herself …</em> is a close paraphrase.</p>
