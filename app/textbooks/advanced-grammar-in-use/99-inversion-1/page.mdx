In a statement the verb normally follows the subject: *Alex stood in the doorway*. Sometimes, though, we deliberately reverse that order — we call this **inversion**. This unit gathers the everyday cases: the narrative *in came the doctor*, the conversational *here comes the bus*, the formal *were the talks to fail*, and the comparative *than do their children*. Getting them right is one of the clearest signals of a controlled, advanced style.

## A — What inversion is, and its two types

In statements it is usual for the verb to follow the subject, but sometimes this word order is reversed. There are **two main types** of inversion:

**1. The whole verb comes before the subject** (here inversion is often *optional*):

<SayIt text="Alex stood in the doorway." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">→ In the doorway <strong>stood Alex</strong>. <span className="text-slate-400">(Not <em>In the doorway Alex stood</em>.)</span></p>

**2. Only the auxiliary comes before the subject**, and the rest of the verb phrase follows it (here inversion is usually *necessary*):

<SayIt text="I had rarely seen such a view." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">→ Rarely <strong>had I seen</strong> such a view. <span className="text-slate-400">(Not <em>Rarely I had seen</em> ...)</span></p>

<Callout type="info" title="Inversion is a tool for emphasis">
  Inversion brings about <em>fronting</em> — re-ordering a sentence to give emphasis to a particular place. Often it pushes an element to the end, where it lands with extra weight: <em>In the doorway stood <strong>Alex</strong></em> throws the spotlight onto Alex. Think of inversion less as a rule and more as a way of steering the reader's attention.
</Callout>

## B — Here comes ..., There goes ..., and in came the doctor

In conversation we use **There comes / Here comes + noun** and **There goes + noun**, with inversion of verb and subject, to talk about things and people moving **towards** or **away from** the speaker:

<SayIt text="Here comes the bus." />
<br />
<SayIt text="There goes Per Alvin, the conductor." />

**Here comes ...** is also used to say that something is about to happen soon, and **There goes ...** to talk about things (particularly money) being lost, or to say that something such as a phone or a doorbell is ringing:

<SayIt text="Here comes lunch." />
<br />
<SayIt text="My bike's been stolen! There goes £100!" />
<br />
<SayIt text="There goes the phone. Can you answer it?" />

We also put the verb before the subject when we use **adverbs of movement** — *along, away, back, down, in, off, out, up* — with verbs such as *come, fly, go*. This pattern is common in **narrative**, to mark a change in events:

<SayIt text="The door opened and in came the doctor." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Less formally: <em>... and the doctor came in.</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="As soon as I let go of the string, up went the balloon, high into the sky." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Less formally: <em>... the balloon went up ...</em>)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="Noun subject vs pronoun subject">
  The word order changes depending on the subject: <br />
  ✅ <strong>Noun</strong> subject → adverb + <strong>verb + noun</strong>: <em>In came <strong>the doctor</strong>. · Off went <strong>the balloon</strong>.</em> <br />
  ✅ <strong>Pronoun</strong> subject → adverb + <strong>pronoun + verb</strong>: <em>In <strong>he</strong> came. · Off <strong>she</strong> went.</em> <br />
  ❌ Not <em>In came he</em>, ❌ not <em>Off went she</em>.
</Callout>

<p className="text-sm text-slate-500">For more on inversion after adverbs of place and direction, see Unit 76A.</p>

## C — Inversion instead of if (conditionals)

We can use clauses with inversion instead of certain kinds of **if-clause** (see Unit 83). Drop the *if*, then bring **were**, **should** or **had** to the front. Compare:

<div className="not-prose my-6 overflow-hidden rounded-2xl border-2 border-violet-200 shadow-sm">
  <div className="grid grid-cols-2 text-sm">
    <div className="bg-sky-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-sky-900">With <em>if</em> — neutral</div>
    <div className="bg-violet-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-violet-900">With inversion — more formal</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">It would be a serious setback, <strong>if</strong> the talks <strong>were</strong> to fail.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 bg-violet-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">It would be a serious setback, <strong>were</strong> the talks to fail.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>If</strong> you <strong>should</strong> need more information, please telephone our main office.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 bg-violet-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>Should</strong> you need more information, please telephone our main office.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>If</strong> Andy <strong>had</strong> asked, I would have been able to help.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 bg-violet-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>Had</strong> Andy asked, I would have been able to help.</div>
  </div>
</div>

The sentences with inversion are rather more formal than those with *if*.

<Callout type="warning" title="Never contract the negative">
  In negative clauses with inversion we do <strong>not</strong> use contracted forms. The <em>not</em> goes after the subject: <br />
  ✅ <strong>Had the plane not been</strong> diverted, they would have arrived early. <br />
  ❌ <em>Hadn't the plane been diverted ...</em>
</Callout>

## D — Inversion after as and than

In formal written language we commonly use inversion after **as** and **than** in comparisons:

<SayIt text="Paper was invented in China, as was the process of printing." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Most young people did not vote in the election, as was the case in 2010." />
<br />
<SayIt text="I believed, as did my colleagues, that the plan would work." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Research shows that parents watch more television than do their children." />

<Callout type="warning" title="But not when the subject is a pronoun">
  We do <strong>not</strong> invert subject and verb after <em>as</em> or <em>than</em> when the subject is a <strong>pronoun</strong>: <br />
  ✅ We now know a lot more about the universe <strong>than we did</strong> ten years ago. <br />
  ❌ <em>... than did we</em> ten years ago.
</Callout>
