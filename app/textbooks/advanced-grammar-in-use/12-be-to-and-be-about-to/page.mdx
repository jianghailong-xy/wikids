Both structures in this unit map the near future, but from opposite registers. **Be to + infinitive** is the language of the newsroom, the memo and the official notice — planned events, formal arrangements and orders. **Be about to + infinitive** is the language of conversation — something on the very edge of happening. The pay-off of the unit is a single, sharp restriction on the first structure: *be to* only works for futures that **people can control**.

## A — Be to + infinitive: news, arrangements and orders

**Be to + infinitive** is commonly used in **news reports** to talk about events that are likely to happen in the near future — often in the **passive** to sound formal and impersonal:

<SayIt text="Police officers are to visit every home in the area." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The main Rome-to-Naples railway line is to be reopened today." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(passive form)</p>

It is also used to talk about **formal or official arrangements**, **formal instructions**, and to **give orders**:

<SayIt text="You are not to leave the school without my permission." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The European Parliament is to introduce a new law on safety at work." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Children are not to be left unsupervised in the museum." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(passive form — the passive makes orders and instructions more impersonal)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="The one rule that governs everything: it must be controllable">
  We use <strong>be to + infinitive</strong> only for future events that <strong>can be controlled by people</strong> — an arrangement, a decision, a plan. For things no one can control (weather, natural phenomena, the movement of a comet), use <strong>will</strong> instead.
</Callout>

Compare a controllable future (either form works) with an uncontrollable one (only *will*):

<SayIt text="In the next few years, thousands of speed cameras are to appear on major roads." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(a planned rollout people control — <em>or</em> ... <strong>will appear</strong> ...)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Scientists say they can't predict when or where the disease will appear again." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>the disease is to appear</em> — its appearance can't be controlled)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The President is to return to Brazil later today." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(a scheduled trip — <em>or</em> ... <strong>will return</strong> ...)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The comet will return to our solar system in around 500 years." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>The comet is to return</em> — the movement of the comet can't be controlled)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="One exception: the future seen from the past">
  When <strong>be to + infinitive</strong> refers to the <strong>future from the past</strong> (see Unit&nbsp;14B), we often use it to describe what happened to someone, <strong>whether or not they could influence events</strong> — so the controllable rule is relaxed:<br /><br />
  <em>Matthew Flinders sailed past Tasmania in 1770, but it <strong>was to be</strong> a further 30 years before he landed there.</em><br />
  <em>Clare Atkins <strong>was to write</strong> two more books about her experiences in Africa before her death in 1997.</em>
</Callout>

## B — Be to + infinitive in if-clauses

We often use **be to + infinitive** in **if-clauses** to say that something must happen first (in the **main clause**) before something else — the goal — can happen (in the **if-clause**):

<SayIt text="If the human race is to survive, we must look at environmental problems now." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The law needs to be revised if justice is to be done." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(passive form)</p>

Compare **be to + infinitive** with the **present simple** for the future in if-clauses — they are not interchangeable, because they arrange cause and effect differently:

<SayIt text="If Lopez is to win gold at the next Olympics, he needs to work on his fitness." />
<br />
<SayIt text="If Lopez wins gold at the next Olympics, he has said that he will retire from athletics." />

<Callout type="tip" title="The cause and effect are reversed">
  With these two tenses the order of <strong>cause</strong> and <strong>effect</strong> in the if-sentence flips:<br /><br />
  • <em>If Lopez <strong>is to win</strong> gold</em> (= the <strong>effect</strong>/goal), <em>he needs to work</em> (= the <strong>cause</strong>)<br />
  • <em>If Lopez <strong>wins</strong> gold</em> (= the <strong>cause</strong>), <em>he has said he will retire</em> (= the <strong>effect</strong>)<br /><br />
  So <em>is to</em> puts the desired outcome in the if-clause and the requirement in the main clause; the present simple does the opposite.
</Callout>

## C — Be about to + infinitive

We use **be about to + infinitive** mainly in **conversation** to say that something will (or won't) happen in the **very near future**:

<SayIt text="We're about to eat. Do you want to join us?" />
<br />
<SayIt text="Appearing on TV might make her famous, but it's not about to make her rich." />
<br />
<SayIt text="'Why don't you switch it off and turn it back on again?' 'Yes, I was about to try that when you came in.'" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>Yes, I was to try ...</em> — for the very near future seen from the past, use <em>was about to</em>, not <em>was to</em>)</p>

  Read a few news headlines and rewrite each as a full sentence, choosing <em>be to</em> or <em>will</em> by the one test that matters — <strong>can people control this event?</strong> A minister's resignation, a factory closure, a rail strike: <em>be to</em>.
