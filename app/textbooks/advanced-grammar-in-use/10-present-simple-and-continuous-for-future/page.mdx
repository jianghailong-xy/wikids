Units 1 and 2 laid out how the present simple and present continuous work for the **here and now**. Unit 9 gave you *will* and *be going to* for the future. This unit brings the two halves together: when do the **present** tenses themselves point to the future? That happens in four specific slots — a timetable, a time clause, a conditional clause, and a firm personal arrangement. Get them right and your future reference starts sounding native.

## A — Present simple for timetabled future events

We can often use **either the present simple or will** to talk about future events that are part of some **timetabled or programmed arrangement or routine**. However, we **prefer the present simple** for fixed, unchangeable events. Compare:

<SayIt text="Does the sale finish on Thursday or Friday?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>Will the sale finish …?</em> — both possible, but the simple feels more definite.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The sun rises at 5:16 tomorrow." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(more likely than <em>The sun will rise …</em> — a fixed, unchangeable astronomical event.)</p>

We **avoid** the present simple when we talk about **less formal or less routine arrangements**, or **predictions**. Instead we use **will**, **be going to**, or the **present continuous**:

<SayIt text="Are you staying in to watch TV tonight, or are you coming dancing?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1"><span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>Do you stay to watch TV tonight, or do you come …?</em></p>
<br />
<SayIt text="It's only a problem in Britain now, but it will affect the rest of Europe soon." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1"><span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>… but it affects the rest of Europe soon.</em></p>

<Callout type="tip" title="The timetable test">
  If you could find the event printed on a ticket, a timetable board, or a programme — a train, a film, a class, a sunrise — the present simple is at home. If the event depends on someone's <strong>personal decision</strong> or is simply a <strong>guess about the future</strong>, reach for <em>will</em>, <em>be going to</em>, or the present continuous instead.
</Callout>

## B — Present simple in time clauses (when, after, before …)

We use the **present simple** — **not** will — to refer to the future in **time clauses** with conjunctions such as **after, as soon as, before, by the time, when, while, until**:

<SayIt text="When you see Ben, tell him he still owes me some money." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1"><span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>When you will see Ben …</em></p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I should be finished by the time you get back." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1"><span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>… by the time you will get back.</em></p>

<Callout type="warning" title="A trap even advanced learners fall into">
  The main clause <strong>can</strong> have <em>will</em>: <em>I'll call you <strong>when I arrive</strong>.</em> The time clause itself (<em>when I arrive</em>) stays in the present simple. The rule is about the clause introduced by the conjunction — not the whole sentence.
</Callout>

## C — Present simple in conditional clauses + suppose / supposing / what if

We use the **present simple** — not will — in **conditional clauses** with **if, in case, provided, unless**:

<SayIt text="Provided the right software is available, I should be able to solve the problem." />
<br />
<SayIt text="I'll bring some sandwiches in case we don't find anywhere decent to eat." />

We also use the present simple when we talk about possible future events with **suppose, supposing**, and **what if** at the beginning of a sentence. Note that the **past simple** can be used with a similar meaning:

<SayIt text="Suppose we miss the bus — how will we get home?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>Suppose we missed the bus …</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="What if the train's late? Where shall I meet you then?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>What if the train was late?</em>)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="Past simple with suppose / what if">
  Using the past simple after <em>suppose</em>, <em>supposing</em> or <em>what if</em> makes the event sound <strong>more remote or less likely</strong> — the same distancing effect you see in second conditionals. Both are correct; the choice is about how probable you think the event is.
</Callout>

## D — Present continuous for planned future events

We can often use **either the present continuous or be going to** with a similar meaning to talk about **planned future events**. The present continuous indicates that we have a **firm intention** or have made a **definite decision** to do something, although this may not already be arranged:

<SayIt text="Are you seeing the doctor again next week?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>Are you going to see …?</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I'm not asking Tom to the party." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>I'm not going to ask …</em>)</p>

However, we **don't** use the present continuous for the future —

**When we make or report predictions about activities or events over which we have no control** (we can't arrange these):

<SayIt text="I think it's going to rain soon." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1"><span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>I think it's raining soon.</em></p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Scientists say that the satellite won't cause any damage when it falls to Earth." />

**When we talk about permanent future situations:**

<SayIt text="People are going to live / will live longer in the future." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1"><span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>People are living longer in the future.</em></p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Her new house is going to have / will have three floors." />

<Callout type="tip" title="Go and come: skip the double 'going to'">
  Many people avoid <strong>be going to + go / come</strong> and use the present continuous forms of <em>go</em> and <em>come</em> instead:<br />
  ✅ <em>I'm going to town on Saturday.</em> (rather than <em>I'm going to go to town …</em>)<br />
  ✅ <em>Are you coming home for lunch?</em> (rather than <em>Are you going to come …?</em>)
</Callout>
