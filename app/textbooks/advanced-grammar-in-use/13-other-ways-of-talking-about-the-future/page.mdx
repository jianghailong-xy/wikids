*Will* and *be going to* (Unit 9) are only the beginning. English has a rich second layer of ways to point at the future — fixed phrases like *be on the verge of*, *be due to* and *be set to*; ordinary verbs of intention such as *aim*, *hope* and *intend* that take a *to*-infinitive; and, at the formal edge, *shall*. This unit maps that layer: what each phrase signals, which verbs allow a continuous form, how the same expressions look at the future *from a point in the past*, and where *shall* still survives.

## A — Fixed phrases: about to happen, expected, likely, ready

Some phrases mean much the same as **be about to + infinitive** (see Unit 12C). Use **be on the verge of … / on the brink of … / on the point of …** (+ **-ing** or a **noun**) to say that something will happen very soon:

<SayIt text="People are on the verge of starvation as the drought continues." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Scientists are on the brink of making major advances in the fight against AIDS." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Exhausted, mentally and physically, she was on the point of collapse." />

<Callout type="tip" title="What follows the phrase — and what it signals">
  These phrases take an <strong>-ing</strong> form or a <strong>noun</strong>, never a bare <em>to</em>-infinitive: <em>on the brink of <strong>making</strong></em>, <em>on the point of <strong>collapse</strong></em>. And <strong>be on the brink of</strong> in particular usually points to something <strong>important, exciting, or very bad</strong> — a war, a breakthrough, a disaster — not everyday events.
</Callout>

Three more phrases each carry their own shade of meaning:

- **be due to (+ infinitive)** — something is *expected* to happen at a particular time;
- **be sure / bound to (+ infinitive)** — something is *likely or certain* to happen;
- **be set to (+ infinitive)** — something is *ready* to happen.

<SayIt text="The company's chief executive is due to retire next year, but following today's announcement of further losses she is sure to be asked to leave sooner." />
<br />
<SayIt text="'Will there be somewhere to get a coffee at the station?' 'Oh, yes, there's bound to be.'" />
<br />
<SayIt text="Her new film is set to be a great success." />

<Callout type="warning" title="Trap: due to + noun is not about the future">
  <strong>Due to + noun</strong> gives the <strong>reason</strong> for something — it does not talk about the future: <em>Due to fog, all flights from the airport have been cancelled.</em> Only <strong>be due to + infinitive</strong> ( <em>is due to retire</em> ) has the future meaning.
</Callout>

## B — Verbs of intention with a to-infinitive

We use a number of verbs with a **to-infinitive** to talk about **intentions**:

<SayIt text="We guarantee to refund your money if you are dissatisfied with the computer." />

With a smaller set of these verbs — the ones marked **\*** below — you can use **either the present simple + to-infinitive** *or* **the present continuous + to-infinitive** to talk about an intention:

<SayIt text="I aim to get to Bangkok by the end of June." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>I'm aiming to get …</em>; <em>I was aiming to get …</em> is also possible, but more tentative)</p>

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-emerald-700">Verbs of intention + to-infinitive</p>
  <p className="mt-2 text-sm text-emerald-900">
    <strong>aim*</strong> · agree · <strong>expect*</strong> · <strong>hope*</strong> · <strong>intend*</strong> · mean · <strong>plan*</strong> · promise · <strong>propose*</strong> · resolve · undertake · <strong>want*</strong>
  </p>
  <p className="mt-2 text-sm text-emerald-800">The <strong>*</strong> verbs also work in the <strong>present continuous</strong> + <em>to</em>-infinitive (<em>I'm aiming to …</em>). The unmarked verbs — <em>agree, mean, promise, resolve, undertake</em> — and <em>guarantee</em> stay in the <strong>present simple</strong>.</p>
</div>

In speech and in journalism especially, some people use **be looking + to-infinitive** to mean *planning a course of action*:

<SayIt text="We're looking to create 3,000 jobs in the city over the next year." />

<Callout type="warning" title="It's looking to + infinitive, not to + -ing">
  Keep the bare infinitive after <em>looking</em>: <em>We're looking <strong>to create</strong> jobs</em> — <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>looking to creating</em>.
</Callout>

## C — The same phrases in the past: the future seen from the past

When the phrases and verbs in **A** and **B** are used with **past tense forms**, they are usually concerned with **future events seen from the past** (see also Unit 14):

<SayIt text="It was his 64th birthday in 2006 and he was due to retire the following year." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Nathan had resolved to become fluent in Spanish before he left university." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The new management had been looking to create 20 new jobs." />

<Callout type="info" title="Looking back at a 'then-future'">
  <em>Was due to retire</em>, <em>had resolved to become</em> and <em>had been looking to create</em> all describe something that lay in the future <strong>from the standpoint of that past moment</strong> — whether or not it actually happened. Unit 14 develops this idea with <em>was going to</em>, <em>was to</em> and <em>was about to</em>.
</Callout>

## D — shall and shan't

Some people use **shall** (and **shan't**) instead of **will** (and **won't**) in statements about the future with **I** and **we**. However, it is more common to use **will** — particularly its contracted form **'ll** — and **won't**:

<SayIt text="He was a good friend and we shall miss him greatly." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(more commonly <em>… we'll miss …</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I'm just going to buy a newspaper. I shan't be long." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(more commonly <em>I won't …</em>)</p>

In current English we don't usually use **shall / shan't** with **other subjects** to talk about the future — although this survives in **formal rules** and in **older literary styles**:

<SayIt text="The match referee shall be the sole judge of fair play." />
<br />
<SayIt text="All people of the world shall live together as brothers." />

<Callout type="tip" title="Quick decision for shall vs will">
  With <em>I / we</em>: both are possible, but <strong>'ll / won't</strong> is the everyday choice. With <em>he / she / it / they / a noun</em>: use <strong>will / won't</strong> for ordinary futures, and reserve <strong>shall</strong> for regulations (<em>Members shall pay the fee by 1 May</em>) or a deliberately old-fashioned, ceremonial tone.
</Callout>
