Unit 1 sorted out *what* the present continuous and present simple mean. This unit is about a different skill: the surprising places where a present tense is chosen for **effect** — telling a story, calling a match live, passing on news, writing a headline, or complaining about someone. Same two tenses, used in ways the meaning-based rules alone don't predict.

## A — The 'historic present': stories, jokes and anecdotes

In informal spoken English we often tell a story or a joke in the **present** simple and present continuous, even though the events are clearly finished. It creates the impression that the events are happening *now* — it makes the telling more direct and exciting, and holds the listener's attention.

<SayIt text="She goes up to this man and looks straight into his eyes. He's not wearing his glasses, and he doesn't recognise her ..." />
<br />
<SayIt text="This man's playing golf when a kangaroo bounds up to him, grabs his club and hits his ball about half a mile ..." />

The pattern inside a story is consistent: the **main events**, told in sequence, go in the **present simple** (*goes, looks, grabs, hits*); **longer background events** running underneath them go in the **present continuous** (*is playing, isn't wearing*).

<div className="not-prose my-6 overflow-hidden rounded-2xl border-2 border-violet-200 shadow-sm">
  <div className="grid grid-cols-2 text-sm">
    <div className="bg-violet-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-violet-900">Present simple</div>
    <div className="bg-sky-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-sky-900">Present continuous</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">the main events, one after another</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">the longer background they happen against</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 bg-violet-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">She <strong>goes</strong> up · <strong>grabs</strong> his club · <strong>hits</strong> his ball</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 bg-sky-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">He's <strong>playing</strong> golf · he's not <strong>wearing</strong> his glasses</div>
  </div>
</div>

In narratives and anecdotes the present simple can also be used to **highlight a single event** — to make it leap out. It often appears right after past tenses, with a phrase such as **suddenly** or **all of a sudden**:

<SayIt text="I was sitting in the park, reading a newspaper, when all of a sudden this dog jumps at me." />

<Callout type="tip" title="Feel the switch">
  Notice the gear-change in that last example: <em>was sitting … reading</em> (past, setting the scene) → <em>jumps</em> (present simple, the punch). Dropping into the present at the key moment is exactly how a good storyteller makes you flinch.
</Callout>

## B — Live commentaries

We also use the present simple and present continuous in **live commentaries** — sports events above all — when the report happens at the **same time** as the action. The division of labour is the same as in section A: quick, completed actions in the **simple**; ongoing or background description in the **continuous**.

<SayIt text="King serves to the left-hand court and Adams makes a wonderful return. She's playing magnificent tennis in this match ..." />

<Callout type="info" title="Why not the continuous for every move?">
  A serve or a return is over in an instant, so the commentator uses the simple — <em>serves, makes</em> — to keep pace with the play. <em>She's playing magnificent tennis</em> is a judgement about the whole match so far, so it takes the continuous. Speed of the action, not the clock, decides.
</Callout>

## C — Reporting what you've heard, read or been told

A set of fixed present-simple phrases introduces news you've **heard, read, seen on television, or been told**. The commonest are:

<div className="not-prose my-6 grid gap-3 sm:grid-cols-2">
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-emerald-700">from what you've read / seen</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-emerald-900">It says here …, I see …, I hear …</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-sky-200 bg-sky-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-sky-700">from what you understand</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-sky-900">I gather …, I understand …</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-amber-200 bg-amber-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-amber-700">from what people say</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-amber-900">They say …, Anna says …</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-rose-200 bg-rose-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-rose-700">from what you were told</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-rose-900">Sophia tells me …</p>
  </div>
</div>

<SayIt text="I gather you're worried about Pedro." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Sophia tells me you're thinking of emigrating." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Professor Hendriks is at the conference and I hear she's an excellent speaker." />

<Callout type="tip" title="Past tense works too">
  These phrases also have a past form for news that reached you earlier: <em>It <strong>said</strong> here …</em>, <em>I <strong>heard</strong> …</em>, <em>Sophia <strong>told me</strong> …</em>. The present-tense version simply frames the news as fresh — as if you're passing it on the moment you got it.
</Callout>

## D — Headlines, and the contents of books and films

The present simple is standard in **news headlines** for events that have **recently happened** — it is punchy and saves space:

<div className="not-prose my-6 grid gap-3 sm:grid-cols-2">
  <div className="rounded-xl border-l-4 border-slate-800 bg-slate-100 px-4 py-3 font-bold uppercase tracking-wide text-slate-800">Second quake <span className="text-rose-600">hits</span> Japan</div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-l-4 border-slate-800 bg-slate-100 px-4 py-3 font-bold uppercase tracking-wide text-slate-800">Fire <span className="text-rose-600">breaks out</span> in hotel room</div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-l-4 border-slate-800 bg-slate-100 px-4 py-3 font-bold uppercase tracking-wide text-slate-800">Scientists <span className="text-rose-600">find</span> ice on the Moon</div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-l-4 border-slate-800 bg-slate-100 px-4 py-3 font-bold uppercase tracking-wide text-slate-800">Foreign minister <span className="text-rose-600">resigns</span></div>
</div>

The same simple present describes the **contents of books, films and newspapers** — what a writer does on the page, or what happens in the story:

<SayIt text="Thompson gives a list of the largest European companies in Chapter 6." />
<br />
<SayIt text="At the beginning of the book, three men find $4 million in a crashed plane." />
<br />
<SayIt text="In the film, Loni Baranski takes the role of a private detective." />

<Callout type="info" title="Why the present for a finished book?">
  The events in a novel or film are 'timeless' — they happen every time someone reads or watches. So we treat them as generally true and use the present simple, exactly as we do for facts. <em>Hamlet <strong>kills</strong> Polonius</em>, not <em>killed</em>.
</Callout>

## E — always / forever + -ing, and regular actions at a set time

We can put the present continuous with **always, constantly, continually** or **forever** to stress that something happens so often it is **characteristic** of a person, group or thing — very often with a note of **disapproval** or complaint:

<SayIt text="A: I think I'll stay here after all.  B: You're constantly changing your mind." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Jacob is a really kind person. He's always offering to help me with my work." />

The **past** continuous works the same way for a past habit that irritated you:

<SayIt text="Was Olivia always asking you for money, too?" />

<Callout type="warning" title="always + simple vs always + continuous">
  These say different things: <br />
  ✅ She <strong>always arrives</strong> on time. <span className="text-slate-500">(a neutral fact — it's just true)</span> <br />
  ✅ She's <strong>always arriving</strong> late! <span className="text-slate-500">(a complaint — too often, and it annoys me)</span> <br />
  The continuous adds the attitude; the simple just states the habit.
</Callout>

Finally, the present continuous can describe **what you regularly do at a particular time** — a routine pinned to a moment on the clock:

<SayIt text="At eight o'clock I'm usually driving to work, so phone me on my mobile." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Seven o'clock is a bit early. We're generally eating then." />

<Callout type="tip" title="Simple habit vs habit-at-a-time">
  <em>I <strong>drive</strong> to work</em> tells us your habit in general. <em>At eight I'm <strong>driving</strong> to work</em> zooms in on where that habit puts you at a set time — which is why it pairs so naturally with <em>so phone me then</em>.
</Callout>
