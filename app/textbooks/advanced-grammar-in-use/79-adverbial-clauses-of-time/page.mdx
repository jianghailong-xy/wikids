An adverbial clause of time pins one event to another on a timeline. The everyday conjunctions — **when, while, as, before, after, until** and the immediacy trio **hardly / no sooner / scarcely** — are familiar from elementary level. The advanced work is no longer *which word means 'time'* but *which word is exact*: **when** vs **as** vs **while** for overlapping actions, **until** vs **before** for a situation that runs up to a point and then stops, and the near-fixed pairings that the front-shifted **hardly**, **no sooner** and **scarcely** demand.

## A — As, when and while

We can often use **as**, **when** or **while** to mean *'during the time that'* — to talk about something that happens when something else takes place:

<SayIt text="As / When / While Miguel was eating, the doorbell rang." />

But the three are not freely interchangeable. We use **when** (*not* **as** or **while**) to introduce a clause that talks about:

- **an event that takes place at the same time as some longer event** in the main clause:

<SayIt text="They were playing in the garden when they heard a scream." />

- **the circumstances** in which the event in the main clause happens:

<SayIt text="When they are fully grown these snakes can be over two metres long." />

We also use **when** to mean *'every time'*, and we prefer **when** to talk about **past periods of our lives**:

<SayIt text="I still feel tired when I wake up in the morning." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= every time — a repeated occasion.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="His mother called him Robbie when he was a baby." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= throughout a past period of his life.)</p>

We prefer **when** if one event happens **immediately after** another, particularly if one **causes** the other:

<SayIt text="You'll see my house on the right when you cross the bridge." />
<br />
<SayIt text="When the lights went out, I lit some candles." />

<Callout type="info" title="Why not 'as' or 'while' here?">
  In <em>You'll see my house when you cross the bridge</em>, <strong>as</strong> or <strong>while</strong> would suggest <em>'during the time that'</em> and pull the verb towards the continuous (<em>…as you are crossing…</em>). In <em>When the lights went out…</em>, <strong>as</strong> or <strong>while</strong> would be very unlikely, because lights usually go out <strong>instantaneously</strong> — there is no stretch of time to be <em>'during'</em>.
</Callout>

We prefer **as** to say that **when one thing changes, another thing changes at the same time**:

<SayIt text="As the cheese matures, its flavour improves." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(rather than <em>When the cheese matures…</em> — the two changes track each other. <strong>While</strong> also works, especially with a continuous tense: <em>While the cheese is maturing…</em>)</p>

We prefer **while** or **as** (rather than **when**) to talk about **two longer actions that go on at the same time** — though **while** is more common than **as** in informal speech:

<SayIt text="I went shopping while Liam cleaned the house." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>…as Liam cleaned the house.</em>)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="The 'as' = 'because' trap">
  Use <strong>while</strong> or <strong>when</strong> (rather than <strong>as</strong>) where <strong>as</strong> could be misread as <em>'because'</em>. <em>While you were playing golf, I went to the cinema</em> is unambiguously about time. <em>As you were playing golf, I went to the cinema</em> could be heard as <em>Because you were playing golf…</em>
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Future time: present tense, not 'will'">
  After <strong>when, while, as, before, after</strong> and <strong>until</strong>, we use a <strong>present</strong> tense — not <em>will</em> — for future time: <em>You'll see my house when you <strong>cross</strong> the bridge</em> (not <em>when you will cross</em>); <em>I'll wait here until you <strong>get</strong> back</em>.
</Callout>

## B — Before, after and until

We use **before** or **after** to talk about an event happening **earlier or later** than another event:

<SayIt text="I put on my coat before I went out." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The message arrived after I'd left." />

We can often use **either until or before** when a situation continues **up to a time** indicated in the adverbial clause:

<SayIt text="I had to wait six weeks until / before the parcel arrived." />

However, we use **until** (not *before*) to talk about an action that **continues to a particular time and then stops**:

<SayIt text="They sat on the beach until the sun sank below the horizon, and then they went home." />

…and when the adverbial clause describes the **result** of an action in the main clause:

<SayIt text="He cleaned his shoes until they shone." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(The <em>shining</em> is the result of the <em>cleaning</em> — so <strong>until</strong>, not <em>before</em>.)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="The until-only test">
  Ask: does the action <strong>run right up to that moment and then stop</strong> (<em>worked until midnight</em>), or does it produce that <strong>result</strong> (<em>scrubbed until it was clean</em>)? If yes to either, only <strong>until</strong> works. If the clause merely marks an earlier/later point, <strong>before</strong> / <strong>after</strong> are available too.
</Callout>

## C — Hardly, no sooner and scarcely

When we say that one event happened **immediately after** another, we can use sentences with **hardly**, **no sooner** and **scarcely** (see also Unit 100). The second clause takes a fixed linking word:

- after **hardly** and **scarcely** → the second clause begins with **when** or **before**;
- after **no sooner** → it begins with **than** or **when**.

<SayIt text="The concert had hardly begun before all the lights went out." />
<br />
<SayIt text="I had no sooner lit the barbecue than it started to rain." />

We often use a **past perfect** in the clause with **hardly** / **no sooner** / **scarcely**, and a **past simple** in the other:

<div className="not-prose my-6 overflow-hidden rounded-2xl border-2 border-violet-200 shadow-sm">
  <div className="grid grid-cols-2 text-sm">
    <div className="bg-violet-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-violet-900">First clause — past perfect</div>
    <div className="bg-sky-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-sky-900">Second clause — past simple</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">I had <strong>hardly</strong> sat down</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>when / before</strong> the phone rang.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 bg-violet-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">She had <strong>scarcely</strong> finished</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 bg-sky-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>when / before</strong> the applause began.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">We had <strong>no sooner</strong> arrived</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>than / when</strong> it began to pour.</div>
  </div>
</div>

<Callout type="tip" title="Mind the pairings">
  Keep the partners straight: <strong>hardly … when / before</strong>, <strong>scarcely … when / before</strong>, but <strong>no sooner … than / when</strong>. The classic slip is <em>no sooner … <span className="text-rose-600">when only</span></em> while forgetting <strong>than</strong>, or pairing <em>hardly … <span className="text-rose-600">than</span></em>. Fronting these words for emphasis triggers inversion (<em>Hardly had the concert begun…</em>) — that is Unit 100.
</Callout>
