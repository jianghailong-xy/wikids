## A — Participle adjectives

Some **-ing forms** (present participles) and **-ed forms** (past participles) of verbs can be used as **adjectives**. Most of these *participle adjectives* work in the two places adjectives normally do — **before the noun** they describe, or **after a linking verb** (see Unit 21):

<SayIt text="The hotel had a welcoming atmosphere." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(-ing participle, before the noun.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I found this broken plate in the kitchen cupboard." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(-ed participle, before the noun.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The students' test results were pleasing." />
<br />
<SayIt text="My mother seemed delighted with the present." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(After the linking verbs <em>were</em> and <em>seemed</em>.)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="Feeling vs cause: the -ed / -ing pair">
  Many participle adjectives come in pairs. The <strong>-ed</strong> form describes how someone <em>feels</em>; the <strong>-ing</strong> form describes the thing that <em>causes</em> the feeling. <em>I was <strong>delighted</strong></em> (my feeling) with the <em><strong>pleasing</strong> results</em> (what caused it). So: an <em>interested</em> visitor reads an <em>interesting</em> leaflet — never the other way round.
</Callout>

## B — Participle adjectives after a noun: 'reduced relatives'

We can use many participle adjectives **immediately after a noun** when they identify or define it. This is similar to a **defining relative clause**, so these are often called **'reduced relatives'**:

<SayIt text="We had to pay for the rooms used." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= the rooms <em>that were used</em>.)</p>

Where the participle can go depends on the word. There are two groups to watch.

**1. Some are used *only after* the noun — never before it:**

<SayIt text="My watch was among the things taken." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(But <strong>not</strong> ✗ <em>the taken things</em>.)</p>

<div className="not-prose my-5 rounded-xl border-2 border-rose-200 bg-rose-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-rose-700">after the noun only</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-rose-900">applying · caused · found · included · provided · taken · used</p>
</div>

**2. Others can go *before or immediately after* the noun, with no change of meaning:**

<SayIt text="The crowd watching grew restless." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The watching crowd grew restless." />

<div className="not-prose my-5 rounded-xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-emerald-700">before or after the noun</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-emerald-900">affected · alleged · allocated · broken · chosen · identified · infected · interested · remaining · resulting · stolen</p>
</div>

<Callout type="tip" title="Test it against the full relative clause">
  A reduced relative is just a defining relative clause with the relative pronoun and <em>be</em> removed: <em>the rooms (that were) <strong>used</strong></em>, <em>the crowd (that was) <strong>watching</strong></em>. If the full clause is passive (<em>that were used</em>), you get an <strong>-ed</strong> participle; if it's active (<em>that was watching</em>), you get an <strong>-ing</strong> participle.
</Callout>

## C — 'That' and 'those' + participle adjective

In **formal English**, **that** and **those** can be used as pronouns before a participle adjective — a compact way of comparing one thing with another of the same kind:

<SayIt text="The flour is of a higher quality than that produced by other varieties of wheat." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>that produced</em> = the flour which is produced.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The touchscreens perform less well than those manufactured elsewhere." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>those manufactured</em> = the touchscreens which are manufactured elsewhere.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Here is some advice for those preparing to go on holiday." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>those</em> = the people who are preparing.)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="that = singular / uncountable · those = plural">
  Match the pronoun to what it stands for: <strong>that</strong> for a singular or uncountable noun (<em>the flour → <strong>that</strong> produced</em>), <strong>those</strong> for a plural (<em>the touchscreens → <strong>those</strong> manufactured</em>). <strong>those</strong> can also mean <em>the people who</em>: <em>those preparing</em>, <em>those wishing to apply</em>.
</Callout>

## D — Compound adjectives

Many **compound adjectives** include a participle adjective joined to another word. These are the common patterns:

<div className="not-prose my-6 overflow-hidden rounded-2xl border-2 border-violet-200 shadow-sm">
  <div className="grid grid-cols-1 divide-y divide-violet-100 text-sm sm:grid-cols-2 sm:divide-x">
    <div className="px-4 py-3"><span className="font-bold text-violet-900">adverb + -ed</span><br /><span className="text-slate-600">They are <strong>well-behaved</strong> children.</span></div>
    <div className="px-4 py-3"><span className="font-bold text-violet-900">adverb + -ing</span><br /><span className="text-slate-600">Social networking is a <strong>fast-growing</strong> activity.</span></div>
    <div className="px-4 py-3"><span className="font-bold text-violet-900">adjective + -ed</span><br /><span className="text-slate-600">She lives on <strong>ready-made</strong> meals.</span></div>
    <div className="px-4 py-3"><span className="font-bold text-violet-900">adjective + -ing</span><br /><span className="text-slate-600">He's the <strong>longest-serving</strong> employee here.</span></div>
    <div className="px-4 py-3"><span className="font-bold text-violet-900">noun + -ed</span><br /><span className="text-slate-600">The public square was <strong>tree-lined</strong>.</span></div>
    <div className="px-4 py-3"><span className="font-bold text-violet-900">noun + -ing</span><br /><span className="text-slate-600">I hope it will be a <strong>money-making</strong> enterprise.</span></div>
    <div className="px-4 py-3 sm:col-span-2"><span className="font-bold text-violet-900">-ed participle + particle</span><br /><span className="text-slate-600">Was it really true, or a <strong>made-up</strong> story? (from two-word verbs)</span></div>
  </div>
</div>

<Callout type="warning" title="Some participle adjectives exist only in the compound">
  Sometimes the participle can't stand on its own — the meaning is incomplete without the adverb or noun. We can say <em>well-behaved children</em> but not ✗ <em>behaved children</em>; a <em>money-making enterprise</em> but not ✗ <em>a making enterprise</em>.
</Callout>

More compound adjectives built the same way:

<div className="not-prose my-5 flex flex-wrap gap-2">
  {["New York-based", "Paris-born", "brick-built", "easy-going", "peace-keeping", "long-lasting", "good-looking", "home-made", "hair-raising", "far-reaching", "well-resourced", "sweet-smelling", "strange-sounding", "soft-spoken", "sour-tasting", "nerve-wracking"].map((w) => (
    <span key={w} className="rounded-lg border-2 border-sky-200 bg-sky-50 px-3 py-1.5 text-sm font-semibold text-sky-800">{w}</span>
  ))}
</div>

<Callout type="info" title="Not every compound adjective contains a participle">
  Plenty are built from other word classes: <em>The problem is <strong>short-term</strong>. · It was just a <strong>small-scale</strong> project.</em> (adjective + noun). Don't force an <em>-ed</em> onto them — it's <em>short-term</em>, not ✗ <em>short-termed</em>.
</Callout>
