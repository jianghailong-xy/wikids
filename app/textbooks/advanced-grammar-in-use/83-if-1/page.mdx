This is the first of three units on *if*. It deals with **real conditionals** — situations the speaker treats as genuinely possible — and with two more formal patterns: *if ... were + to-infinitive* for unlikely imaginary futures, and *if it were not for / Were it not for / but for* to say that one thing depends on another. The recurring theme is which **tense** goes in the *if*-clause, and the subtle change in meaning each choice signals. *(Reminder → M9–M17.)*

## A — Real conditionals

In real conditionals we usually use a **present tense** verb in the *if*-clause to talk about the future:

<SayIt text="If you leave now, you'll be able to catch the 5 o'clock train." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or, for an arrangement already under way: <em>if you're leaving now ...</em>)</p>

However, in conversation we also use **be going to** instead of a present tense verb:

<SayIt text="If I'm going to catch the train, I'll have to leave now." />
<br />
<SayIt text="We'll need more chairs if we're going to invite so many people to the performance." />

When we make offers, and give instructions or advice, we can use an **imperative** in the main clause:

<SayIt text="Take another sandwich if you're hungry." />
<br />
<SayIt text="If you have a mobile phone, check that it is turned off." />

<Callout type="tip" title="The shape of a real conditional">
  Present tense (or <em>be going to</em>) after <strong>if</strong> → <strong>will</strong>, an imperative, or another future form in the main clause. We almost never put <em>will</em> in the <em>if</em>-clause itself: <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>If you will leave now ...</em>
</Callout>

## B — if meaning 'when'

We can use *if*-clauses with a present tense verb to introduce certain **conditions under which something is true**. In cases like this, *if* has a meaning similar to **when** — every time the condition holds, the result holds:

<SayIt text="The video pauses if you click on this button." />
<br />
<SayIt text="If age-related changes are taken into account, the conclusion remains the same." />

<Callout type="info" title="if ≈ when, but not always interchangeable">
  Use this <em>if</em> = <em>when</em> only for a general truth or repeated cause-and-effect. For a one-off future event you are not certain about, keep ordinary <em>if</em>: <em>If it rains tomorrow, we'll cancel</em> (uncertain) is not the same as <em>When it rains, the match is cancelled</em> (a standing rule).
</Callout>

## C — Possible future events: present perfect in the if-clause

We can talk about possible future events with a **present perfect** verb in the *if*-clause and a future form (**will**, present continuous, or **be going to**) in the main clause. Often a present perfect or a present simple can be used with a similar meaning:

<SayIt text="I'll lend you War and Peace if I've finished it before you go on holiday." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or ... <em>if I finish</em> ...)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="If you haven't paid the bill by Friday, we're taking the carpets back." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>If you don't pay</em> ...)</p>

However, to focus on the **future consequences of a past event**, we use the present perfect, and the two versions then mean different things. Compare:

<SayIt text="If I've failed my exam again, I'm giving up the course." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Suggests I have <strong>already taken</strong> the exam — I just don't know the result yet.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="If I fail my exam again, I'm giving up the course." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(I may or may not have taken the exam — it is still open.)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="The trap">
  <em>If I've failed</em> looks back at something done; <em>If I fail</em> looks forward at something still to happen. When the deadline-or-result is in the past but its consequence is in the future, the present perfect is the precise choice.
</Callout>

## D — Unreal conditionals: if ... were + to-infinitive

In unreal conditionals we can use **if ... + were + to-infinitive** rather than *if + past simple* to talk about **imaginary future situations**, particularly when it is unlikely that the situation in the *if*-clause will happen *(see also Unit 14)*:

<SayIt text="If the technology were to become available, we would be able to expand the business." />

However, we **don't** usually use this pattern with verbs that describe a **state** — for example **belong, doubt, enjoy, know, like, remember, understand**:

<SayIt text="If I knew they were honest, I'd gladly lend them the money." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>If I were to know they were honest ...</em>)</p>

We sometimes use this pattern to make a **suggestion sound more polite**:

<SayIt text="Would it be too early for you if we were to meet at 5:30?" />

<Callout type="info" title="were to = a hypothetical, distant future">
  <em>were to + infinitive</em> pushes the situation further into the realm of the unlikely than the plain past simple does. Reserve it for <strong>action</strong> and <strong>event</strong> verbs (<em>become, hold, close, happen</em>); for state verbs (<em>belong, like, doubt, understand</em>) use the past simple instead.
</Callout>

## E — if it was/were not for; Were it not for; but for

We use **if it was not for + noun phrase** (or, more formally, **if it were not for + noun phrase**) to say that one situation **depends on** another situation or on a person *(see also Unit 85A)*. When we talk about the past we can also use **if it had not been for + noun phrase**:

<SayIt text="If it wasn't / weren't for Nina, the conference wouldn't be going ahead." />
<br />
<SayIt text="If it hadn't been for Dad, I wouldn't have gone to college." />

In more **formal** language we can also front the verb and drop *if*, using **Were it not for ...** and **Had it not been for ...** *(see Unit 84A)*:

<SayIt text="Were it not for Nina, the conference wouldn't be going ahead." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Had it not been for Dad, I wouldn't have gone to college." />

We can use **but for + noun** with a similar meaning, particularly in formal contexts:

<SayIt text="The village school would have been closed years ago but for the determination of teachers and parents to keep it open." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= ... <em>if it hadn't been for the determination</em> ...)</p>
