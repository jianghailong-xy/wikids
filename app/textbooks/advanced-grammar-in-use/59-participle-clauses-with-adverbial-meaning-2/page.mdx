Unit 58 built participle clauses out of a bare *-ing* or *-ed* form. This unit puts a **signpost word** in front of that participle — a preposition (*after, before, by, in, on, since, while, without*…) or, in the last section, a conjunction or adjective with **no verb at all**. The signpost makes the adverbial meaning explicit (the timing, the method, the reason) instead of leaving the reader to infer it, and it lets you compress a whole finite clause into a few words. The grammar is tight and the register is formal — learn what each signpost contributes and you can fold a subordinate clause into the front of almost any sentence.

## A — Prepositions + *-ing* clause

We can use prepositions such as **after, before, besides, by, in, on, since, through, while, with,** and **without** in a present participle (*-ing*) clause with an **adverbial meaning** (see also Unit 51):

<SayIt text="While understanding her problem, I don't know how I can help." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= <em>Although</em> I understand her problem … — concession.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="After spending so much money on the car, I can't afford a holiday." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= <em>Because / Now that</em> I have spent … — time + reason.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Before being changed last year, the speed limit was 70 kph." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= <em>Before</em> it was changed … — a <strong>passive</strong> clause: <em>before being + past participle</em>.)</p>

An alternative is a **clause with a finite verb** that can change according to tense and subject. The *-ing* version is more compact and more formal. Compare:

<SayIt text="Since moving to London, we haven't had time to go to the theatre." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(preposition + <em>-ing</em> — more formal.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Since we moved to London, we haven't had time to go to the theatre." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(conjunction + subject + finite verb — less formal.)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="The implied subject still has to match">
  Exactly as in Unit 58, the unstated subject of the <em>-ing</em> clause is read as the subject of the main clause. In <em>While understanding her problem, I don't know how I can help</em>, the person who understands is <strong>I</strong> — the same <strong>I</strong> who doesn't know how to help. Make them different and the clause dangles: <span className="text-rose-600">✗</span> <em>After being repaired, I drove the car home</em> says <em>I</em> was repaired. Write <em>After being repaired, the car was ready to drive</em>, or unfold it: <em>After the car had been repaired, I drove it home.</em>
</Callout>

## B — *by*, *in*, *on* + *-ing*

Three of these prepositions are worth singling out, because each adds a **different** adverbial meaning:

<div className="not-prose my-6 overflow-hidden rounded-2xl border-2 border-violet-200 shadow-sm">
  <div className="grid grid-cols-[1fr_auto] text-sm">
    <div className="bg-violet-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-violet-900">Example</div>
    <div className="bg-sky-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-sky-900">The <em>-ing</em> clause indicates …</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>By</strong> working hard, she passed her maths exam. · They only survived <strong>by</strong> eating roots and berries.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 px-4 py-3 font-semibold text-sky-900">the <strong>method or means</strong> used</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 bg-violet-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>On</strong> returning from Beijing, he wrote to the embassy. · Josh was the first person I saw <strong>on</strong> leaving hospital.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 bg-sky-50/50 px-4 py-3 font-semibold text-sky-900"><strong>when</strong> — immediately after</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>In</strong> criticising the painting, I knew I would offend her. · <strong>In</strong> choosing Marco, the party has moved to the left.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 px-4 py-3 font-semibold text-sky-900"><strong>cause</strong> — the act itself produces the result</div>
  </div>
</div>

We can often use **by + *-ing*** or **in + *-ing*** with a similar meaning, although **by** is preferred in informal contexts:

<SayIt text="By writing about Spanish culture, I came to understand the country better." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>By writing …</em> = the <strong>method</strong> I used to understand it better.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="In writing about Spanish culture, I came to understand the country better." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>In writing …</em> = understanding it better was a <strong>consequence</strong> of writing.)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="When only 'by' will do">
  When you mean a deliberate <strong>method</strong> — not a by-product — use <strong>by</strong>, not <strong>in</strong>: <em>By telephoning every hour, she managed to speak to the doctor.</em> Here the calling is the technique that got her through; <span className="text-rose-600">✗</span> <em>In telephoning every hour …</em> would wrongly suggest that speaking to the doctor was just an accidental result of phoning.
</Callout>

## C — *with* + *-ing*; *without* + *-ing*

**With + *-ing*** often introduces a **reason** for something in the main clause. This use is fairly informal, and a **subject has to come between *with* and the *-ing* form**:

<SayIt text="With Louise living in Spain, we don't see her very often." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= <em>Because</em> Louise lives in Spain … — note <em>with</em> + <strong>Louise</strong> + <em>living</em>.)</p>

**With** — and **what with** — can also be used with a **noun phrase** (no participle) to introduce a reason:

<SayIt text="With my bad back, I won't be able to lift a heavy suitcase." />
<br />
<SayIt text="What with the traffic and the heavy rain, it's no wonder you were late." />

We use **without + *-ing*** to say that a **second action does not happen**:

<SayIt text="I went to work without eating breakfast." />
<br />
<SayIt text="They left the restaurant without paying." />

Often, however, *without + -ing* has a meaning close to **'although … not'** or **'unless'**:

<SayIt text="Without meaning to, I seem to have offended her." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= <em>Although</em> I didn't mean to …)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Without using the app, I won't be able to judge how good it is." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= <em>Unless</em> I use the app …)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't lose the subject after 'with'">
  Unlike a plain participle clause, a <em>with</em>-clause normally carries its <strong>own</strong> subject, slotted in before the <em>-ing</em>: <em>With <strong>the kids</strong> away this week, the house is very quiet.</em> Drop it and the sentence collapses — <span className="text-rose-600">✗</span> <em>With living in Spain, we don't see her often</em> has no one doing the living.
</Callout>

## D — A conjunction or adjective with no verb

Adverbial meanings can also be added by a clause **beginning with a conjunction or an adjective but containing no verb**. This kind of clause has the same meaning as a clause beginning with a **conjunction + subject + *be***, and is used in fairly formal English (more informal alternatives are given in brackets):

<SayIt text="While in Poland, they will play two concerts in Warsaw." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>While they are in Poland …</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Although just two feet apart, they didn't speak." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>Although they were just two feet apart …</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I try to use public transport whenever possible." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>… whenever it is possible.</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Unhappy with the decision, Johnson swore at the referee." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>Because he was unhappy with the decision …</em> — an <strong>adjective</strong> alone, no conjunction.)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="What you are really doing">
  You are deleting the <strong>subject + a form of <em>be</em></strong> and leaving the rest standing: <em>While <s>they are</s> in Poland</em>, <em>Although <s>they were</s> just two feet apart</em>, <em>whenever <s>it is</s> possible</em>. As with every clause in this unit, the missing subject is recovered from the main clause — so keep them the same, and keep it for writing rather than casual speech.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Take it further">
  The cleanest test for all of these clauses is the same one from Unit 58: read the noun straight after the comma and ask whether it is the one doing the <em>-ing</em>. <em>After being repaired, <strong>the car</strong> ran perfectly</em> ✓ — the car was repaired. <em>After being repaired, <strong>I</strong> drove the car home</em> ✗ — I wasn't repaired.
