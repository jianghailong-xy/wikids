## A — Comparatives: -er vs more / less … than

The default rule you already know: add **-er** to one-syllable adjectives and adverbs to make the comparative (*old → older*, *fast → faster*). At advanced level the value is in the exceptions — the one-syllable words that still take **more**.

**1. One-syllable past participle adjectives** (see Unit 69) such as *bored, creased, pleased, worn* take **more**, not *-er*:

<SayIt text="After I'd ironed my shirt it looked more creased than before." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Not <em>creaseder</em>.)</p>

**2. The words fun, real, right and wrong** take **more**:

<SayIt text="I expected the film to be rather dull, but I couldn't have been more wrong." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Not <em>wronger</em>.)</p>

**3. When we compare two qualities of the same thing**, we use **more** — even with a one-syllable adjective:

<SayIt text="'Wasn't he brave to swim across?' 'I think he was more mad than brave.'" />
<br />
<SayIt text="Although the paint was called 'Sky Blue', I thought it was more green than blue." />

<Callout type="tip" title="Two ways to say the same comparison">
  When you weigh one quality against another, English gives you three patterns: <em>he was <strong>more mad than</strong> brave</em> · <em>he wasn't <strong>so much</strong> brave <strong>as</strong> mad</em> · <em>it was blue <strong>rather than</strong> green</em>. All three say "X is the better description, not Y."
</Callout>

Finally, with a small set of one-syllable adjectives we can use **more** instead of *-er* simply to **emphasise** the comparison:

<SayIt text="You might think it's dark here, but it's more dark — or darker — in the cellar." />

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-2xl border-2 border-amber-200 bg-amber-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-amber-700">Also take an emphatic <em>more</em></p>
  <p className="mt-2 text-base font-semibold text-amber-900">clear · cold · deep · fair · rough · soft · true</p>
  <p className="mt-2 text-sm text-amber-800">With these, both forms are fine — <em>deeper</em> / <em>more deep</em>, <em>truer</em> / <em>more true</em> — but the plain <em>-er</em> form is still the neutral choice.</p>
</div>

## B — Two-syllable adjectives that prefer more / less

Some two-syllable adjectives are **most commonly used with more / less**, never with *-er*. Learn the groups:

<div className="not-prose my-6 grid gap-3 sm:grid-cols-2">
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-rose-200 bg-rose-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-rose-700">participle adjectives</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-rose-900">worried, boring, tired, pleasing</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-sky-200 bg-sky-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-sky-700">ending -ful and -less</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-sky-900">careful, careless, useful, useless</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-violet-200 bg-violet-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-violet-700">the a- adjectives</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-violet-900">afraid, alert, alike, alone, ashamed, aware</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-emerald-700">and others, including</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-emerald-900">active, cautious, certain, complex, direct, eager, exact, formal, frequent, modern, recent, special</p>
  </div>
</div>

Most two-syllable adjectives ending in **-y, -ow** and **-ure** can take **either** form — *happy → happier / more happy*, *narrow → narrower / more narrow* — although the **-er** form is the one used more often.

<Callout type="warning" title="Absolute adjectives resist comparison">
  Adjectives such as <strong>complete, equal, favourite, ideal, perfect</strong> and <strong>unique</strong> already carry a comparative or superlative idea, so they aren't normally used with <em>-er / more / less</em> or <em>-est / most / least</em>. We bend the rule only for deliberate, emphatic effect: <em>The weather today was good, but <strong>less perfect</strong> than yesterday.</em>
</Callout>

## C — Superlatives: keeping or dropping "the"

We normally put **the**, a possessive **'s**, or a possessive pronoun before a superlative adjective or adverb (*the cheapest*, *Anna's oldest child*, *my best work*). But in **informal** contexts we sometimes leave out **the** before an *-est* or *most +* adjective superlative **after a linking verb**, especially at the end of a sentence:

<SayIt text="'Why did you go by bus?' 'It was (the) cheapest.'" />
<br />
<SayIt text="'Which of these was (the) most expensive?'" />

You **cannot** drop **the**, though, once you go on to say *what group* is being compared:

<SayIt text="'Why did you buy these oranges?' 'They were the cheapest ones I could find.'" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Not <em>They were cheapest ones I could find.</em>)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="most without 'the' means 'very'">
  When <strong>most + adjective / adverb</strong> appears without <em>the</em>, it stops being a superlative and means something like <em>very</em>: <em>I checked the form <strong>most carefully</strong></em> (= very carefully) <em>but didn't notice the mistake.</em>
</Callout>

## D — After a superlative: of + plural, in + singular

To name **the objects being compared**, we use **of + a plural noun phrase**:

<SayIt text="Adam's the oldest of my three brothers." />

We can move that *of*-phrase to the front of the sentence to emphasise it:

<SayIt text="Of my three brothers, Adam's the oldest." />

But to give the **location or context** within which the comparison is made, we use **in + a singular noun phrase**:

<SayIt text="It was the tallest tree in the forest." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Not <em>the tallest tree of the forest.</em>)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="A quick reminder on the forms themselves">
  Spelling: one-syllable words double a final consonant after a single vowel (<em>big → bigger / biggest</em>) and change <em>-y</em> to <em>-i</em> (<em>happy → happier / happiest</em>). And the irregulars never take <em>-er / -est</em>: <strong>good → better → best</strong>, <strong>bad → worse → worst</strong>, <strong>far → further/farther → furthest/farthest</strong>, <strong>little → less → least</strong>, <strong>much/many → more → most</strong>.
</Callout>
