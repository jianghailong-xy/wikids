Adjectives fall into two camps, and the camp decides which adverb you can put in front of it. Some qualities come in **degrees** — you can be *a bit* rich or *extremely* rich — and these **gradable** adjectives take grading adverbs like *very* and *rather*. Others are **absolute** — you can't be *slightly* impossible or *very* unique — and these **non-gradable** adjectives take a different set of adverbs (*absolutely*, *completely*, *almost*) that stress how total the quality is. Getting the pairing right is what makes advanced English sound native rather than merely correct.

## A — Gradable and non-gradable adjectives

**Gradable adjectives** can be used with **grading adverbs** such as *very* or *extremely* to say that a thing or person has **more or less** of a particular quality. Here are some adjectives used as gradable in their most common meanings:

  </div>
  <div className="rounded-2xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50 p-4">

    <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-emerald-900">angry · big · busy · clever · common · different · fast · friendly · happy · important · low · popular · quiet · rich · strong · weak · young</p>

</div>

<br />
<SayIt text="It's hugely popular." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The people there are reasonably friendly." />
<br />
<SayIt text="They're slightly different." />

**Non-gradable adjectives** are **not** used with adverbs such as *very* or *extremely*, because these adjectives don't refer to qualities that come in different degrees. Instead we use **non-grading adverbs** which emphasise their **extreme or absolute** nature — *absolutely*, *completely*, etc. Many **classifying** adjectives (see Unit 66) are usually non-gradable, and adverbs such as *almost*, *exclusively*, etc. — which indicate the **extent** of the quality — are commonly used with them.

    <p className="mt-2 text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-violet-700">…and extent adverbs (with classifying adjectives)</p>

    <p className="mt-2 text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-amber-700">…and classifying adjectives (Unit 66C)</p>

  </div>
</div>

<SayIt text="She's completely wrong." />
<br />
<SayIt text="He was practically unknown to the public." />
<br />

<br />

Gradable adjectives are **sometimes** used with non-grading adverbs, and non-gradable adjectives with grading adverbs, to give **special emphasis** or to be **humorous**:

<SayIt text="What you're asking isn't just difficult — it's extremely impossible!" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(grading adverb <em>extremely</em> + non-gradable adjective <em>impossible</em> — deliberately over the top)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="You've won a hundred pounds? Wow, you're virtually rich!" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(non-grading adverb <em>virtually</em> + gradable adjective <em>rich</em> — playful understatement)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="Not every adverb fits every adjective">
  Even within a camp, collocation is picky. We say <em>absolutely huge</em>, but we <strong>wouldn't</strong> normally say <em>completely huge</em> — unless it was for particular emphasis or for humour. Learn the adverb + adjective pairings the way you learn vocabulary: as fixed partnerships, not as a free choice from the box.
</Callout>

## B — fairly, really and pretty

Three adverbs sit comfortably with **both** gradable and non-gradable adjectives:

<div className="not-prose my-6 grid gap-2 sm:grid-cols-3">
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-teal-200 bg-teal-50 px-4 py-3">
    <p className="text-sm font-bold text-teal-900">fairly</p>
    <p className="text-xs text-teal-700">to quite a large degree, but usually <em>less</em> than 'very'</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-teal-200 bg-teal-50 px-4 py-3">
    <p className="text-sm font-bold text-teal-900">really</p>
    <p className="text-xs text-teal-700">= 'very (much)'</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-teal-200 bg-teal-50 px-4 py-3">
    <p className="text-sm font-bold text-teal-900">pretty</p>
    <p className="text-xs text-teal-700">similar to 'fairly'; informal</p>
  </div>
</div>

<SayIt text="She's fairly popular at school." />
<br />
<SayIt text="I'm really busy at the moment." />
<br />
<SayIt text="It's a pretty important exam." />
<br />
<SayIt text="It was a fairly awful film." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The flooding was really terrible." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The bill was pretty huge." />

However, we **don't** generally use **fairly** (or **very**) with non-gradable adjectives that say something is **very good** or **necessary**. Use *really* or *pretty* instead:

<SayIt text="Experience is really / pretty essential for the job." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>… fairly essential …</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The weather was really / pretty perfect." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>… fairly perfect.</em>)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="The same trap with these too">
  <strong>invaluable · superb · tremendous · wonderful</strong> behave like <em>essential</em> and <em>perfect</em>: they're strong, near-absolute adjectives, so <em>really</em> and <em>pretty</em> work but <em>fairly</em> and <em>very</em> sound wrong. Say <em>really invaluable</em>, <em>pretty tremendous</em> — not <em>fairly invaluable</em>, <em>very tremendous</em>.
</Callout>
