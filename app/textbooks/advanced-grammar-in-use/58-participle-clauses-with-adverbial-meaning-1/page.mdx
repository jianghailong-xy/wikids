A **participle clause** is a clause built around a participle — a *present participle* (*-ing*: *opening*, *being*) or a *past participle* (*-ed*: *faced*, *built*) — with no subject and no finite verb of its own. Used adverbially, it does the job of a whole adverbial clause of **time, reason, condition or result**, but in a tighter, more literary register. This unit is about how to form them, how the reader works out their hidden subject, and the one mistake that turns an elegant sentence into a comic one: the dangling participle.

## A — What participle clauses express

We can use **present participle (*-ing*)** and **past participle (*-ed*)** clauses with an adverbial meaning. They often give information about the **timing**, **causes** and **results** of the events described:

<SayIt text="Opening her eyes, the baby began to cry." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= <em>When</em> she opened her eyes … — <strong>time</strong>.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Faced with a bill for £10,000, Ivan has taken an extra job." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= <em>Because</em> he is faced … — <strong>reason</strong>; past participle.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Looked after carefully, the plant can live through the winter." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= <em>If</em> it is looked after … — <strong>condition</strong>; past participle.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Having finished the book, I had a holiday." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= <em>When / Because</em> I had finished … — <strong>perfect</strong>.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The fruit was expensive, being imported." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= … <em>because</em> it was imported — <strong>simple passive</strong>.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Having been hunted close to extinction, the rhino is once again common in this area." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= <em>Although</em> it had been hunted close to extinction … — <strong>perfect passive</strong>.)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="The four building blocks">
  A past participle clause (<em>Faced …</em>, <em>Looked after …</em>) always carries a <strong>passive</strong> meaning. For an <strong>active</strong> meaning use the present participle (<em>Opening …</em>), and add <em>having</em> for a completed action (<em>Having finished …</em>) or <em>being / having been</em> to make it passive (<em>being imported</em>, <em>having been hunted</em>).
</Callout>

## B — The implied subject: keep it the same

The **implied subject** of a participle clause — a subject the reader knows but which is not directly mentioned — is usually the **same as the subject of the main clause**:

<SayIt text="Arriving at the party, we saw Ruth standing alone." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= When <strong>we</strong> arrived … <strong>we</strong> saw … — one subject for both clauses.)</p>

However, sometimes the implied subject is **not referred to** in the main clause at all:

<SayIt text="Having wanted to drive a train all his life, this was an opportunity not to be missed." />

<Callout type="warning" title="The dangling participle — the unit's key trap">
  In careful speech and writing we <strong>avoid different subjects</strong> for the participle and the main clause. Read this aloud:<br /><br />
  <span className="text-rose-600">✗</span> <em>Turning round quickly, the door hit me in the face.</em><br />
  The implied subject of <em>turning</em> is <strong>I</strong>, but the subject of the main clause is <strong>the door</strong> — so it literally says the door turned round. Rewrite with a full clause and an explicit subject:<br /><br />
  <span className="text-emerald-600">✓</span> <em>When I turned round quickly, the door hit me in the face.</em>
</Callout>

## C — Participle clauses with their own subject

In **formal English**, the participle clause sometimes has its **own subject**, which is often a pronoun or includes one:

<SayIt text="The collection of vases is priceless, some being over 2,000 years old." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Her voice breaking with emotion, Vasiliki spoke about her father's illness." />

We use the present participle (*-ing*) clause here to talk about something **happening at the same time** as an event in the main clause, or to **give extra information** about the facts given in the main clause.

<Callout type="tip" title="When the subjects differ on purpose">
  Section B's dangling participle is an <em>accident</em>: the writer forgot the subjects didn't match. This construction is <em>deliberate</em> — you supply the second subject (<em>some</em>, <em>her voice</em>) precisely <strong>because</strong> it is different from the main-clause subject. State it, and the sentence is correct.
</Callout>

## D — Where *not* goes

When we use **not** in a participle clause it usually comes **before** the participle. However, it can **follow** the participle, depending on the meaning:

<SayIt text="Not understanding the rules, I found the cricket match boring." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>not</em> before = <strong>because</strong> I didn't understand the rules.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Hoping not to be recognised, I chose a seat in a dark corner." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>not</em> after = I hoped that I <strong>wouldn't</strong> be recognised — the negative belongs to the second verb.)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="Read the negation, not a rule">
  <strong>Not + participle</strong> negates the participle itself (<em>not understanding</em> = didn't understand). <strong>Participle + not + to-infinitive</strong> negates what follows it (<em>hoping not to be recognised</em> = hoping <em>that I wouldn't</em>). Decide which idea is being made negative and put <em>not</em> next to it.
</Callout>

## E — *-ing* clause vs *having + past participle*

We use a clause beginning with **having + past participle** rather than a present participle when the action in the main clause is the **consequence** of the event in the participle clause:

<SayIt text="Having broken her leg the last time she went, Giorgia decided not to go on the school skiing trip this year." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>After breaking her leg …</em> — but <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>Breaking her leg …</em> on its own.)</p>

We can use **either** a present participle (*-ing*) clause **or** a *having + past participle* clause, with a similar meaning, when the action in the participle clause is **complete before** the action in the main clause begins. But when the two actions are **simultaneous**, only the *-ing* clause works. Compare:

<SayIt text="Taking off his shoes, Ram walked into the house." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>Having taken off …</em> has a similar meaning — the action is complete first, so <strong>either</strong> form is fine.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Running across the field, I fell and hurt my ankle." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= <em>While</em> I was running … — simultaneous. <em>Having run …</em> would suggest I fell <strong>after</strong> I had run across the field.)</p>

  In <em>s1</em> the car wasn't waiting for the bus; in <em>s4</em> the town wasn't looking down from the hill; in <em>s5</em> the boat wasn't feeling sick.
