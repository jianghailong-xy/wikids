English lets the same adverb sit in more than one slot in a clause, and the slot you choose carries meaning — emphasis, scope, even which verb the adverb attaches to. This unit maps the three working positions (**front**, **mid** and **end**), the two placements you should *avoid*, and the default order when several adverbials pile up at the end of a clause.

## A — Three positions: front, mid and end

There are three main positions for **adverbs that modify a verb**: **end**, **front** and **mid** position.

In **end position**, the adverb comes after the verb — either immediately after it, or later in the clause:

<SayIt text="They played quietly all day." />
<br />
<SayIt text="He tried to leave quietly." />
<br />
<SayIt text="He sat in the corner quietly." />

In **front position**, the adverb comes before the subject:

<SayIt text="Finally he could stand the noise no longer." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Sometimes I feel like leaving." />

In **mid position**, the adverb comes between the subject and the verb, immediately after **be** used as a main verb, or after the first auxiliary verb:

<SayIt text="He usually plays better than this." />
<br />
<SayIt text="She is usually here by ten." />
<br />
<SayIt text="They would usually come by car." />

Many adverbs can go in **any** of these positions, depending on context or style:

  <div className="grid grid-cols-[auto_1fr] text-sm">
    <div className="bg-emerald-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-emerald-900">End</div>
    <div className="border-l border-emerald-100 bg-emerald-50/40 px-4 py-2 text-slate-700">He turned round <strong>slowly</strong>.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-emerald-100 bg-emerald-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-emerald-900">Front</div>
    <div className="border-t border-l border-emerald-100 bg-emerald-50/40 px-4 py-2 text-slate-700"><strong>Slowly</strong> he turned round.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-emerald-100 bg-emerald-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-emerald-900">Mid</div>
    <div className="border-t border-l border-emerald-100 bg-emerald-50/40 px-4 py-2 text-slate-700">He <strong>slowly</strong> turned round.</div>
  </div>
</div>

## B — End position: after the object, not straight after the verb

In end position we usually put an adverb **after an object** rather than immediately after the verb:

<SayIt text="We considered the problem briefly." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>We considered briefly the problem.</em>)</p>

However, if the object is **very long**, other positions become possible — the adverb can slip in front of the object so the reader isn't kept waiting:

<SayIt text="We considered briefly the long-term solution to the problem." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>We briefly considered the long-term solution to the problem.</em>)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="Why the object comes first">
  The default <em>verb → object → adverb</em> order keeps the verb and its object together. Only when the object grows long enough to strand the adverb far from the verb do we relax the rule and move the adverb earlier.
</Callout>

## C — Don't split a verb from its *-ing* form or *to*-infinitive

We **avoid putting an adverb between a main verb and a following *-ing* form or *to*-infinitive**. Place it before the verb (mid) or after the whole verb phrase (end) instead:

<SayIt text="He began running quickly." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">or <em>He quickly began running.</em> — <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>He began quickly running.</em></p>
<br />
<SayIt text="She tried to leave quietly." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">or <em>She quietly tried to leave.</em> — <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>She tried quietly to leave.</em></p>

The position of the adverb can **change the meaning** of the sentence (see Unit 75A). Compare:

<SayIt text="I recall telling him clearly that he had won." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= I told him clearly — <em>clearly</em> modifies <em>telling him</em>.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I clearly recall telling him that he had won." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= I clearly recall it — <em>clearly</em> modifies <em>recall</em>.)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="The ambiguous middle">
  <em>I recall <strong>clearly</strong> telling him that he had won</em> is also possible, but it is <strong>ambiguous</strong> — it can carry either meaning above. In speech the intended meaning is usually signalled by intonation; in writing, prefer the unambiguous front or mid placement.
</Callout>

## D — Order when several adverbials meet: manner, place, time

When there is **more than one adverbial in end position**, the usual order in written English is **adverbial of manner** (= saying *how* something is done), then **place**, then **time**:

<SayIt text="In the accident she was thrown violently forwards." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= manner + place)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="We arrived here on Saturday." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= place + time)</p>

For special emphasis we can move an adverbial to the very end, off the usual order:

<SayIt text="In the accident she was thrown forwards, violently." />

Two refinements override the basic manner–place–time order:

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-sky-200 bg-sky-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-sky-700">Length wins</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm text-sky-900">If one adverbial is <strong>much longer</strong> than another, it is usually placed <strong>last</strong>, whatever the manner–place–time rule would say:</p>
  <p className="mt-2 text-sm font-semibold text-sky-900">They left <em>at three</em> <em>with a great deal of noise</em>. <span className="font-normal text-sky-700">(= time + manner)</span></p>
</div>

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-violet-200 bg-violet-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-violet-700">Adverb before phrase</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm text-violet-900">A one-word adverb usually comes <strong>before</strong> a prepositional phrase when the two have the <strong>same function</strong> (both manner, or both place, or both time):</p>
  <p className="mt-2 text-sm font-semibold text-violet-900">She went <em>downstairs</em> <em>to the cellar</em>. <span className="font-normal text-violet-700">(= place + place)</span></p>
</div>

## E — End position for place, definite frequency and definite time

End position is the usual home for many adverbials of **place**, **definite frequency** and **definite time**:

<SayIt text="They live upstairs." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>They upstairs live.</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="She goes weekly." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>She weekly goes.</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Have you heard the good news? Eva had a baby in May." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>Eva in May had a baby.</em>)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="Two qualifications">
  Adverbs of <strong>indefinite</strong> time (<em>already, soon, recently, still</em>) usually go in <strong>mid</strong> position instead (see Unit 75).<br /><br />
  Note too that in <strong>journalism</strong>, other adverbs of definite time are often placed in mid position where we would normally use end (or front) position: <em>The government <strong>yesterday</strong> announced an increase in education spending.</em>
</Callout>
