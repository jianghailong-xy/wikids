Unit 56 looked at adding information to a noun with a second noun phrase (apposition). This unit takes the next step: the other compact post-modifiers that do the work of a relative clause — above all the **prepositional phrase** — and how, in careful writing, you can stack several of them after one noun. The pay-off is density; the risk, as section C shows, is ambiguity.

## A — Prepositional phrases instead of relative clauses

We very commonly add information about a thing or a person with a **prepositional phrase** that follows the noun. These often mean roughly the same as a relative clause:

<SayIt text="What's the name of the man by the window?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or … the man <em>who's by the window</em>?)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="It's in the cupboard under the stairs." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or … the cupboard <em>that's under the stairs</em>.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="She lives in the house with the red door." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or … the house <em>which has the red door</em>.)</p>

In some cases, however, the prepositional phrase has **no corresponding relative clause** — the preposition is simply part of how the noun is completed:

<SayIt text="You need to keep a careful record of what you spend." />
<br />
<SayIt text="There is likely to be an increase in temperature tomorrow." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(We can't unpack <em>a record which is of…</em> or <em>an increase which is in…</em>.)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="Prefer the relative clause for be + preposition / have">
  In <strong>non-defining</strong> relative clauses we normally keep the full clause when the verb would be <strong>be + preposition</strong> or <strong>have</strong> as a main verb — the bare prepositional phrase sounds clipped:
  <br /><br />
  ✅ Mr Chen, <strong>who was in the store</strong> at the time of the robbery, was able to identify two of the men.
  &nbsp;<span className="text-slate-400">(rather than <em>Mr Chen, in the store…</em>)</span>
  <br /><br />
  ✅ Davide Gallo, <strong>who has a farm near Pisa</strong>, has decided to grow only organic vegetables.
  &nbsp;<span className="text-slate-400">(rather than <em>Davide Gallo, with a farm near Pisa…</em>)</span>
</Callout>

## B — Stacking post-modifiers: a series of phrases and clauses

In written English, and especially in **academic writing**, a series of prepositional phrases and relative clauses is often strung together to pile information onto one noun phrase. Notice that a prepositional phrase here can also carry an **adverbial** meaning (telling us *when* or *where*), not just describe the noun — *in the last six months* below modifies *taken*, not *patients*:

<SayIt text="Doctors are contacting patients with diabetes who have taken the drug in the last six months." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Scientists in Spain who have developed the technique are optimistic that it will be widely used in laboratories within the next decade." />

We can also bring in **participle clauses** and **noun phrases in apposition** (see Unit 56) in the same chain of post-modifiers:

<SayIt text="The waxwing is the only bird found in Britain with yellow and red tail feathers." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(participle clause <em>found in Britain</em> + prepositional phrase <em>with … feathers</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Mr Bob Timms, leader of the Democratic Party, MP for Threeoaks, has announced his resignation." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(two noun phrases in apposition, both describing <em>Mr Bob Timms</em>)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="Read the chain from the head noun outward">
  Each phrase or clause hooks onto the noun that comes <em>immediately before it</em>. To check a long noun phrase, point at the head noun and ask of every following chunk: <em>which word does this describe?</em> If the answer keeps changing, your reader will struggle too.
</Callout>

## C — When a string of phrases turns ambiguous

A series of prepositional phrases can easily produce more than one reading, because a phrase can attach to different earlier words:

<SayIt text="The protesters were demonstrating about the mistreatment of animals on farms." />

Does *on farms* tell us **where the protesters were** (demonstrating on farms) or **where the animals were** (animals on farms)? Both readings are available. We can force a single meaning by moving the phrase or by switching to a clause:

<div className="not-prose my-6 space-y-3">
  <div className="rounded-xl border border-slate-200 bg-white p-4 shadow-sm">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-emerald-700">meaning 1 — the protesters are on the farms</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm text-slate-700">The protesters were demonstrating <strong>on farms</strong> about the mistreatment of animals.</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border border-slate-200 bg-white p-4 shadow-sm">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-sky-700">meaning 2 — the animals are on the farms</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm text-slate-700">The protesters were demonstrating about the mistreatment of animals <strong>kept on farms</strong>.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<Callout type="info" title="Why it happens">
  Ambiguity comes from a prepositional phrase (or participle) sitting where it could modify <em>two</em> different earlier elements — usually a nearby noun <em>or</em> the verb. The cure is to put the modifier next to the word it belongs with, or to expand it into a relative / participle clause that pins it down.
</Callout>
