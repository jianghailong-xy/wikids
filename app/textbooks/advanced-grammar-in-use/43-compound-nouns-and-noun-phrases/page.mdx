## A — Noun + noun: the second noun names the class, the first names the type

In a compound made of **noun + noun**, the second noun usually gives the **general class** of thing the compound belongs to, and the first noun says **which type** within that class. Crucially, the first noun normally stays **singular**, even when the meaning is plural:

<SayIt text="I've written your number in my address book." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">An <strong>address book</strong> = a book for addresses — <em>not</em> "an addresses book". The book holds many addresses, but the first noun is still singular.</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="Keep the first noun singular">
  ✅ a <strong>shoe</strong> shop &nbsp;·&nbsp; a <strong>toothbrush</strong> &nbsp;·&nbsp; a <strong>car</strong> park &nbsp;·&nbsp; a <strong>book</strong> shop <br />
  ❌ a shoes shop &nbsp;·&nbsp; a teethbrush &nbsp;·&nbsp; a cars park
</Callout>

There are, however, several systematic **exceptions**.

**1. When the first noun only exists in a plural form**, it keeps that plural:

<div className="not-prose my-6 grid gap-2 sm:grid-cols-2">
  {[
    ["a savings account", "savings has no singular here"],
    ["a customs officer", "customs = border control"],
    ["a clothes shop", "compare a shoe shop"],
    ["the arms trade", "arms = weapons"],
  ].map(([w, note]) => (
    <div key={w} className="rounded-xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50 p-3">
      <p className="text-sm font-bold text-emerald-900">{w}</p>
      <p className="text-xs text-emerald-700">{note}</p>
    </div>
  ))}
</div>

<Callout type="info" title="When a plural first noun changes the meaning">
  Some of these contrast neatly with a singular form: <br />
  - <strong>a glasses case</strong> (= a case for your spectacles) &nbsp;vs&nbsp; <strong>a glass case</strong> (= a case made of glass) <br />
  - <strong>an arts festival</strong> (the arts = music, drama, film, dance, painting…) &nbsp;vs&nbsp; <strong>an art festival</strong> (art = painting, drawing and sculpture)
</Callout>

**2. When the compound names an institution, industry or department** that deals with *more than one kind* of thing, the first noun is plural:

<SayIt text="She works in the building materials industry." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Send the manuscript to the publications department." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">The industry handles many <em>materials</em>; the department handles many <em>publications</em> — so the first noun is plural.</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="Making a compound noun plural">
  To pluralise a compound noun, we normally pluralise the <strong>second</strong> noun, not the first: <br />
  one coal mine → two <strong>coal mines</strong> &nbsp;·&nbsp; an office-worker → office-<strong>workers</strong> &nbsp;·&nbsp; a tea leaf → tea <strong>leaves</strong>
</Callout>

## B — When to use noun + 's or noun + preposition + noun

Sometimes a plain `noun + noun` is wrong, and English prefers **noun + 's + noun** (the possessive) or **noun + preposition + noun** instead.

### Prefer noun + 's + noun…

**…when the first noun is the *user* — a person or animal — of the item in the second noun:**

<SayIt text="The baby's bedroom is right next to ours." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">Also: a <strong>lion's den</strong>, a <strong>women's clinic</strong>, a <strong>girls' school</strong>, <strong>birds' nests</strong>.</p>

**…when the item in the second noun is *produced by* the thing (often an animal) in the first:**

<SayIt text="This salad is delicious with a little goat's cheese." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">Also: <strong>duck's eggs</strong>, <strong>cow's milk</strong>.</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="…but watch the meat exceptions">
  When the food <em>is</em> the animal's body (meat), we use plain noun + noun, not the possessive: <br />
  ✅ <strong>lamb chops</strong> &nbsp;·&nbsp; <strong>chicken drumsticks</strong> &nbsp;&nbsp; (❌ lamb's chops, ❌ chicken's drumsticks)
</Callout>

**…when we talk about parts of *people or animals*** — but we use plain `noun + noun` for parts of *things*. Compare:

<div className="not-prose my-6 overflow-hidden rounded-2xl border-2 border-violet-200 shadow-sm">
  <div className="grid grid-cols-2 text-sm">
    <div className="bg-violet-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-violet-900">Part of a person / animal → 's</div>
    <div className="bg-sky-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-sky-900">Part of a thing → noun + noun</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">a woman's face</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">a pen top</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 bg-violet-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">a boy's arm</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 bg-sky-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">a computer keyboard</div>
  </div>
</div>

### Prefer noun + preposition + noun…

**…when we talk about a container together with its contents.** This contrasts sharply with the plain compound:

<div className="not-prose my-6 space-y-3">
  <div className="rounded-xl border border-slate-200 bg-white p-4 shadow-sm">
    <p className="text-sm text-slate-600"><strong className="text-sky-700">a cup of tea</strong> = a cup with tea in it (the contents matter)</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border border-slate-200 bg-white p-4 shadow-sm">
    <p className="text-sm text-slate-600"><strong className="text-sky-700">a tea cup</strong> = a cup designed for drinking tea from (the purpose matters)</p>
  </div>
</div>

**…when the combination does *not* name a well-known class of item.** A recognised category can be a compound; a one-off description usually takes a preposition:

<SayIt text="Everyone has to pay income tax." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1"><strong>income tax</strong> is a recognised class of tax — so the compound is fine.</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The government has proposed a tax on children's clothes." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">This isn't an established category, so we say <strong>a tax on children's clothes</strong>, not "a children's clothes tax".</p>

## C — Compound nouns from phrasal (two- and three-word) verbs

Some compound nouns are built from a verb plus a preposition or adverb, and relate to a **two- or three-word verb**. Compare the verb (two words, often written separately) with the noun (one word or hyphenated):

<div className="not-prose my-6 space-y-3">
  <div className="rounded-xl border border-slate-200 bg-white p-4 shadow-sm">
    <p className="text-sm text-slate-600"><span className="font-semibold text-sky-700">verb:</span> He <strong>broke out of</strong> the prison by dressing as a woman. <span className="text-slate-400">(= escaped)</span></p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm text-slate-600"><span className="font-semibold text-sky-700">noun:</span> There was a major <strong>break-out</strong> from the prison last night. <span className="text-slate-400">(= prisoners escaped)</span></p>
  </div>
</div>

Countable compound nouns like these form their plural by adding **-s** to the end, as normal:

<div className="not-prose my-6 grid grid-cols-2 gap-2 sm:grid-cols-4">
  {["read-outs", "push-ups", "intakes", "outcomes"].map((w) => (
    <span key={w} className="rounded-lg border-2 border-amber-200 bg-amber-50 px-3 py-2 text-center text-sm font-semibold text-amber-800">{w}</span>
  ))}
</div>

<Callout type="warning" title="…but a few pluralise the first part">
  A handful of these add the <strong>-s</strong> to the <em>first</em> element instead: <br />
  <strong>lookers-on</strong> (or <strong>onlookers</strong>) &nbsp;·&nbsp; <strong>runners-up</strong> &nbsp;·&nbsp; <strong>passers-by</strong> &nbsp;·&nbsp; <strong>hangers-on</strong>
</Callout>

<Callout type="info" title="One verb, two nouns">
  The same phrasal verb can give different nouns for different senses. <em>Break out</em> → a <strong>break-out</strong> (prisoners escaping) but an <strong>outbreak</strong> (of disease or war). Let the meaning, not the verb alone, choose the form.
</Callout>

## D — Hyphenated phrases before a noun

We can also form longer **hyphenated phrases** and place them directly *before* a noun to say more precisely what it refers to. They behave like a single adjective:

<SayIt text="She's just bought a state-of-the-art tablet PC." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1"><strong>state-of-the-art</strong> = very modern, the latest technology.</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Make sure the figures are up-to-date." />

<Callout type="tip" title="A useful family of these phrases">
  Many follow fixed patterns you can collect and reuse: <br />
  <strong>day-to-day</strong> (routine) &nbsp;·&nbsp; <strong>round-the-clock</strong> (all day and night) &nbsp;·&nbsp; <strong>down-to-earth</strong> (practical) &nbsp;·&nbsp; <strong>step-by-step</strong> (one stage at a time) &nbsp;·&nbsp; <strong>once-in-a-lifetime</strong> (very rare) &nbsp;·&nbsp; <strong>larger-than-life</strong> (exaggerated) &nbsp;·&nbsp; <strong>middle-of-the-road</strong> (moderate) &nbsp;·&nbsp; <strong>man / woman-in-the-street</strong> (ordinary person)
</Callout>
