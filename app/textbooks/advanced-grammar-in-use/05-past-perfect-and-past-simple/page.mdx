## A — Telling past events out of order

When we give an account of a sequence of past events, we usually put them in **chronological order** using the **past simple**. But when we want to refer to an event **out of order** — one that happened *before* the event we have just been describing — we use the **past perfect** (**had** + past participle).

Read this short email, where the writer mentions things in a different order from the one in which they happened:

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-2xl border-2 border-amber-200 bg-gradient-to-br from-amber-50 to-orange-50 p-5 shadow-sm">
  <p className="text-base leading-relaxed text-slate-700">
    I <strong className="text-sky-700">wrote</strong> Clara an email to thank her for the present she <strong className="text-violet-700">had given</strong> me for my birthday last week. But as soon as I pressed the <em>send</em> button, I <strong className="text-sky-700">realised</strong> that I <strong className="text-violet-700">had made</strong> a mistake — and sent it to her sister instead.
  </p>
</div>

<SayIt text="I wrote Clara an email to thank her for the present she had given me for my birthday." />
<br />
<SayIt text="As soon as I pressed send, I realised that I had made a mistake." />

The two **had**-verbs point back to events that happened *earlier* than the point the story has reached. Notice how the order the events are **mentioned** differs from the order they **happened**:

<div className="not-prose my-6 grid gap-3 sm:grid-cols-2">
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-violet-200 bg-violet-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-violet-700">order the events happened</p>
    <ol className="mt-2 list-decimal space-y-1 pl-5 text-sm text-violet-900">
      <li>gave present</li>
      <li>wrote email</li>
      <li>made mistake</li>
      <li>realised mistake</li>
    </ol>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-sky-200 bg-sky-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-sky-700">order the events are mentioned</p>
    <ol className="mt-2 list-decimal space-y-1 pl-5 text-sm text-sky-900">
      <li><strong>wrote</strong> email</li>
      <li><strong>had given</strong> present <span className="text-sky-500">(out of order)</span></li>
      <li><strong>realised</strong> mistake</li>
      <li><strong>had made</strong> mistake <span className="text-sky-500">(out of order)</span></li>
    </ol>
  </div>
</div>

<Callout type="tip" title="The core idea">
  The past simple keeps the main story moving forward in time. The past perfect is your way of stepping <em>back</em> behind that line to slot in something that had already happened.
</Callout>

## B — Once the earlier time is clear, drop the past perfect

When we **understand** that we are talking about events before another past event, we don't have to **keep** using the past perfect. After the first one has signalled the earlier time-frame, the past simple can take over with no loss of meaning:

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-2xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50 p-5 text-slate-700">
  <p className="leading-relaxed">
    We <strong>bought</strong> a new car last month. We<strong>'d driven</strong> <span className="text-slate-400">(or had driven)</span> my parents' old car for ages, but it <strong>started</strong> <span className="text-slate-400">(or had started)</span> to fall apart. We <strong>put</strong> <span className="text-slate-400">(or had put)</span> a new engine in it, but that <strong>didn't solve</strong> <span className="text-slate-400">(or hadn't solved)</span> the problems we were having.
  </p>
</div>

<SayIt text="We bought a new car last month. We'd driven our old one for ages, but it started to fall apart." />

<Callout type="info" title="Why this is allowed">
  The phrase <em>last month</em> and the first past perfect have already established that everything that follows belongs to an earlier stretch of time. With the order no longer in doubt, the simpler past simple reads more smoothly — repeating <em>had</em> in every clause would feel heavy.
</Callout>

## C — When the order is already clear, either tense works

If the order of past events is **clear from the context** — for example, when a **time expression or conjunction** (*after, before, by the time, earlier, already*) makes the sequence obvious — we can often use **either** the past perfect **or** the past simple:

<SayIt text="After Ivan had finished reading, he put out the light." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>After Ivan finished reading, he put out the light.</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The two leaders agreed to meet, even though earlier talks had failed to reach an agreement." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>... even though earlier talks failed to reach an agreement.</em>)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="Let the conjunction do the work">
  Words like <em>after</em>, <em>before</em> and <em>by the time</em> already pin down which event came first. Once they do, the past perfect becomes <strong>optional</strong> — it adds nothing the conjunction hasn't already told us.
</Callout>

## D — The past perfect in reporting

The past perfect is often used when we **report** what was originally said or thought in the **present perfect** or **past simple** (see also Unit 35). Crucially, the two original tenses behave differently:

| Original words | Reported with the past perfect |
|---|---|
| 'I **have met** him before.' | I was sure that I **had met** him before. *(not I met him …)* |
| 'The village **hasn't changed** much.' | I found that the village **hadn't changed** much. *(not the village didn't change …)* |
| '225 people **drowned** in the recent floods.' | Police said that 225 people **had drowned** in the recent floods. *(or … drowned …)* |
| 'I **stole** the watch.' | She admitted that she **had stolen** the watch. *(or … stole …)* |

<Callout type="warning" title="The split that catches people out">
  • If the original was a <strong>present perfect</strong> (<em>have met, hasn't changed</em>), you <strong>must</strong> use the past perfect — the past simple is wrong. <br />
  • If the original was a <strong>past simple</strong> (<em>drowned, stole</em>), you may use <strong>either</strong> the past perfect or the past simple.
</Callout>

## E — *had hoped*, *had planned*: intentions that came to nothing

We can use the past perfect (and often the past continuous too) with verbs of **hoping, intending and expecting** to talk about things we **meant to do but didn't** — or won't now do. The past perfect quietly signals that the plan fell through:

<SayIt text="I had hoped to visit the gallery before I left Florence, but it's closed on Mondays." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>I was hoping ...</em> / <em>I had been hoping ...</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Aron had planned to retire at 65, but we've persuaded him to stay on for a few more years." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>Aron planned ...</em> / <em>Aron was planning ...</em> / <em>Aron had been planning ...</em>)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="Verbs that take this 'unfulfilled' meaning">
  <em>hope, plan, intend, expect, mean, want, think</em> — in the past perfect they carry the unspoken ending <em>'... but it didn't happen.'</em> That implication is the whole point of choosing <em>had hoped</em> over a plain <em>hoped</em>.
</Callout>
