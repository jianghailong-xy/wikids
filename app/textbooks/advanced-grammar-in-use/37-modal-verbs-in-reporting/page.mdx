## A — The big picture: modals in reporting

When the original statement, question, suggestion, etc. contains a **modal verb** (*will, can, may, must, shall…*), that modal sometimes changes when you report what was said or thought — and sometimes it stays exactly as it was. Whether it shifts depends on **which modal** it is and on **whether the situation still exists or is still in the future**.

Here is the whole system in one table. The rest of the unit works through each row.

<Callout type="info" title="Why some modals can't shift">
  <em>Could, would, should, might, ought to</em> and the rest are already past or distanced forms — there is no further "back" for them to shift to. So they simply carry over unchanged. The work happens with the present-form modals: <em>will, can, may, shall, must</em>.
</Callout>

## B — Modals that stay the same

After a past reporting verb, **could, would, should, might, needn't, ought to, used to** and the *have* forms (*could have, should have*) don't change.

> 'I **could** meet you at the airport.'
<SayIt text="He said that he could meet us at the airport." />

> 'We **might** drop in if we have time.'
<SayIt text="They said they might drop in if they have time." />

> 'You **should have** contacted me earlier.'
<SayIt text="She said I should have contacted her earlier." />

<Callout type="tip" title="No backshift needed">
  Notice <em>they might drop in if they <strong>have</strong> time</em> — the present tense can stay too, because the situation is still open. The modal itself never moves: <em>might</em> stays <em>might</em>.
</Callout>

## C — will, can, may → would, could, might

These three modals **can** shift back to *would, could, might* — and often do:

> 'She **may** have already left.'
<SayIt text="He thought she might have already left." />

But you **keep** *will, can, may* when the situation you're reporting **still exists or is still in the future** *and* the reporting verb is in the **present** tense (see Unit 32):

> 'I**'ll** be in Paris at Christmas.'
<SayIt text="She tells me she'll be in Paris at Christmas." />

Compare a finished, past-framed warning, where the shift back is natural:

> 'Careful! You**'ll** fall through the ice!'
<SayIt text="I warned him he would fall through the ice." />

And when the situation still exists or is still in the future **but** the reporting verb is in the **past** tense, **either** form is fine — *will* or *would*, *can* or *could*, *may* or *might*:

> 'The problem **can** be solved.'
<SayIt text="They said the problem can — or could — be solved." />

<Callout type="warning" title="The deciding question">
  Ask yourself two things: <strong>Is the situation still true / still future?</strong> and <strong>Is the reporting verb present or past?</strong> &nbsp;Still-real + present verb → keep <em>will/can/may</em>. &nbsp;Still-real + past verb → either. &nbsp;Finished or no longer relevant → shift to <em>would/could/might</em>.
</Callout>

## D — shall → would or should

*Shall* has no report form of its own, so it becomes something else depending on its meaning.

**Talking about the future** (*shall / 'll*) → **would**:

> 'I **shall** (I'll) call you on Monday.'
<SayIt text="She told me she would call me on Monday." />

**Offers and asking for advice** (*Shall I…? Where shall…?*) → **should**:

> 'Where **shall** I put this box?'
<SayIt text="He asked where he should put the box." />

## E — must and mustn't in reporting

**must = necessity** → **must** *or* **had to** (had to is usually more natural in speech):

> 'You **must** be home by 9 o'clock.'
<SayIt text="She said I must — or had to — be home by 9 o'clock." />

**must = a conclusion** (working something out) → **must**, unchanged:

> 'I didn't hear the phone. I **must** be going deaf.'
<SayIt text="Alex thought he must be going deaf." />

**mustn't** → **mustn't**, unchanged:

> 'You **mustn't** tell my brother.'
<SayIt text="He warned me that I mustn't tell my brother." />

<Callout type="info" title="had to ≠ must, sometimes">
  When <em>must</em> means a conclusion (<em>he must be going deaf</em>), you cannot swap in <em>had to</em> — <em>had to</em> only reports the necessity meaning. Keep <em>must</em> for deductions.
</Callout>

## F — A modal in the report where there was none in the original

Sometimes the original sentence has **no modal at all**, yet the most natural report uses one — because a modal captures the meaning more economically.

> 'You're not allowed to smoke here.'
<SayIt text="She told me that I mustn't smoke there." />

> 'My advice is to look for a new job now.'
<SayIt text="She said that I should look for a new job now." />

<Callout type="tip" title="Reading the intention">
  An order or prohibition often reports as <em>must / mustn't</em>; advice as <em>should / ought to</em>; a refusal as <em>wouldn't</em>; a possibility as <em>might / could</em>; a permission as <em>could</em>. You're translating the speaker's <strong>intention</strong>, not just their words.
</Callout>
