At intermediate level you learned the headline contrast: the **past simple** (*I did*) for finished time, the **present perfect** (*I have done*) for time that reaches up to now. At advanced level the interesting cases are the ones where the *same* time expression — *this morning, today, this month* — could be either. The tense you pick doesn't depend on the event; it depends on **how you frame the time around it**.

## A — "This morning", "today": a finished period or one that's still open?

Time expressions that point to the present — **this morning / week / month** and **today** — go with **either** the past simple or the present perfect. The choice tells your listener how you're thinking about the period:

- If you treat it as a **past, completed** stretch of time → **past simple**.
- If you treat it as a period that **still includes the present moment** → **present perfect**.

<SayIt text="I didn't shave this morning." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(The morning is over and I didn't shave — finished period.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I haven't shaved this morning." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(It's still the morning, and I might shave later — the period is still open.)</p>

<div className="not-prose my-6 overflow-hidden rounded-2xl border-2 border-violet-200 shadow-sm">
  <div className="grid grid-cols-2 text-sm">
    <div className="bg-sky-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-sky-900">Past simple — finished time</div>
    <div className="bg-violet-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-violet-900">Present perfect — time up to now</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">the period is over and done</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">the period still has time left in it</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 bg-sky-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><em>I saw Anna this morning.</em> (it's now afternoon)</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 bg-violet-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><em>I've seen Anna this morning.</em> (it's still morning)</div>
  </div>
</div>

<Callout type="tip" title="The framing test">
  Ask yourself: <em>is this period still running?</em> If you've mentally closed it (it's evening; the day's work is done), reach for the past simple. If it's still open — there's time for the situation to change or recur — reach for the present perfect. The verb follows your sense of the <strong>time</strong>, not the other way round.
</Callout>

## B — Time clauses with *since*

In a sentence with a time clause introduced by **since**, we normally pair a **past simple** verb in the *since*-clause with a **present perfect** verb in the main clause. The *since*-clause marks a **single point in the past** — the moment something started:

<SayIt text="Since Mr Dodson became president, unemployment has increased." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Not <em>… has become president …</em> — becoming president was one moment.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="She hasn't been able to play tennis since she broke her arm." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Not <em>… since she has broken her arm</em>.)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="The exception worth knowing">
  Use the present perfect in the <em>since</em>-clause too when <strong>both</strong> situations stretch all the way to the present:
  <br /><br />
  ✅ <em>Have you met any of your neighbours <strong>since you've lived</strong> here?</em> &nbsp;(not <em>since you lived</em>) <br />
  Living here isn't a single past point — it's an ongoing state that reaches now, so it takes the present perfect, exactly like the main clause.
</Callout>

## C — Time clauses with *after, when, until, as soon as, once, by the time*

After these conjunctions — and the time expressions **the minute / second / moment** — the tense draws a sharp line:

- **past simple** → the event is **past and completed**;
- **present perfect** → the event lies in the **future**.

<SayIt text="After she left hospital, she had a long holiday." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(past → past)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="After I've finished school, I'll be spending six months in India." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(future → future: the present perfect in the time clause, will in the main clause)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The minute I got the news about Anna, I telephoned my parents." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(past → past)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I'll contact you the minute I've got my exam results." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(future)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="Two interchangeable variants">
  In sentences like these you can swap in the <strong>past perfect</strong> for the past simple (<em>After she <strong>had left</strong> hospital …</em>) and the <strong>present simple</strong> for the present perfect (<em>After Lucas <strong>leaves</strong> …</em>) with no real change in meaning. See Unit 5 for the past-perfect side of this.
</Callout>

## D — The present perfect in news reports

Listen to any news bulletin and you'll hear the pattern: a **recent event is announced with the present perfect**, then the **details follow in the past simple** (or other past tenses).

<div className="not-prose my-6 space-y-4">
  <div className="overflow-hidden rounded-2xl border-2 border-rose-200 shadow-sm">
    <div className="bg-rose-600 px-4 py-1.5 text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-widest text-white">Breaking news</div>
    <div className="bg-rose-50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-800">
      <p>A Russian spacecraft <strong>has returned</strong> safely to Earth with its two passengers. US astronaut Scott Keane and Russian cosmonaut Olga Kaleri <strong>landed</strong> in the early hours of Wednesday.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div className="overflow-hidden rounded-2xl border-2 border-amber-200 shadow-sm">
    <div className="bg-amber-500 px-4 py-1.5 text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-widest text-white">In other news</div>
    <div className="bg-amber-50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-800">
      <p>An American woman <strong>has become</strong> the first person to make 2 million contributions to Wikipedia. Esther Miller <strong>began</strong> editing the site eight years ago.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<Callout type="tip" title="Headline then detail">
  The present perfect says <em>this just happened and it matters now</em>. Once the audience has the headline, the reporter zooms in on the specifics — <em>when, where, who</em> — and those anchored, finished facts switch to the past simple.
</Callout>

## E — *It / This / That is the first time …*

After the pattern **It / This / That is / will be the first time …**, the clause that follows normally takes the **present perfect**:

<SayIt text="That's the first time I've seen Jan look embarrassed." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(reporting a past event)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="It won't be the first time she has voted against the government." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(talking about a future event)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="… but 'was the first time' takes the past perfect">
  When the opening verb is past — <strong>It / This / That was the first time …</strong> — the following clause shifts to the <strong>past perfect</strong> (see Unit 5):
  <br /><br />
  <em>It was the first time I'<strong>d talked</strong> to Dimitra outside the office.</em>
</Callout>
