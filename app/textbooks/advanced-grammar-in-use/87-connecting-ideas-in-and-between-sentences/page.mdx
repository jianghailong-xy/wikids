## A — Sentence connectors vs conjunctions

At advanced level the difference between two families of linking words matters, because it decides your punctuation.

**Sentence connectors** join one sentence to a **previous** sentence or sentences. They often (but not always) sit at the beginning of the sentence:

<SayIt text="There was no heating in the building. As a result, the workers had to be sent home." />

**Conjunctions** join clauses **within** a single sentence:

<SayIt text="While I was waiting, I read a magazine." />
<br />
<SayIt text="I stood up so that I could see better." />

Here is the working reference. Read across each row: the same idea can usually be expressed either *between* sentences (a connector) or *within* one (a conjunction).

| Type of connection | Sentence connectors — link *between* sentences | Conjunctions — link *within* a sentence |
|---|---|---|
| **Contrast / the unexpected** | after all, all the same, alternatively, anyway, by contrast, even so, however *(but see C)*, in any case, in contrast, instead, nevertheless, on the contrary, on the other hand | although, even though, though, whereas, while, yet |
| **Reasons & results** | as a consequence, as a result, consequently, for one thing, so; hence, in consequence, therefore, thus *(the last four rather formal)* | as, because, for, in that, since, insofar as, so, so that |
| **Adding information** | above all, after all, also, besides, furthermore, in addition, likewise, moreover, similarly, what's more; as well, too *(the last two not at the start of a sentence)* | — |
| **Condition** | if not, if so, otherwise | as long as, assuming (that), if, on condition that, provided (that), so long as, supposing (that), unless |
| **Time — one event at the same time as another** | at that time, at the same time, meanwhile | as, when, whenever, while |
| **Time — one event before or after another** | after, after that, afterwards, before, before that, earlier, later, previously, soon, subsequently, then | after, as soon as, before, since, until |

<Callout type="info" title="Words that do both jobs">
  <strong>after</strong>, <strong>before</strong> and <strong>so</strong> can be <em>either</em> a connector or a conjunction, depending on the sentence — so don't assume a word belongs to only one column.
</Callout>

The punctuation trap is the whole point of this unit:

<Callout type="warning" title="A connector can't join two clauses with just a comma">
  ❌ I expect to be promoted, <strong>if not</strong> I'll leave. <br />
  A comma alone can't glue two clauses together around a sentence connector.<br /><br />
  ✅ I expect to be promoted. <strong>If not</strong>, I'll leave. &nbsp;(two sentences)<br />
  ✅ I expect to be promoted; <strong>if not</strong>, I'll leave. &nbsp;(a semi-colon)
</Callout>

A connector **can** appear inside one sentence if the clauses are already joined by **and, but, or, so** or by a **semi-colon (;)**, **colon (:)** or **dash (–)**:

<SayIt text="The building was extremely well constructed and, as a result, difficult to demolish." />
<br />
<SayIt text="You could fly via Singapore; however, this isn't the only way." />

## B — even though vs even so

These two look alike and mean almost opposite things about *where* they connect.

**Even though** is a **conjunction**. It says that a fact doesn't make the rest of the sentence untrue, and it links ideas **within** one sentence (see also Unit 82):

<SayIt text="Even though it was midday, I put on the light." />

**Even so** is a **sentence connector**. It introduces a fact that is surprising given what was just said, and it links ideas **between** sentences:

<SayIt text="It was midday. Even so, I put on the light." />

<Callout type="tip" title="Same idea, two builds">
  Both sentences say <em>midday + light on = surprising</em>. Use <strong>even though</strong> to fold the two clauses into <em>one</em> sentence; use <strong>even so</strong> to keep them as <em>two</em>. Swapping them breaks the grammar: <span className="whitespace-nowrap">❌ <em>Even so it was midday, I put on the light.</em></span>
</Callout>

## C — however: three different words

**However** is most often a sentence connector (*= but / nevertheless*). But watch for two other uses that trip advanced learners up:

**1. As an adverb**, when it is followed by an **adjective**, **adverb**, or **much / many** — it means "to whatever degree":

<SayIt text="We just don't have the money to do the work, however necessary you think it is." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>however necessary</em> = no matter how necessary)</p>

**2. As a conjunction**, when it means "in whatever way":

<SayIt text="However she held the mirror, she couldn't see the back of her neck." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>However she held it</em> = in whatever way she held it)</p>

  </div>
