This unit pulls Units 1–7 together. There's no new tense here — just the question that every previous unit has been circling: given a present or past moment, do you focus on the **activity** (continuous), the **fact** (simple), the **connection to a later time** (perfect), or some **combination** of these? Three choices, made together, decide the verb form.

## A — Continuous and simple

When we focus on an **activity itself** — something that started before, is going on at, and possibly continues beyond a particular point of time — rather than on an action as a **completed event**, we use **continuous** forms.

<SayIt text="Ingrid can't come to the phone. She's washing her hair." />
<br />
<SayIt text="As you're not using your car at the moment, can I borrow it?" />
<br />
<SayIt text="This time yesterday I was flying over the Pacific." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Past continuous — in progress at a particular past moment.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Was she wearing that red dress when you saw her?" />

We use **simple** forms to talk about **general situations, habits, and things that are or were always true** — and, in the past, about **completed events**.

<SayIt text="When I worked as a postman I got up at three o'clock every morning." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(A past habit, viewed as a whole — not one morning in progress.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Miguel doesn't play golf very often." />
<br />
<SayIt text="These birds build their nests on the ground." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(A general truth.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The earthquake struck the area at midday yesterday." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Past simple for a completed event.)</p>

We use simple forms with verbs that describe **unchanging states** — verbs that stay the same rather than name an action:

<SayIt text="She intends to work hard at school and go on to university." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Did you understand the instructions we were given?" />

<Callout type="tip" title="...but a state verb can take the continuous when the state is changing or temporary">
  When a normally-stative verb describes something <em>happening</em> or <em>developing</em> rather than a fixed state, the continuous becomes available — and the meaning shifts towards a process in progress:
  <br /><br />
  <SayIt text="She was intending to talk to Tony about the idea, but she didn't get the opportunity." />
  <br />
  <SayIt text="I'm understanding physics much better now that Mr Davies is teaching us." />
  <p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Not a fixed state of understanding, but a gradual change — so the continuous fits. See Units 1–2.)</p>
</Callout>

## B — Perfect

We use **perfect** verb forms to describe one event or state **from the point of view of a later time**. The **present perfect** suggests a connection between something that happened in the past and the **present**.

<SayIt text="I've finished that book you wanted, so you can borrow it now." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Finishing it is past; the relevance — it's available now — is present.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Have you turned the heating off? I don't like it to be on when I'm not at home." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Your nose is bleeding. Has somebody hit you?" />

<Callout type="info" title="The event need not reach the present — only matter to it">
  This is the point people miss. The situation or event <strong>does not have to continue until the time of speaking</strong>; it only has to have some <strong>connection or relevance</strong> to the present. The nose was hit at some past moment — what makes the present perfect right is that the result (the bleeding) matters <em>now</em>.
</Callout>

The **past perfect** does the same job, but the "later point of view" is itself **in the past**: it locates a past event **before another past event**.

<SayIt text="I invited Dan to dinner, but he said he had already eaten." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(The eating came before the invitation — both in the past.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="By the time I picked up the phone, they had rung off." />

## C — Combinations of perfect and continuous

Combine the two and you get the **continuous perfect** forms, which keep the perfect's "view from a later time" but add the continuous's focus on an **activity in progress**.

The **present perfect continuous** describes an activity in progress **at, or recently before, the time of speaking** — and possibly continuing beyond it.

<SayIt text="I have been following the discussions on the forum with great interest." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Look at the dirt on your clothes! Have you been digging in the garden again?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(The digging may be finished — but its recent activity explains the present mess.)</p>

The **past perfect continuous** has a similar meaning, but the point of reference is **not 'now'** (as it is with the present perfect continuous) — it is a **point in the past**.

<SayIt text="When we met Lena and Marko, they had been crying." />
<br />
<SayIt text="It had been snowing heavily for hours, and when I went to the door I couldn't open it." />

  <div className="grid grid-cols-[1fr_auto] text-sm">

    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 bg-sky-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>had been doing</strong> — it had been snowing.</div>
  </div>

<Callout type="tip" title="One sentence, three decisions">
  Before you commit to any verb form, run the three questions of this unit in order. <strong>1)</strong> Am I viewing this as an <em>activity in progress</em> or a <em>fact / completed event</em>? (continuous vs simple) <strong>2)</strong> Am I looking at it <em>from a later time</em>? (perfect or not) <strong>3)</strong> Is that later time <em>now</em> or a <em>past moment</em>? (present vs past). Answer all three and only one form is left standing.
</Callout>
