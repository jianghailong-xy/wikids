## A — *need* as an ordinary verb or a modal verb

**need** leads a double life. It can be an **ordinary verb** (it changes its tense and adds *-s* in the third person, and it is followed by a *to*-infinitive or a noun), or a **modal verb** (it doesn't change its tense, takes no *-s*, and is followed by a **bare infinitive**). Compare:

<SayIt text="I needed to leave early." />
<SayIt text="She's thirsty. She needs a drink." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(ordinary verb — tense and <em>-s</em> as normal)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="You needn't speak so loudly." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(modal verb — bare infinitive, no <em>to</em>, no <em>-s</em>)</p>

As a modal verb, **need** is most commonly used in **negative** sentences:

<SayIt text="I've already cleaned the car, so you needn't bother to do it." />
<br />
<SayIt text="I was very nervous before the interview, but I needn't have worried — I got the job!" />

<Callout type="info" title="Verbs that attract needn't">
  A handful of verbs turn up again and again after <strong>need not / needn't</strong>: <em>apply, concern, fear, involve, mean, panic</em>. <br />
  <em>You needn't <strong>panic</strong> — there's plenty of time.</em> · <em>The new rule needn't <strong>concern</strong> you.</em>
</Callout>

Modal **need** is sometimes used in **questions**, but we usually prefer ordinary-verb **need** or **have to**:

<SayIt text="Need you go so soon?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(modal — rather formal)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Do you need to go so soon?  /  Do you have to go so soon?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(ordinary verb / have to — the everyday choice)</p>

Modal **need** is **rare in affirmative** sentences (that is, ones that are neither questions nor negatives), but it is sometimes found in written English, particularly in fiction:

<SayIt text="We need have no fear for Nicole; she can take care of herself." />

In other kinds of formal written English it appears in this affirmative way alongside **negative words** such as *hardly, never, nobody / no one* and *only*:

<SayIt text="The changes need only be small to make the proposals acceptable." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(less formally: The changes only need to be small ...)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Nobody ever need know about the money." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(less formally: Nobody ever needs to know ...)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="'I don't want my parents to know.' 'They need never find out.'" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(less formally: They never need to find out.)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="needn't have done ≠ didn't need to do">
  Both talk about the past, but they say opposite things about what happened. <br />
  <strong>I needn't have worried</strong> = I <em>did</em> worry, but it turned out to be unnecessary. <br />
  <strong>I didn't need to worry</strong> = it was unnecessary — and usually the worrying never happened. <br />
  (The book cross-refers here to the Grammar Reminder <strong>C25–C28</strong> on modal verbs.)
</Callout>

## B — *needn't* and *don't need to*: permission and general necessity

To give someone **permission not to do** something, you can use **either** **needn't** or **don't need to**:

<SayIt text="You needn't cut the grass — I'll do it later." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or: You don't need to cut the grass ...)</p>

But to talk about a **general necessity** — a rule or a fact that holds for people in general — we prefer **don't need to**:

<SayIt text="You don't need to be over 18 to get into a nightclub." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(rather than: You needn't be over 18 ...)</p>

## C — *needn't* and *don't have to*: saying something is unnecessary

To say that it is **unnecessary** to do something, we can often use **either** **needn't** or **don't have to**:

<SayIt text="You needn't whisper. Nobody can hear us." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or: You don't have to whisper ...)</p>

However, some speakers draw a fine distinction: they prefer **needn't** when it is **the speaker** who decides that something is unnecessary, and **don't have to** when **somebody else, or an external rule**, makes it unnecessary. Compare:

<SayIt text="You needn't come in until ten tomorrow morning." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(the speaker's own decision)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="We've been told that we don't have to be at work until ten tomorrow." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(reporting someone else's decision)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="Who lifts the obligation?">
  It's the same test you met with <em>must</em> vs <em>have to</em> (Unit 18), just turned negative: <strong>needn't</strong> leans towards the speaker's authority here-and-now; <strong>don't have to</strong> leans towards rules and other people. When it doesn't matter, either is fine.
</Callout>

## D — *needn't* / *don't have to* for 'not necessarily true' — never *mustn't*

We can use **needn't** (or **don't have to**) to say that something is **not necessarily true**. We do **not** use **mustn't** in this way (see also Unit 18C):

<SayIt text="Volcanoes don't have to erupt constantly to be classified as 'active'." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or: Volcanoes needn't erupt constantly ...; <strong>not</strong> Volcanoes mustn't erupt ...)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Nowadays it needn't cost a fortune to own an e-book reader." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or: Nowadays it doesn't have to cost ...; <strong>not</strong> Nowadays it mustn't cost ...)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't reach for mustn't">
  <strong>mustn't</strong> means <em>it is forbidden / it is essential that you don't</em> — a prohibition. It can never mean <em>it isn't necessarily so</em>. To deny a conclusion, use <strong>needn't</strong> or <strong>don't have to</strong>: <br />
  ✅ A high price <strong>needn't</strong> mean high quality. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ A high price mustn't mean high quality.
</Callout>
