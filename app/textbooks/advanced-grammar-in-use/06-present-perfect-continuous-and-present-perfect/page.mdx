Both tenses reach from the past **up to now** — that's why they feel so close. The choice turns on what you are pointing at: the **activity and how long it lasted** (*have been doing*), or its **result, completion, or quantity** (*have done*). This unit sorts out when each is natural, and where only one will do.

<Callout type="info" title="Forms at a glance">
  **Present perfect continuous:** has / have + <strong>been</strong> + <em>-ing</em> &nbsp;→&nbsp; <em>She has been writing.</em> <br />
  **Present perfect:** has / have + <strong>past participle</strong> &nbsp;→&nbsp; <em>She has written.</em>
</Callout>

## A — An activity in progress until recently (how long)

We use the **present perfect continuous** to express the idea of an activity — a task, a piece of work, etc. — **in progress until recently or until the time of speaking**:

<SayIt text="Have you been working in the garden all day? You look exhausted." />
<br />
<SayIt text="She's been writing the book since she was in her twenties, and at last it's finished." />

<div className="not-prose my-6 flex items-center gap-3 text-sm text-slate-500">
  <span className="font-semibold text-violet-700">past</span>
  <span className="h-0.5 flex-1 bg-gradient-to-r from-violet-300 to-violet-500" />
  <span className="rounded-full border border-violet-300 bg-violet-50 px-3 py-1 font-semibold text-violet-800">now</span>
</div>

Note that we often use time expressions like **since** and **for** to say **how long** the activity has been in progress.

<Callout type="warning" title="Not with state verbs">
  Don't use the present perfect continuous with verbs that describe **unchanging states** — <strong>belong, know, (dis)like, understand</strong>: <br />
  ✅ Have you <strong>known</strong> each other long? &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ Have you been knowing each other long? <br />
  ✅ I <strong>haven't liked</strong> ice cream since I ate too much and was sick. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ I haven't been liking ice cream ...
</Callout>

When we talk about **situations** — general characteristics or circumstances that exist until the present — we can often use **either** form with no real difference:

<SayIt text="We've been looking forward to this holiday for ages." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>We've looked forward to this holiday for ages.</em> — both work here.)</p>

## B — Recently finished, with results you can still see (process vs result)

We often use **either** tense for something that has **recently finished** when we can still see its results. But we generally prefer the **continuous** with verbs that suggest **extended or repeated** activity. Compare:

<SayIt text="He's broken his finger and is in a lot of pain." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>not</em> He's been breaking ... — a single, instant event, not extended.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I've been playing squash and need a shower!" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(more likely than <em>I've played ...</em> — an extended activity whose effect we can see.)</p>

We choose the **continuous** when we **draw a conclusion** from what we can see, hear, etc. — often to **complain or criticise**:

<SayIt text="Who's been messing around with my papers? They're all over the place." />
<br />
<SayIt text="You've been eating chocolate, haven't you? There's some on your shirt." />

<Callout type="tip" title="The evidence test">
  If you are reading a situation off the visible <em>evidence</em> — and especially if you are grumbling about it — reach for <strong>have been doing</strong>. The continuous puts the spotlight on the activity that left the trace, not just the end state.
</Callout>

Now the key contrast. When we talk about the **result** of an activity (an amount, a finished quantity), we use the **present perfect**, *not* the continuous. When we focus on the **process**, either form is usually fine:

  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-sky-200 bg-sky-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-sky-700">result — the figure</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm text-sky-900">Prices <strong>have decreased</strong> by 7%.</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-xs text-sky-700/80">(<em>not</em> have been decreasing by 7% — the 7% is a completed result.)</p>
    <p className="mt-3 text-sm text-sky-900">I<strong>'ve used</strong> three tins of paint on the walls.</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-xs text-sky-700/80">(<em>not</em> I've been using three tins — three tins is a finished quantity.)</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-violet-200 bg-violet-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-violet-700">process — either form</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm text-violet-900">Prices <strong>have been decreasing</strong> recently.</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-xs text-violet-700/80">(or Prices <strong>have decreased</strong> recently.)</p>
    <p className="mt-3 text-sm text-violet-900">I<strong>'ve been using</strong> a new kind of paint on the walls.</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-xs text-violet-700/80">(or I<strong>'ve used</strong> a new kind of paint.)</p>
  </div>
</div>

## C — Ongoing and repeated vs once or a set number (how long vs how many)

The **present perfect continuous** emphasises that an activity is **ongoing and repeated**; the **present perfect** suggests it happened **only once, or on a specified number of occasions**:

<SayIt text="Miguel has been kicking a football against the wall all day." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(more likely than <em>... has kicked ...</em> — an ongoing, repeated activity.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="He has played for the national team in 65 matches so far." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>not</em> He has been playing ... in 65 matches — a counted total, <em>how many</em>.)</p>

The same split shows up with *call for*:

<SayIt text="The workers have been calling for the chairman's resignation." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= a number of times, probably over an extended period.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Workers have called for management to begin negotiations on pay." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= maybe a number of times, or only once.)</p>
