## A — Verbs that are both transitive and intransitive

Many verbs can be used **either transitively** (with an object) **or intransitively** (without one). The switch lets us focus on different things: the **transitive** use puts the spotlight on the person or thing *performing* the action; the **intransitive** use puts it on the person or thing *affected* by it.

<SayIt text="She closed the door." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">Transitive — we name the agent (<em>she</em>) who did it.</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The door closed." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">Intransitive — the affected thing (<em>the door</em>) is now the subject; the agent disappears.</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I've ripped my shirt." />
&nbsp;·&nbsp;
<SayIt text="My shirt has ripped." />

These are often called **'change' verbs** (sometimes *ergative* verbs), because the intransitive version describes a change happening *to* something without saying who or what caused it:

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-violet-200 bg-violet-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-violet-700">used both transitively and intransitively</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-violet-900">begin · bend · break · burn · change · decrease · drop · finish · increase · move · open · shut · start · vary · wake</p>
</div>

<Callout type="tip" title="Why the intransitive form is so useful">
  The intransitive use is the everyday way to report a change when the cause is unknown, obvious, or simply not the point: <em>The temperature dropped overnight.</em> · <em>Prices have increased again.</em> · <em>The meeting finished early.</em> No agent needed.
</Callout>

## B — Transitive verbs used without an object

Some normally transitive verbs can drop their object altogether **when the meaning is clear from the context** — the missing object is understood.

<SayIt text="I often sing (songs) in the shower." />
<br />
<SayIt text="She plays (the saxophone) beautifully." />

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-emerald-700">object can be left out when it's understood</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-emerald-900">answer · ask · change · cook · dance · drink · drive · eat · fail · park · phone · read · smoke · study · wash · wash up · wave · win · write</p>
</div>

<Callout type="info" title="Not the same as section A">
  In **A**, dropping the object changes the subject (<em>The door closed</em>). Here in **B**, the subject stays the same and we simply leave an understood object unspoken: <em>I was reading</em> (= reading something), <em>Nobody answered</em> (= answered the phone/the question). The agent is still doing the action.
</Callout>

## C — Verbs that need a complement

After some verbs we usually have to add a **complement** — a phrase (often an **adverb** or **prepositional phrase**) that completes the meaning. The verb sounds unfinished without it.

<SayIt text="The disease originated in Britain." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">Not <em>The disease originated.</em> — we need to say where or how it began.</p>

Other verbs **usually** take a complement but can manage without one. Compare:

<SayIt text="He paused for a few moments." />
&nbsp;·&nbsp;
<SayIt text="He paused." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">Both fine — the complement adds detail but isn't obligatory.</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="The trap">
  Verbs like <em>originate, refer, head (for), rely</em> feel complete but aren't: ❌ <em>The river originates.</em> → ✅ <em>The river originates <strong>in the mountains</strong>.</em> Always check whether the verb is leaning on a phrase that has to be there.
</Callout>

## D — Verb + preposition + object

Some verbs are followed by a **particular preposition (or prepositions)** and *then* an object. The preposition is part of the verb's pattern — you can't leave it out (see also Unit 94).

<SayIt text="We had to deal with hundreds of complaints." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">Not <em>We had to deal.</em></p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I'm sure that blue car belongs to Murad." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">Not <em>I'm sure that blue car belongs.</em></p>

<div className="not-prose my-6 grid gap-3 sm:grid-cols-2">
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-sky-200 bg-sky-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-sky-700">verb + preposition + object</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-sky-900">adhere to · aspire to · culminate in / with · differentiate between · incline to / towards · specialise in</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border border-slate-200 bg-white p-4 text-sm text-slate-600 shadow-sm">
    <p>The verb + preposition behaves as a unit: <em>She <strong>aspires to</strong> the leadership.</em> · <em>The talks <strong>culminated in</strong> a deal.</em> · <em>He can't <strong>differentiate between</strong> the two.</em></p>
  </div>
</div>

## E — Verb + object + prepositional complement

Other verbs take an **object first**, then a **prepositional complement**. Both parts are needed.

<SayIt text="I always associate pizza with Italy." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">Not <em>I always associate pizza.</em></p>
<br />
<SayIt text="She put the report on the floor." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">Not <em>She put the report.</em> — <em>put</em> demands a place.</p>

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-amber-200 bg-amber-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-amber-700">verb + object + preposition + object</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-amber-900">attribute … to · base … on / upon · equate … with · inflict … on · mistake … for · regard … as · remind … of</p>
</div>

## F — Verb + object + adjective complement

Some verbs are often followed by an **object + an adjective (or adjective phrase)** complement — the adjective says something about the object.

<SayIt text="Conti pronounced herself fit for the match." />

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-rose-200 bg-rose-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-rose-700">verb + object + adjective</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-rose-900">assume · believe · consider · declare · find · judge · prove · report · think</p>
  <p className="mt-2 text-xs text-rose-700">After <strong>declare, find, pronounce</strong> and <strong>prove</strong>, the object is usually a <strong>reflexive pronoun</strong>: <em>declared herself bankrupt</em>, <em>found himself unable to speak</em>.</p>
</div>

This object + adjective pattern is rather **formal**. To make a sentence less formal, add **to be** after the object, or use a **that-clause**:

<SayIt text="Dr Adams argues that house prices will fall, but other economists believe the opposite true." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">More formal — bare adjective.</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="… other economists believe the opposite to be true." />
&nbsp;·&nbsp;
<SayIt text="… other economists believe that the opposite is true." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">Less formal — <em>to be</em> + adjective, or a <em>that</em>-clause.</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="Quick register check">
  Bare adjective = formal/written: <em>The court found him liable.</em> Add <em>to be</em> or a <em>that</em>-clause for everyday speech: <em>The court found that he was liable.</em>
</Callout>
