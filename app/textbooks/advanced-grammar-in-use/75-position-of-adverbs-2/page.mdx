In Unit 74 you saw the three slots an adverb can fill — **front**, **mid** and **end**. This unit looks more closely at which adverbs prefer which slot, and at the cases where moving an adverb changes either the **emphasis** or the **meaning** of the whole sentence.

## A — Front position

Most types of adverb can go in **front position** (before the subject). Three kinds gravitate there in particular, because each one ties what you are about to say back to what came before:

    <p className="mt-1 text-sm text-emerald-900">make the logical relation to the previous sentence immediately clear.</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-sky-200 bg-sky-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-sky-700">time &amp; place adverbs</p>

  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-violet-200 bg-violet-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-violet-700">comment &amp; viewpoint adverbs</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm text-violet-900">flag the speaker's attitude to what is coming (see Unit 78).</p>
  </div>

<SayIt text="The value of the yen has fallen. As a result, Japan faces a crisis." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The last few days have been hot. Tomorrow the weather will be much cooler." />
<br />
<SayIt text="She has just heard that her sister is ill. Presumably, she will want to go home." />

<Callout type="info" title="Front is the default, not the only option">
  Note, however, that <em>other</em> positions are also possible for these adverbs — front position is simply where they most naturally fall when they connect a clause to its surroundings.
</Callout>

**Comment adverb or adverb of manner?** Some words can work either way — and the position tells you which. As a **comment adverb** it usually goes in front position and relates to the **whole clause**; as an **adverb of manner** it usually goes in end position and modifies just the **verb**. Compare:

<SayIt text="Naturally, I'll do all I can to help." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(comment — <em>of course</em>; it colours the whole statement)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The radioactive gas occurs naturally in many areas." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(manner — <em>in a natural way</em>; it modifies <em>occurs</em>)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="Front position for emphasis">
  For special emphasis or focus, adverbs that <em>usually</em> sit in mid position (see <strong>B</strong>) or end position can be brought to the front: <br />
  <em><strong>In May</strong>, Maxine had a baby.</em> &nbsp;·&nbsp; <em><strong>Regularly</strong>, Helena works on several paintings at once.</em>
</Callout>

## B — Mid position

Three families of adverb usually go in **mid position** — that is, before the main verb, or after the first auxiliary, or after the verb *be*:

**1. Degree adverbs** — *almost, hardly, nearly, quite, rather, scarcely*:

<SayIt text="The street lighting was so bad that we almost missed the turning." />

Some degree adverbs (*completely, enormously, entirely, greatly, slightly*) can go in **end position** as well as mid:

<SayIt text="I admire your work greatly. / I greatly admire your work." />

<Callout type="warning" title="Some degree adverbs resist certain verbs">
  A few degree adverbs are <em>not</em> normally used in mid position with particular verbs: <br />
  <strong>enormously</strong> avoids mid position with <em>develop, differ, go up, vary</em> → ✅ rates <strong>differ enormously</strong> &nbsp; ❌ rates enormously differ <br />
  <strong>greatly</strong> avoids mid position with <em>care, suffer</em> → ✅ he <strong>suffered greatly</strong> &nbsp; ❌ he greatly suffered
</Callout>

**2. Adverbs that mark the order of events** — *first, last, next*. They usually come **before** a phrase that gives the time of the event, but can also go in end position. We don't normally put them in front position — except when listing actions:

<SayIt text="I first met her in 1997. / I met her first in 1997." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Next, add three teaspoons of sugar." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(front position is fine here because we're listing steps)</p>

**3. Adverbs of frequency** that say in an *indefinite* way how often something happens — *hardly ever, often, rarely, regularly, seldom*, plus *always* and *never*:

<SayIt text="We hardly ever see Kaspar nowadays — he's so busy at the office." />

<Callout type="info" title="Multi-word frequency phrases stay out of mid position">
  Adverbial <em>phrases</em> of indefinite frequency — <em>as a rule, on many occasions, from time to time, every so often</em> — go in front or end position, never mid: <br />
  ✅ <strong>As a rule</strong>, I go every six months. &nbsp; ✅ … every six months, <strong>as a rule</strong>. &nbsp; ❌ I <strong>as a rule</strong> go …
</Callout>

## C — Long adverbials, prepositional and noun phrases

We rarely drop **long adverbials** — including clauses (see Units 58 and 59) — and **prepositional** or **noun phrases** into mid position. They normally go in **end position**, or are moved to **front position for emphasis**. Compare:

<SayIt text="She phoned home, anxious for news. / Anxious for news, she phoned home." />
<br />
<SayIt text="He picked up the vase with great care. / With great care he picked up the vase." />
<br />
<SayIt text="I'd seen Tarik the day before. / The day before, I'd seen Tarik." />

<Callout type="tip" title="The quick map">
  <strong>Front</strong> = connect, comment, or emphasise. &nbsp; <strong>Mid</strong> = degree, order, frequency (single words). &nbsp; <strong>End</strong> = manner, and anything long or phrasal.
</Callout>
