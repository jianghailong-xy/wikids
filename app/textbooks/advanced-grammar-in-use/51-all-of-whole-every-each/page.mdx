This unit gathers the words English uses for **totality** and for the members that make it up — *all (of)*, *whole* and *entire*, and the pair *every* and *each*. They overlap enough to feel interchangeable, but each has a position it prefers, a noun type it takes, and a shade of meaning the others can't quite reach. Getting them right comes down to three questions: **where** does the word sit in the clause, is the noun **singular or plural**, and are you viewing the group as **one mass** or as its **separate members**?

## A — All (of): where it goes

We sometimes put **all** *after* the noun or pronoun it refers to, spreading the meaning across the whole subject:

<SayIt text="His songs all sound much the same to me." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>All [of] his songs sound ...</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="We all think Kushi's working too hard." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>All of us think ...</em>)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="Put all after be — and after the first auxiliary">
  We usually put <strong>all</strong> after the verb <strong>be</strong>, and after the first auxiliary verb if there is one:<br /><br />
  ✅ They are <strong>all</strong> going to Athens during the vacation. — <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>They all are going ...</em><br />
  ✅ You should <strong>all</strong> have three question papers. — <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>You all should have ...</em><br /><br />
  In spoken English, though, we <em>can</em> say 'You <strong>all</strong> should have ...' for particular emphasis. When there is no be or auxiliary, <strong>all</strong> takes the same mid-position, before the main verb: <em>The children <strong>all</strong> started to speak at once.</em>
</Callout>

## B — Negatives: not all (of) vs none of

To make a negative with **all (of)** we normally use **not all (of)** rather than **all ... not** (although *all ... not* does turn up in informal spoken English):

<SayIt text="Not all (of) the seats were taken." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>The seats were <strong>not all</strong> taken.</em>)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="'Not all' and 'none of' do not mean the same thing">
  <strong>Not all (of)</strong> my cousins were at the wedding. (= <em>some</em> of them were there) <br />
  <strong>None of</strong> my cousins were at the wedding. (= <em>not one</em> of them was there)
</Callout>

## C — All and whole (and entire)

Before **singular countable nouns** we usually use **the whole** rather than **all the**:

<SayIt text="They weren't able to stay for the whole concert." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(rather than <em>... for all the concert.</em>)</p>

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-emerald-700">But all does go with some time and 'made-of-parts' words</p>
  <p className="mt-2 text-sm text-emerald-900"><strong>all + day / week / night / month / winter</strong>, etc. (but not usually <em>all October / all 2001 / all 21st May</em>; <em>all Monday / Tuesday</em> etc. are informal), plus <strong>all the time</strong> and <strong>all the way</strong>.</p>
  <p className="mt-2 text-sm text-emerald-900">In informal speech we can also use <strong>all the</strong> with things seen as being made up of parts: <em>all the world / house / city / country / department</em>.</p>
</div>

<SayIt text="After the fire the whole city was covered in dust." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>... all the city ...</em> in informal speech.)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="entire = whole (right before a noun)">
  You can use <strong>entire</strong> instead of <strong>whole</strong> immediately before a noun: <em>The <strong>whole / entire</strong> building has recently been renovated.</em>
</Callout>

Before **plural nouns** both **all (of)** and **whole** are possible, but they mean **different** things. Compare:

<SayIt text="All (of) the towns had their electricity cut off." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= <strong>every</strong> town in an area)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="After the storm, whole towns were left without electricity." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= <strong>some</strong> towns were <strong>completely</strong> affected — and note we don't say <span className="text-rose-600">whole the towns</span>.)</p>

## D — Every and each

Often we can use **every** or **each** with little difference in meaning. However, four situations call specifically for **every**:

- with **almost, virtually, nearly** + noun, to emphasise we mean the group **as a whole**:
<SayIt text="Almost every visitor stopped and stared." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>Almost each visitor ...</em>)</p>

- with a **plural noun after a number**:
<SayIt text="I go to the dentist every six months." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(rather than <em>... each six months</em>)</p>

- with **abstract uncountable nouns** such as *chance, confidence, hope, reason, sympathy*, where **every** means 'complete' or 'total':
<SayIt text="She has every chance of success in her application for the job." />

- in **fixed phrases** of regular or repeated events: **every other (kilometre), every single (day), every so often, every few (months), every now and again** (= occasionally).

By contrast, **each** has two uses of its own:

- before a **noun** or **one**, to talk about **both** people or things in a **pair**:
<SayIt text="I only had two suitcases, but each one weighed over 20 kilos." />

- as a **pronoun** standing on its own:
<SayIt text="I asked many people and each gave the same answer." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>... each / every one gave ...</em>)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="each can float to mid-position — every can't">
  Like <em>all</em>, <strong>each</strong> can drift to a mid-sentence slot, tied back to the subject: <em>The ten winners will <strong>each</strong> receive £1,000.</em> You cannot move <strong>every</strong> in this way.
</Callout>
