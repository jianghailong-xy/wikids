When we don't want to repeat a noun we have just used, English lets us pick it up again with the substitute words **one** (singular) and **ones** (plural). They look simple, but they are fenced in by precise rules: they replace *countable* nouns only, they refuse to follow a noun used as an adjective, they often give way to *some*, *mine* or *yours*, and after certain words we can — or must, or must not — drop them altogether. This unit sorts out exactly when each move is allowed.

## A — *one* / *ones* for a repeated countable noun

We use **one** instead of repeating a **singular countable noun**, and **ones** instead of repeating a **plural countable noun**, when the context makes clear what we mean:

<SayIt text="Can I get you a drink? — It's okay, I've already got one." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>one</em> = a drink)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I think his best poems are his early ones." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>ones</em> = poems)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="Not for uncountable nouns">
  We <strong>don't</strong> use <em>one / ones</em> in place of an uncountable noun — we use <strong>some</strong> (or nothing at all):<br /><br />
  <em>If you need any more paper, I'll bring you <strong>some</strong>.</em> &nbsp;(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>I'll bring you a one / ones</em>)<br />
  <em>I asked him to get apple juice, but he got <strong>orange</strong>.</em> &nbsp;(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>he got orange one / ones</em>)
</Callout>

We also **can't use *ones*** on its own without something that defines *precisely which* group we mean. When there is no such defining word, we use **some** instead. Compare:

<SayIt text="We need new curtains. — Okay, let's buy green ones this time. / ... ones with flowers on / ... those ones." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>green</em>, <em>with flowers on</em>, <em>those</em> all define which ones — fine.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="We need new curtains. — Okay, let's buy some." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>Okay, let's buy ones.</em> — nothing defines them, so use <em>some</em>.)</p>

## B — Where *one* / *ones* is blocked

**After a noun used as an adjective** (a *noun adjunct*) we do **not** use *one / ones*. We simply repeat the head noun:

<SayIt text="I thought my memory stick was in my trouser pocket, but it was in my coat pocket." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>... in my coat one</em> — <em>coat</em> is a noun acting as an adjective.)</p>

**After a possessive determiner** (*my, your, her*, etc.) we prefer the **possessive pronoun** *mine, yours, hers*, etc. — though *possessive + one / ones* is heard in informal speech:

<SayIt text="I'd really like a smartphone like yours." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>... like your one</em> is informal only — prefer <em>yours</em>.)</p>

We usually use **ones** for **things**, not people:

<SayIt text="We need two people to help. We could ask those men over there." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>... ask those ones</em> — for people, repeat the noun.)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="...but ones for people in two cases">
  <strong>1. Comparative</strong> sentences referring to groups of people: <em>Older students seem to work harder than younger <strong>ones</strong>.</em> (or <em>... than younger students</em>)<br /><br />
  <strong>2. Fixed expressions</strong>: <em>the <strong>little ones</strong></em> (= small children), <em>(your) <strong>loved ones</strong></em> (= close family), <em>(one of) the <strong>lucky ones</strong></em>.
</Callout>

## C — When we can *leave out* *one* / *ones*

We can **omit** *one / ones* after the following:

- **after *which*:**
<SayIt text="When we buy medicines, we have no way of knowing which (ones) contain sugar." />

- **after superlatives:**
<SayIt text="Look at that pumpkin! It's the biggest (one) I've ever seen." />

- **after *this*, *that*, *these*, *those*:**
<SayIt text="The last test I did was quite easy, but some parts of this (one) are really difficult." />
<SayIt text="Help yourself to grapes. These (ones) are the sweetest, but those (ones) taste best." />

- **after *either*, *neither*, *another*, *each*, *the first / second / last*** (etc.):
<SayIt text="Karl pointed to the paintings and said I could take either (one)." />
<SayIt text="She cleared away the cups, washed each (one) thoroughly, and put them on the shelf." />

<Callout type="warning" title="A note on 'these ones / those ones'">
  Some people regard <em>these ones</em> and <em>those ones</em> as incorrect, particularly in <strong>formal English</strong>. Leaving <em>ones</em> out (<em>These are the sweetest</em>) sidesteps the objection.
</Callout>

## D — When we *keep* *one* / *ones* in

We do **not** leave out *one / ones* after these:

- **after *the*, *the only*, *the main*, and *every*:**
<SayIt text="When you cook clams you shouldn't eat the ones that have broken shells." />
<SayIt text="After I got the glasses home, I found that every one was broken." />

- **after adjectives:**
<SayIt text="My shoes were so uncomfortable that I had to go out today and buy some new ones." />

<Callout type="tip" title="The colour-adjective exception">
  After a <strong>colour adjective</strong> we can often drop <em>one / ones</em> when we are answering a question:<br /><br />
  <em>Have you decided which jumper to buy? — Yes, I think I'll take the <strong>blue (one)</strong>.</em>
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Take it further">
  Run a quick three-way test on any noun you're about to substitute. <strong>Is it countable?</strong> If not, use <em>some</em>, not <em>one/ones</em>. <strong>Is it a person?</strong> Then only use <em>ones</em> in a comparison or a fixed phrase (<em>the little ones</em>). <strong>What comes before it?</strong> A noun-adjective or possessive blocks <em>one/ones</em>; <em>which</em>, a superlative or <em>this/that</em> let you drop it; <em>the</em>, <em>every</em> or an ordinary adjective make you keep it.
</Callout>
