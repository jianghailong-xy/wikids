*Can*, *could*, *be able to* and *be allowed to* overlap, but they are not interchangeable. This unit sorts out which form does the work of **ability**, which does **possibility**, and which does **permission** — and, just as importantly, the places where *could* sounds right but is actually wrong: a single past achievement, and permission for one particular occasion. Get those two traps under control and the rest of the system falls into place.

## A — Can, could and be able to: ability

We sometimes use **be able to** instead of **can** and **could** to talk about ability. But we **avoid** *be able to* in three situations, preferring *can/could*:

- when we talk about something that is happening **as we speak**:

<SayIt text="Watch me, Mum; I can stand on one leg." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(not <em>I'm able to stand on one leg</em> — it's happening right now.)</p>

- when the meaning is **'know how to'**:

<SayIt text="Films can now easily be streamed online." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(rather than <em>Films are now easily able to be streamed …</em>)</p>

- **before passives**:

<SayIt text="Can you cook?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(rather than <em>Are you able to cook?</em>)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="When be able to earns its place">
  <strong>Be able to</strong> comes into its own where <em>can/could</em> have no form of their own — after another modal (<em>You <strong>might be able to</strong> help</em>), as a to-infinitive (<em>I'd love <strong>to be able to</strong> ski</em>), as an <em>-ing</em> form, and in the perfect (<em>She <strong>has been able to</strong> walk again since the operation</em>). Everywhere else, reach for <em>can/could</em> first.
</Callout>

## B — could or be able to for the past

If we talk about a **single achievement** — one particular occasion — rather than a **general ability** in the past, we usually use **be able to** rather than *could*. Compare:

<SayIt text="Sophie could play the flute quite well." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>… was able to …</em> — a <strong>general</strong> ability she had.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="She swam strongly and was able to cross the river easily, even though it was swollen by the heavy rain." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(not <em>She swam strongly and could cross …</em> — a <strong>specific</strong> achievement on one occasion.)</p>

However, **could** is usually more natural than *be able to* —

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-emerald-700">Where <em>could</em> wins, even for a single past event</p>
  <ul className="mt-2 space-y-2 text-sm text-emerald-900">
    <li>▪ in <strong>negative</strong> sentences: <em>I tried to get up but I <strong>couldn't</strong> move.</em></li>
    <li>▪ with verbs of the <strong>senses</strong> (<em>feel, hear, see, smell, taste</em>) and of <strong>'thinking'</strong> (<em>believe, decide, remember, understand</em>): <em>I <strong>could</strong> remember the crash, but nothing after that.</em></li>
    <li>▪ after <strong>the only thing / place / time</strong>, and after <strong>all</strong> when it means 'the only thing': <em>All we <strong>could</strong> see were his feet.</em></li>
    <li>▪ to suggest something <strong>almost didn't happen</strong> — with <em>almost, hardly, just, nearly</em>: <em>I <strong>could</strong> nearly touch the ceiling.</em></li>
  </ul>
</div>

<Callout type="warning" title="The classic slip">
  For a one-off past success in an affirmative sentence, <em>could</em> is wrong: ✅ <em>The firefighters <strong>were able to</strong> put out the flames in minutes.</em> — <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>could put out the flames</em>. The negative flips it back: ✅ <em>They <strong>couldn't</strong> save the roof.</em>
</Callout>

## C — Can and can't: possibility

To talk about the **theoretical** possibility of something happening we use **could**, not *can*. But we use **can**, not *could*, to say that something is possible and **actually happens** from time to time. Compare:

<SayIt text="It could be expensive to keep a cat." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= it's theoretically possible — it may or may not turn out expensive.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="It can be expensive to keep a cat." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= it's possible, and it sometimes really is.)</p>

We use **can't**, not *couldn't*, to say that something is theoretically or actually **impossible**:

<SayIt text="There can't be many people who haven't watched television." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The doctor can't see you this morning; he's busy at the hospital." />

<Callout type="info" title="can't ≠ couldn't for impossibility">
  For present impossibility, <em>can't</em> is the only choice: <em>You <strong>can't</strong> be serious.</em> Using <em>couldn't</em> here changes the meaning (it becomes tentative or past), so keep <em>can't</em> when you mean 'it is impossible'.
</Callout>

## D — could for future possibility

We use **could** to say there is a **real possibility** of a future event. Choosing *could* over *can* suggests the outcome is **less likely**, or that there is some **doubt** about it. Compare:

<SayIt text="We can stay with Jake in Oslo." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= we <strong>will be able to</strong> stay — it's settled.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="We could stay with Jake in Oslo." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= it's <strong>possible</strong> — perhaps, if he's there. One option among others.)</p>

## E — could and be allowed to: permission

To say that in the past someone had **general permission** to do something — that is, to do it **at any time** — we can use **either** *could* **or** *was / were allowed to*. But to talk about permission for **one particular past action**, we use *was / were allowed to*, **not** *could*. Compare:

<SayIt text="Anyone was allowed to fish in the lake when the council owned it." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>… could fish …</em> — <strong>general</strong> permission, any time.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Although he didn't have a ticket, Tom was allowed to come in." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(not <em>… could come in</em> — permission on <strong>one particular</strong> occasion.)</p>

In **negative** sentences we can use **either** *couldn't* **or** *wasn't / weren't allowed to*, whether the permission was general or particular:

<SayIt text="I couldn't / wasn't allowed to open the present until my birthday." />

Deliberately force in <strong>one</strong> of each: a general ability (<em>could</em>), a single success (<em>was/were able to</em>), an impossibility (<em>can't/couldn't</em>), and a permission (<em>was/were allowed to</em>). The moment you can switch between them without stopping to think, this unit has done its job.
</Callout>
