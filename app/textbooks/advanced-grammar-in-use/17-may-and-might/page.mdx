At this level you already know that **may** and **might** express possibility. This unit is about the distinctions that matter at C1 — when to prefer one over the other, the one construction where *may* is blocked, the formal/literary use of *might* for past habits, the compensation pattern, and the full tense-aspect spread of *may / might + have / be + -ing*.

## A — May and might: possibility

**May** and **might** often have a similar meaning when we talk about possibility. However, we prefer **may** in academic or formal language to talk about **characteristics or behaviour**:

<SayIt text="The seeds from the plant may grow up to 20 centimetres in length." />

And in speech we prefer **might** to say what we will **possibly do in the future**:

<SayIt text="I might paint the kitchen purple." />

### Questions about possibility

We **don't** use *may* to ask questions about the possibility of something happening. Instead we use, for example, **could(n't)** or the phrase **be likely**:

<SayIt text="Could it be that you don't want to leave?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Not <em>May it be that you ...?</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Are you likely to be in Spain again this summer?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Not <em>May you be in Spain ...?</em>)</p>

It is possible to use **might** in this type of question, but it is rather **formal**:

<SayIt text="Might they be persuaded to change their minds?" />

<Callout type="info" title="May for permission and offers">
  Note that we <strong>can</strong> use <em>may</em> in <strong>formally asking for permission</strong> and <strong>offering help</strong>:<br />
  <em>May I leave now?</em><br />
  <em>May I help you?</em>
</Callout>

## B — Might for typical past behaviour

**Might** (not 'may') + bare infinitive is sometimes used to talk about what was **typically the case in the past**. This is a formal or literary use:

<SayIt text="During the war, the police might arrest you for criticising the government." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Years ago children might be sent down mines at the age of six." />

We can also use **could** + bare infinitive in examples like this to talk about past ability (see Unit 15). For example, *During the war, the police could arrest you ...* means that the police were legally able to arrest you.

<Callout type="warning" title="May cannot do this">
  Only <strong>might</strong> (and <em>could</em>) can express typical past behaviour. <em>May</em> is not used this way: <em>Years ago children may be sent down mines ...</em> is ❌.
</Callout>

## C — May / might … but … (the compensation pattern)

When we say that a person or thing **compensates** to some extent for a limitation or weakness by having another characteristic, we can use a pattern with **may / might not + bare infinitive ... but ...** or **may / might not have + past participle ... but ...**:

<SayIt text="The painting may not be a masterpiece, but the colours are remarkable." />
<br />
<SayIt text="She might not have danced very gracefully, but she had a lot of energy and enthusiasm." />

<Callout type="tip" title="The concession test">
  If you can paraphrase the sentence as <em>"Admittedly X isn't true, yet Y makes up for it"</em>, this is the pattern you want. The <em>may/might not</em> half concedes a weakness; the <em>but</em> half points to a redeeming strength.
</Callout>

## D — May / might + have / be + -ing across time

We use **may / might** (not 'can') **+ have + past participle** and **may / might** (not 'can') **+ be + -ing** to talk about possible events in the **past, present and future**:

<SayIt text="Do you think Laura may / might have completed the report by now?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(past — a completed action before now.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="His maths may / might have improved by the time the exam comes round." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(future — a completed action before a future point.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Marco isn't in his office. He may / might be working at home today." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(present — an action in progress around now.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="When I go to Vienna I may / might be staying with Max, but I'm not sure yet." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(future — a possible ongoing arrangement.)</p>

Note that **could** can be used in these sentences instead of *may* or *might*:

<SayIt text="Do you think Laura could have completed the report by now?" />

We can also use **may / might have been + -ing** to talk about possible situations or activities that went on **over a period of past time**:

<SayIt text="Callum didn't know where the ball was, but he thought his sister might have been playing with it before she left for school." />

  <em>May</em> and <em>might</em> — not <em>can</em> — are used in these perfect and progressive constructions for possibility. You can say <em>He may have left</em> and <em>He might have left</em> but <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>He can have left</em>. (But <em>could have left</em> is fine.)
</Callout>
