Most adverbs tell you something about the verb — *how*, *when* or *where* an action happens. This unit is about two families that do the opposite: instead of describing the action, they let the speaker **step outside the sentence**. A **comment adverb** comments on the whole of what is being said (*frankly, apparently, surprisingly*); a **viewpoint adverb** limits a statement to one angle or one person's point of view (*financially, technically, in my view*). The skill is partly choosing the right word and partly putting it in the right place — because the same adverb can mean two different things depending on where it sits.

## A — Comment adverbs: standing outside the sentence

A **comment adverb** doesn't describe *how* an action is done — it lets us comment on the whole of what we are saying. The three jobs they do:

| The comment it makes | Typical adverbs |
|---|---|
| **how likely** we think something is | apparently · certainly · clearly · definitely · obviously · presumably · probably · undoubtedly |
| our **attitude to / opinion of** what is said | astonishingly · frankly · generally · honestly · interestingly · luckily · naturally · sadly · seriously · surprisingly · unbelievably |
| our **judgement of someone's actions** | bravely · carelessly · foolishly · generously · kindly · rightly · stupidly · wisely · wrongly |

Comment adverbs usually apply to the **whole sentence** and are most often used in **front position** (see Unit 75A), although they can also come at the **end** or in other positions. In front or end position we separate them from the rest of the sentence by a **comma** in writing, or by **intonation** in speech:

<SayIt text="Presumably, he didn't hear me when I called." />

<SayIt text="The book was based on his experience in China, apparently." />

<SayIt text="If you practise continuously, you will undoubtedly get better." />

<Callout type="info" title="Judgement adverbs follow the subject">
  When a comment adverb passes <em>judgement on an action</em> — <strong>kindly, foolishly, generously, rightly</strong> and the like — it normally comes <strong>after the subject</strong>, though it can be moved to the front for emphasis:<br /><br />
  <em>He <strong>kindly</strong> offered to give me a lift.</em> &nbsp;→&nbsp; <em><strong>Kindly</strong>, he offered…</em> (front position emphasises <em>kindly</em>).
</Callout>

The position matters because the **same adverb** can comment on the whole sentence in one place and modify just one word in another. Compare:

<SayIt text="Astonishingly, she did well in the exam." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Front position, comma — a comment: <em>I was surprised that she did well</em>.)</p>

<SayIt text="She did astonishingly well in the exam." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Mid position, no comma — a degree adverb on <em>well</em>: <em>she did extremely well</em>.)</p>

<SayIt text="You've had a major operation. Obviously, it will be very painful for a while." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(A comment: <em>I expect you already know this</em>.)</p>

<SayIt text="When he stood up it was obviously very painful." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Modifying <em>very painful</em>: <em>the pain was clear to see</em>.)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="Move it, and the meaning moves with it">
  If a comment adverb applies to only <strong>part</strong> of the sentence, take it out of front position. In front position with a comma it comments on everything; tucked in beside an adjective or adverb it scales just that one word. <em>Astonishingly, she sang well</em> ≠ <em>She sang astonishingly well</em>.
</Callout>

## B — Viewpoint adverbs: from which angle?

A **viewpoint adverb** makes clear what *viewpoint* we are speaking from — it identifies **which feature** of something is being talked about:

<SayIt text="Financially, the accident has been a disaster for the owners of the tunnel." />

<SayIt text="The brothers may be alike physically, but they have very different personalities." />

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-sky-200 bg-sky-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-sky-700">Common viewpoint adverbs</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-sky-900">biologically · environmentally · financially · ideologically · industrially · logically · medically · morally · outwardly · politically · technically · visually</p>
</div>

A number of **phrases** are used in the same way, picking out the field we mean:

<SayIt text="Politically, this summer is a crucial time for the government." />

<SayIt text="In political terms, this summer is a crucial time for the government." />

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-violet-200 bg-violet-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-violet-700">Also in this pattern</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-violet-900">politically speaking · in terms of politics · from a political point of view · as far as politics is / are concerned</p>
</div>

A third group of adverbs and phrases says **whose** viewpoint we are expressing:

<SayIt text="The head of National Bank is to receive, according to reports, a £1 million bonus." />

<SayIt text="In my view, the foreign minister should resign immediately." />

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-emerald-700">Whose viewpoint?</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-emerald-900">to my / his / her (etc.) knowledge · from my / his / her (etc.) perspective · personally · in my / his / her (etc.) opinion</p>
</div>

<Callout type="tip" title="Comment vs viewpoint — the quick test">
  Ask what the adverb is doing. If it tells you <strong>how the speaker feels about, judges, or rates the likelihood of</strong> the whole statement, it's a <em>comment</em> adverb (<em>frankly, sadly, presumably</em>). If it tells you <strong>which field or whose angle</strong> the statement is true within, it's a <em>viewpoint</em> adverb (<em>financially, technically, in my view</em>).
</Callout>
