You already know the basic set — *myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves*. This unit is about the choices around them: when a reflexive is the only correct option, when it is optional, when it adds *emphasis*, and the cases where English actually wants an ordinary personal pronoun (*me, him, them*) instead. Getting these right is a reliable marker of advanced, idiomatic English.

## A — *themselves* and *oneself* for people in general

In addition to the usual reflexive pronouns, people use **themselves** to refer to the person who is the subject of the sentence when they want to **avoid saying whether the subject is male or female**:

<SayIt text="The author of the letter describes themselves as 'a senior government official'." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Who wants to go through life by themselves, without friends?" />

**Oneself** (or, less formally, **yourself**) is used to refer to **people in general**:

<SayIt text="I think one has to have the courage to be oneself and say whatever comes naturally." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Less formally: <em>I think you have to have the courage to be yourself …</em>)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="Singular they, fully grammatical">
  Using <strong>themselves</strong> for a single person of unknown or unspecified gender is standard, accepted English — it lets you avoid the clumsy <em>himself or herself</em>. Note the spelling: <strong>themselves</strong>, never <span className="text-rose-600">theirselves</span> or <span className="text-rose-600">themself</span> when the reference is plural.
</Callout>

## B — Reflexive pronouns for emphasis

We can use reflexive pronouns for emphasis in various ways. One common use is to emphasise that **the subject caused a certain action**. Compare:

<SayIt text="Salma worked hard and got promoted." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Neutral — we don't know who or what brought it about.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Salma worked hard and got herself promoted." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Emphasises that it happened <em>through her own efforts</em>.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Salma encouraged me to apply for the senior manager position, but she got promoted herself." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Emphasises that it was <em>Salma</em> who got promoted, not me.)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="Where you put it changes the emphasis">
  Mid-sentence, next to the verb (<em>got <strong>herself</strong> promoted</em>) stresses that she <em>brought it about</em>. At the end of the clause (<em>she got promoted <strong>herself</strong></em>) it stresses <em>who</em> — Salma, as opposed to someone else.
</Callout>

## C — A reflexive pronoun as the object of a transitive verb

If the object of a transitive verb refers to the **same person or thing as the subject**, then that object **must be a reflexive pronoun**. Compare:

<SayIt text="He walked around the golf course to familiarise himself with it." />
<br />
<SayIt text="We walked around to familiarise the children with their new surroundings." />

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-amber-200 bg-amber-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-amber-700">Verbs that <em>require</em> the reflexive (+ a preposition)</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-amber-900">absent … from · avail … of · busy … with · concern … with · occupy … by / with · pride … on · tear … away from · trouble … about / with</p>
  <p className="mt-2 text-sm text-amber-800">e.g. <em>She <strong>prides herself on</strong> her punctuality</em>; <em>He <strong>absented himself from</strong> the meeting</em>.</p>
</div>

With **some** verbs we can use a reflexive pronoun **or leave it out**, with little difference in meaning:

<SayIt text="We are confident that both sets of fans will behave (themselves) at the match." />

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-emerald-700">Verbs where the reflexive is <em>optional</em></p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-emerald-900">acclimatise · adapt · (un)dress · hide · move · prepare · shave · wash</p>
  <p className="mt-2 text-sm text-emerald-800">e.g. <em>I quickly <strong>washed (myself)</strong> and got dressed</em>; <em>The troops <strong>prepared (themselves)</strong> for the attack</em>.</p>
</div>

We **do** include the reflexive pronoun if we want to emphasise that the person or thing referred to in the subject is **affected by the action**:

<SayIt text="Although she helped other athletes prepare for competing at high altitudes, she found it difficult to acclimatise herself." />

## D — Reflexive pronouns after prepositions

When the subject and the **object of a preposition** refer to the same person or thing, we use a **reflexive** pronoun after the preposition:

<SayIt text="He was pleased with himself." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> … pleased with him.)</p>

If the verb already has a **direct object**, we use a **personal** pronoun, not a reflexive pronoun:

<SayIt text="I remember closing the door behind me." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> … closing the door behind myself.)</p>

However, if we need to make it **clear** that the subject and the prepositional phrase refer to the same person or thing, we use a reflexive pronoun after the preposition:

<SayIt text="She bought the bracelet for herself." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>… bought the bracelet for <strong>her</strong></em> would suggest it was bought for someone else.)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="The trap: behind me vs behind myself">
  When the verb has its own object, the reflexive feels wrong: <em>I closed the door behind <strong>me</strong></em>, <em>She pulled the blanket up around <strong>her</strong></em>, <em>He had a few coins on <strong>him</strong></em>. Switch to the reflexive only to remove genuine ambiguity about who benefits — <em>for <strong>herself</strong></em>, not someone else.
</Callout>

## E — *myself* after *and* / *or*

**Myself** is sometimes used after **and** and **or** rather than *I* or *me*, although some people consider this use incorrect and avoid it:

<SayIt text="I believe that Lisa and myself have done a pretty good job." />
<br />
<SayIt text="When you've finished the job, can you send the bill either to Mrs Petrov or myself?" />

Using *myself* here **reduces focus on the speaker or writer**, so it sounds less forceful or more polite.

<Callout type="info" title="In careful writing">
  Test it by removing the other person: you'd say <em>… that <strong>I</strong> have done a good job</em> and <em>send the bill to <strong>me</strong></em> — so the strictly correct forms are <em>Lisa and I</em> (subject) and <em>to Mrs Petrov or me</em> (object). The <em>myself</em> version is widely heard, but a careful writer keeps it for its softening effect, not as a default.
</Callout>

  </div>
