When we talk about situations that are **unreal** — imagined, hypothetical, contrary to fact — English keeps one quiet relic of the old *subjunctive*: the form **were** used after *every* subject, including *I*, *he*, *she* and *it*. You already meet it in the fixed phrase *If I were you*. This unit widens the lens: where **were** replaces **was**, where it lets you drop *if* altogether by inverting (*Were the election to be held ...*), and the cluster of words — *wish, if only, would rather, suppose, as if, even if* — that pull the same form along behind them.

## A — *were* instead of *was* in unreal conditions

In **unreal conditional** sentences we can use **were** after any subject in the **if-clause**, including singular first- and third-person subjects (*I / he / she / it*). This is sometimes called the **past subjunctive**, and is generally preferred only in **formal** contexts. Note that although the verb has a *past* form, the reference is to the **imagined present or future**:

<SayIt text="If your mother were here, I'm sure she wouldn't let you eat all those chocolates." />
<br />
<SayIt text="My job would not exist if it were not for government funding." />

**Was** can be used instead of **were** with the same meaning (*If your mother was here ...*, etc.). However, we strongly prefer **were** rather than **was** when we give **advice** with **If I were you ...**:

<SayIt text="If I were you, I'd take it back to the shop. It's got a hole in it." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>rather than</em> If I was you ...)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="Past form, present meaning">
  The <em>-ed</em>/<em>past</em> shape is a grammatical signal of <strong>distance from reality</strong>, not of past time. <em>If it were not for ...</em> and <em>If I were you ...</em> both describe the imagined <strong>now</strong>. The same logic runs through the whole unit: every <strong>were</strong> below points at something unreal, not something over.
</Callout>

## B — *were* in other patterns for imaginary situations

**Were** turns up in the same way in several other patterns whenever we talk about **imaginary situations**.

### (i) *Were + subject* — the inverted, formal conditional

We can use **were + subject + to-infinitive**, or **were + subject**, as a more **formal** alternative to **if + subject + was / were** (see also Unit 84A). The *if* disappears and **were** moves to the front:

<SayIt text="Were the election to be held today, the Liberals would win easily." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>or</em> If the election was / were held today ...)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Were I not in my seventies and rather unfit, I might consider taking up squash." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>or</em> If I weren't in my seventies and rather unfit ...)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="The inversion needs *were* — and often *to*">
  This fronted pattern works with <strong>were</strong> only: <em>Was the election to be held ...</em> is wrong. To make it negative, put <strong>not</strong> after the subject — <em>Were I not ...</em>, not <em>Weren't I ...</em>. And when an action follows, you usually need the <strong>to-infinitive</strong>: <em>Were anyone <span className="underline">to</span> lean against the window ...</em>.
</Callout>

### (ii) After *wish*

<SayIt text="I enjoy my job enormously, but I wish it were closer to home." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>or</em> ... I wish it was ...)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I'm pleased that Jan has been given the award. I only wish he weren't so boastful about it." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>or</em> ... I only wish he wasn't ...)</p>

### (iii) After *if only*, expressing regret that things aren't different

<SayIt text="'If your job is so bad, why don't you leave?' 'If only it were that simple.'" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>or</em> If only it was ...)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I'd really like to do accounting, if only I weren't so poor at maths." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>or</em> If only I wasn't ...)</p>

### (iv) After *would ('d) rather* and *would ('d) sooner*, for preferences

<SayIt text="I feel embarrassed about what happened and would rather the event were forgotten." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>or</em> ... was forgotten.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="'I've arranged a meeting for the end of July.' 'I'd sooner it were earlier, if possible.'" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>or</em> ... it was earlier.)</p>

### (v) After *suppose*, *supposing* and *imagine*

In sentences or clauses that open with **suppose**, **supposing** or **imagine**, use **were** (often **were to + infinitive**) for the imagined event:

<SayIt text="Suppose I were to lower the price by £100. Would you consider buying the car then?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>or</em> Suppose I was to lower ...)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I know it looks rather dirty now, but imagine the house were repainted. It would look a lot more attractive." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>or</em> ... imagine the house was (to be) repainted.)</p>

### (vi) In comparisons — after *as if*, *as though* and *even if*

<SayIt text="I remember stepping off the boat in New York as if it were yesterday." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Despite losing the election, she continues to act as though she were prime minister." />
<br />
<SayIt text="It's too late to start the work this year even if it were possible to find the money for it." />

<Callout type="tip" title="*as if* vs *even if*">
  Both take <strong>were</strong>, but they mean opposite things. <strong>as if / as though</strong> draws a <em>comparison</em> — it looks like this (but isn't). <strong>even if</strong> makes a <em>concession</em> — this wouldn't change the outcome. <em>She acts <strong>as if</strong> she were in charge</em> (= she only seems to be) ≠ <em>I wouldn't take it <strong>even if</strong> they were to offer it</em> (= the offer wouldn't sway me).
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Take it further">
  The book lets <em>was</em> stand in for <em>were</em> in most of these (informally), but two places resist it: the <strong>fronted inversion</strong> (<em>Were the election ...</em>, never <em>Was the election ...</em>) and the <strong>advice idiom</strong> (<em>If I were you</em>). Drill those two until they're automatic, and let the rest stay as a register choice — <em>were</em> when you want to sound careful, <em>was</em> when you're relaxed.
</Callout>
