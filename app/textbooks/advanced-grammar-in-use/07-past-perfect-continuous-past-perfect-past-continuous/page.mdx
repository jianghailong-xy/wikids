## A — Past perfect continuous vs past perfect

We use the **past perfect continuous** (*had been + -ing*) to talk about something that was **in progress recently, before or up to a past point in time**. We use the **past perfect** (*had + past participle*) when we talk about a **finished action** before a past time:

<SayIt text="I'd been finishing some work in the garden when Lara arrived, so I didn't hear her come in." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(In progress at the moment she arrived — <em>not</em> I'd finished some work … so I didn't hear her come in.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I'd finished all the ironing, so I started cleaning the windows." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(The ironing was complete <em>before</em> the next action — <em>not</em> I'd been finishing all the ironing, so I started …)</p>

<div className="not-prose my-6 grid grid-cols-1 gap-3 sm:grid-cols-2">
  <div className="rounded-2xl border-2 border-violet-200 bg-violet-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-violet-700">had been finishing</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm text-slate-700">an activity <strong>in progress</strong> up to the past point</p>
    <p className="mt-2 text-xs text-violet-500">·····→ | past · · · now</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-2xl border-2 border-sky-200 bg-sky-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-sky-700">had finished</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm text-slate-700">an action <strong>completed</strong> before the past point</p>
    <p className="mt-2 text-xs text-sky-500">──● | past · · · now</p>
  </div>
</div>

We can often use **either** the past perfect continuous **or** the past perfect with a similar meaning:

<SayIt text="I'd been working / I'd worked hard all year, so I felt that I deserved a holiday." />

## B — Counting "how many times" → past perfect, not the continuous

If we talk about **how many times** something happened in a period up to a particular past time, we use the **past perfect**, *not* the past perfect continuous:

<SayIt text="How many times had you met him before yesterday?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Not <em>How many times had you been meeting him …</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I had stayed in the hotel twice in the 1990s." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Not <em>I had been staying in the hotel twice …</em>)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="Quantity vs activity">
  The continuous tells you an activity was <em>going on</em>; it can't count finished events. The moment you say <em>twice</em>, <em>three times</em>, or <em>how many times</em>, switch to the plain past perfect.
</Callout>

## C — Three ways the past perfect continuous relates to a past point

The past perfect continuous can describe a situation or activity that went on before a particular past time and either **(i)** finished at that time, **(ii)** continued beyond it, or **(iii)** finished shortly before it:

<SayIt text="We'd been driving for about an hour when the engine suddenly stopped." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(i) finished <em>at</em> that point.</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="She felt terrible during the interview because she had been suffering from flu since the previous day." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(ii) still <em>continuing</em> beyond that point.</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The last time I saw Omar, he'd been running and was out of breath." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(iii) finished <em>shortly before</em> that point.</p>

If we are **not interested in how long** the activity went on, we can use the **past continuous** instead of the past perfect continuous. Compare:

<SayIt text="When the merger was announced, it became apparent that the two companies had been discussing the possibility since last year." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(We care about the length — <em>since last year</em> — so past perfect continuous.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="A friend told me about a conversation she'd overheard. Two women were discussing their holiday plans." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(No interest in duration — the simpler past continuous is enough.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I first met Mateo and Lucia when they had been going out together for five years." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Karin met Lars when she was going out with his best friend." />

## D — States: use the past perfect, even for "how long"

Remember that we **don't describe states with continuous tenses** (see Unit 1). We use the **past perfect**, not the past perfect continuous, even when we focus on the **length** of a situation up to a particular past time:

<SayIt text="We had only owned the car for six weeks when the clutch broke." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Not <em>We had been owning the car for six weeks …</em> — <em>own</em> is a state.)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="State verbs stay simple">
  <em>own, belong, know, believe, cost, contain, seem, understand …</em> resist the <em>-ing</em> form. With these, "how long" is carried by the past perfect alone: <em>had known</em>, <em>had owned</em>, <em>had cost</em> — never <em>had been knowing / owning / costing</em>.
</Callout>

## E — Register: mainly written, less common in speech

The past perfect continuous is used **mainly in written texts** and is **less common in speech**. Here it is doing typical work in a newspaper report:

<SayIt text="The body of a climber who went missing in the Alps was finally found yesterday. Carl Sims had been climbing alone near the Harz Waterfall, which has claimed many lives in the past." />
