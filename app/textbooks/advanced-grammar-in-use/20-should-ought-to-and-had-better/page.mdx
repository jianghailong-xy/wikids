## A — should / ought to: recommendation, obligation and probability

We can very often use **should** or **ought to** interchangeably — both for **obligations and recommendations** and for **probability** — although in general **should** is the more frequent of the two. **Ought to** belongs more to speech, and is used most often to talk about **obligation** rather than probability.

<SayIt text="You should finish your homework before you go out." />
<br />
<SayIt text="You ought to finish your homework before you go out." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(obligation — the two are interchangeable here)</p>

<SayIt text="It should be ready by now." />
<br />
<SayIt text="It ought to be ready by now." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(probability — but here <em>should</em> is the more natural choice)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="Certainty needs must, not should / ought to">
  When we conclude — <em>on the basis of evidence we have</em> — that something is certain or very likely, we use <strong>must</strong> (see Unit 18), <strong>not</strong> should / ought to: <br />
  ✅ It's the third time she's been skating this week. She <strong>must</strong> really enjoy it. <br />
  ❌ … She <em>should</em> really enjoy it.
</Callout>

Beyond the general overlap, there are three places where we clearly **prefer `should`**:

**1. Reporting what an outside authority recommends:**

<SayIt text="The manual says that the computer should be disconnected from the power supply before the cover is removed." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(rather than <em>… ought to be disconnected …</em>)</p>

**2. Giving advice with *I* — use `should` (or `would`), not `ought to`:**

<SayIt text="I should leave early tomorrow, if I were you." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>I would leave …</em>; or <em>I'd leave …</em> — but not <em>I ought to leave …</em>)</p>

**3. In questions, and particularly *wh*-questions:**

<SayIt text="What should I do if I have any problems?" />
<br />
<SayIt text="Should I ring you at home?" />

## B — should / ought to + have + past participle

We use **should / ought to + have + past participle** in two distinct ways.

**1. Something that *didn't* happen in the past — and we're sorry it didn't** (regret or criticism):

<SayIt text="We should have waited for the rain to stop." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(I'm sorry we didn't.)</p>

The negatives **shouldn't have** / **oughtn't to have** are almost always used this way — to voice regret or criticism:

<SayIt text="You shouldn't have told her — it was supposed to be a secret." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(criticism of something that <em>did</em> happen)</p>

**2. An expectation that something happened, has happened, or will happen:**

<SayIt text="If the flight was on time, he should have arrived in Jakarta early this morning." />

<Callout type="info" title="Two meanings, one form">
  <strong>Regret / criticism:</strong> <em>We should have booked earlier</em> — but we didn't, and it went wrong. <br />
  <strong>Expectation:</strong> <em>The parcel should have arrived by now</em> — I fully expect that it has. <br />
  Context (and often a <em>but …</em> or an <em>if …</em> clause) tells you which one is meant.
</Callout>

## C — should and shall in questions; shall vs should

We can use **should** in questions that are **offers** or that **request confirmation or advice**:

<SayIt text="Should I phone for a taxi for you?" />
<br />
<SayIt text="Who should I pass the message to?" />

In questions like these we can also use **shall** with a very similar meaning:

<SayIt text="Shall I phone for a taxi for you?" />

But compare **shall** and **should** in statements, where *I shall* means *I intend to* and *I should* means *I ought to*:

<SayIt text="I shall read the script on the train tomorrow." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= I intend to; or <em>I'll read …</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I should read the script on the train tomorrow, but I know that I'll be too tired." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= I ought to — an obligation I probably won't meet)</p>

## D — had better

We can use **had better** instead of **should / ought to**, especially in **spoken English**, to say that we think it is a **good idea** to do something now or in the near future:

<SayIt text="If you're not well, you'd better ask Clare to go instead." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>… you should / ought to …</em>)</p>

But we **don't** use *had better* to talk about the **past** or to make **general comments** — here only *should / ought to* will do:

<Callout type="warning" title="Where had better cannot go">
  ✅ You <strong>should / ought to</strong> have caught an earlier train. &nbsp; ❌ You <em>had better have caught</em> an earlier train. <span className="text-slate-400">(the past)</span> <br />
  ✅ I don't think parents <strong>should / ought to</strong> give children sweets. &nbsp; ❌ I don't think parents <em>had better give</em> children sweets. <span className="text-slate-400">(a general comment)</span>
</Callout>

We **prefer had better** when we want to express **particular urgency**, or in **demands and threats**:

<SayIt text="There's someone moving about downstairs. We'd better call the police, quickly." />

The negative form is **had better not**, and in questions the subject comes **after had**:

<SayIt text="He'd better not be late again, or he'll be in trouble." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Had we better get a taxi?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>Should we get …?</em>)</p>
