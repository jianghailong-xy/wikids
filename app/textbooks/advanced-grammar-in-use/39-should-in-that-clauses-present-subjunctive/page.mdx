## A — Reporting advice, orders and suggestions with *should*

We can report advice, orders, requests, suggestions, etc. about things that **need to be done or are desirable** using a **that-clause** with **should + bare infinitive**:

<SayIt text="They have proposed that Felix should move to their Munich office." />
<br />
<SayIt text="We advised that the company should not raise its prices." />

After **should** we very often use **be + past participle** (a passive) or **be + adjective**:

<SayIt text="They directed that the building should be pulled down." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(passive: <em>should + be + pulled down</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="We insist that the showroom should be available to all students in financial difficulties." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>should + be + adjective</em>)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="Active or passive? Let the meaning decide">
  If the subject of the that-clause <strong>does</strong> the action, use <em>should + bare infinitive</em> (<em>that the company <strong>should not raise</strong> its prices</em>). If it <strong>receives</strong> the action, use the passive <em>should + be + past participle</em> (<em>that the building <strong>should be pulled down</strong></em>). Intransitive verbs have no passive: <em>that the office <strong>should remain</strong> in London</em>, never <em>should be remained</em>.
</Callout>

## B — Leaving *should* out: the present subjunctive

In **formal contexts**, particularly in written English, we can often leave out **should** and use the **base form** of the verb — the form you would look up in a dictionary. This form is the **present subjunctive**, and it is used to describe *bringing about* the situation expressed in the that-clause. Notice that the verb does **not** change for a third-person subject:

<SayIt text="They have proposed that Felix move to their Munich office." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(not <em>Felix moves</em> — the subjunctive has no <em>-s</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="They directed that the building be pulled down." />

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-2xl border-2 border-violet-200 bg-violet-50 p-5">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-violet-700">Verbs commonly followed by the present subjunctive</p>
  <p className="mt-2 text-sm font-semibold text-violet-900">advise · ask · beg · command · demand · insist · instruct · intend · order · prefer · recommend · request · require · stipulate · suggest · urge · warn</p>
</div>

To make a **negative**, we put **not** (*not* "do not") directly before the verb:

<SayIt text="We advised that the company not raise its prices." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(not <em>does not raise</em> and not <em>not to raise</em>)</p>

In **less formal** contexts we can use ordinary forms of the verb instead of the subjunctive. Compare the three registers:

<div className="not-prose my-6 space-y-2 text-sm">
  <div className="rounded-xl border border-slate-200 bg-white p-3 shadow-sm">I suggested that he <strong>should give up</strong> golf. <span className="text-slate-400">— neutral · negative: <em>shouldn't give up</em></span></div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border border-slate-200 bg-white p-3 shadow-sm">I suggested that he <strong>give up</strong> golf. <span className="text-slate-400">— more formal (subjunctive) · negative: <em>not give up</em></span></div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border border-slate-200 bg-white p-3 shadow-sm">I suggested that he <strong>gave up</strong> golf. <span className="text-slate-400">— less formal (ordinary tense) · negative: <em>didn't give up</em></span></div>
</div>

<Callout type="info" title="A British / American difference worth knowing">
  In American English the bare subjunctive is the default in this pattern — <em>The board insisted that he <strong>resign</strong></em>. British English is happy with the subjunctive in formal writing but reaches more readily for <em>should</em> in everyday use — <em>The board insisted that he <strong>should resign</strong></em>. Both are correct; the subjunctive simply sounds a touch more formal in BrE.
</Callout>

## C — *that-*clauses after related nouns

We can also use that-clauses with **should** or the subjunctive after a **reporting clause built on a noun** related to the verbs in section B — *advice, order, proposal, recommendation, requirement, suggestion, warning*, etc.:

<SayIt text="The police issued an order that all weapons (should) be handed in immediately." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The weather forecast gave a warning that people (should) prepare for heavy snow." />

<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">The <em>(should)</em> in brackets shows that you may keep it or drop it (subjunctive) — both are correct here.</p>

## D — *should* / subjunctive after *it + be + adjective*

We can use **should** — or sometimes the subjunctive — in a that-clause after **it + be + adjective**, where the adjective says that the action is necessary, desirable or fitting:

<SayIt text="It is inappropriate that he should receive the award again." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or, less formally, <em>... that he receives the award again</em>)</p>

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-2xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50 p-5">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-emerald-700">Adjectives of necessity / desirability (it is ___ that ...)</p>
  <p className="mt-2 text-sm font-semibold text-emerald-900">advisable · appalling · appropriate · (in)conceivable · crucial · essential · imperative · important · obligatory · (un)necessary · urgent · vital</p>
</div>

## E — *should* for your own reaction

We can use **should** in a that-clause when we talk about **our own reaction** to what we are reporting, especially after **be + adjective**. Here *should* does not mean "ought to" — it colours the fact with the speaker's feeling about it:

<SayIt text="I am concerned that she should think I stole the money." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or, less formally, <em>... that she thinks I stole the money</em>)</p>

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-2xl border-2 border-rose-200 bg-rose-50 p-5">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-rose-700">Reaction adjectives (I am ___ that ...)</p>
  <p className="mt-2 text-sm font-semibold text-rose-900">amazed · amused · anxious · astounded · disappointed · shocked · surprised · upset</p>
</div>

<Callout type="warning" title="Two different *shoulds* — don't blur them">
  The <em>should</em> of sections A–D is <strong>mandative</strong>: it reports what someone wants to happen (<em>insist that he should resign</em>) — drop it and you get the subjunctive. The <em>should</em> of section E is <strong>emotive</strong>: it adds your reaction to a fact (<em>surprised that he should say that</em>). When you leave this emotive <em>should</em> out, you use an <strong>ordinary tense</strong>, never a subjunctive — <em>I'm surprised that he says that</em>, not <em>that he say that</em>.
</Callout>

## F — *should* for situations now, in the future, or planned

We can use **should** in a that-clause about a situation that **exists now**:

<SayIt text="It's not surprising that they should be seen together — they're brothers." />

… or one that **may exist in the future**:

<SayIt text="We believe it is important that she should take the exam next year." />

If we are talking about an **intention or plan**, we can often use a **subjunctive** rather than *should*:

<SayIt text="I've arranged that she come to the first part of the meeting." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>... that she should come</em> / <em>... that she comes</em> / <em>... for her to come</em>)</p>
