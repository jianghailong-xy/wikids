When we make a general statement, we often need to carve out something that does **not** fit — an exception. English gives us a family of words for this: *except (for), besides, apart from, but, but for*, and the post-position *excepted / apart / aside*. This unit sorts out which one to reach for, and when they overlap.

## A — except (for) and except

We use **except (for)** to introduce the only thing(s) or person / people that a statement does **not** include:

<SayIt text="The price of the holiday includes all meals except (for) lunch." />
<SayIt text="Everyone seemed to have been invited except (for) Mrs Woodford and me." />
<SayIt text="I had no money to give him except (for) the few coins in my pocket." />

We use **except**, **not** *except for*, with **to-infinitives** and **that-clauses**:

<SayIt text="I rarely need to go into the city centre except to do some shopping." />
<SayIt text="They look just like the real thing, except that they're made of plastic." />

We usually use **except** before **prepositions**, **bare infinitives**, and **that-clauses** (including those where *that* is left out — see Unit 53). However, informally *except for* is sometimes also used, although this is grammatically incorrect:

<SayIt text="There is likely to be rain everywhere today except in Wales." />
<SayIt text="There is nothing more the doctor can do except keep an eye on him." />
<SayIt text="They look just like the real thing, except (that) they're made of plastic." />

<Callout type="warning" title="except for = 'but for'">
  We can use <strong>except for</strong>, but <strong>not</strong> <em>except</em>, with the meaning <em>'but for'</em> — see section C below. This is a distinct usage where <em>except for</em> introduces something without which the situation would have been different.
</Callout>

## B — except (for) vs besides vs apart from

We use **except (for)** to mean that something is **not included** in a statement, but we use **besides** to mean **'as well as'** or **'in addition to'**. Compare:

<SayIt text="I don't enjoy watching any sports except (for) cricket." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= I enjoy only cricket — cricket is the sole exception.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Besides cricket, I enjoy watching football and basketball." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= I enjoy three sports — cricket is added to the list.)</p>

<SayIt text="I haven't read anything by her, except (for) one of her short stories." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Besides her novels and poems, she published a number of short stories." />

**Apart from** can be used with the same meanings as **both** *except (for)* **and** *besides*:

<SayIt text="I don't enjoy watching any sports apart from cricket." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= except for — cricket is the exception.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Apart from cricket, I enjoy watching football and basketball." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= besides; as well as — cricket is included too.)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="The apart from shortcut">
  <em>Apart from</em> is the safest choice when you're unsure: it works for both meanings — exception <strong>and</strong> addition. Native speakers use it heavily in both spoken and written English.
</Callout>

## C — but and but for

We can use **but** with a similar meaning to *except (for)*, particularly after negative words such as **no, nobody, everything, anything,** and **nothing**:

<SayIt text="After the operation he could see nothing but / except (for) / apart from vague shadows." />
<SayIt text="There was no way out but / except / apart from upwards, towards the light." />

**But for** has a **different** meaning from *except for*. We use it to say what would or might have happened **if the thing introduced by *but for* had not happened**:

<SayIt text="The country would now be self-sufficient in food but for the drought last year." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= if there hadn't been the drought ...)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="But for the leg injury he suffered last year, he would probably have been picked for the national team by now." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= if he hadn't injured his leg ...)</p>

However, some people use **except for** in the same way as *but for*, particularly in informal spoken English:

<SayIt text="I'd have got there on time except for the taxi being late." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or ... but for the taxi being late.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Except for the problems with my computer, I would have got the book finished weeks ago." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or But for the problems with my computer ...)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't confuse the two">
  <strong>but for</strong> = hypothetical past condition (≈ <em>if it hadn't been for</em>) — always followed by <em>would have / might have / could have</em>.<br />
  <strong>except (for)</strong> = real exception in a factual statement — no hypothetical result follows.<br />
  The informal use of <em>except for</em> as <em>but for</em> is common in speech, but in formal writing keep them distinct.
</Callout>

## D — excepted, apart, aside

We can use **excepted**, **apart** or **aside** after mentioning a person or thing to say that they are **not included** in the statement:

<SayIt text="It has been, 1984 excepted / apart / aside, the hottest July for the last 100 years." />

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-violet-200 bg-violet-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-violet-700">Post-position exception markers</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm text-violet-800">These three — <strong>excepted, apart, aside</strong> — sit <em>after</em> the noun they exclude, separated by commas. They are formal and more common in writing than speech. <em>Excepted</em> is the most formal of the three.</p>
</div>

  Rewrite these three sentences, each time swapping the exception word for a synonym:<br /><br />
  1. <em>Everyone passed the test except for James.</em> → try <em>apart from, but, with the exception of</em><br />
  2. <em>Besides her job, she runs a small charity.</em> → try <em>apart from, in addition to, as well as</em><br />
