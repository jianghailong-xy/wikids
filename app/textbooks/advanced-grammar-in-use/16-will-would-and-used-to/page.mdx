## A — Will and would: characteristic behaviour and general truths

Alongside their better-known jobs (**will** for the future, **would** for the conditional), these two modals have a quieter, very common use. We use **will** (for the present) and **would** (for the past) to talk about:

**1. Characteristic behaviour or habits:**

<SayIt text="Every day Dan will come home from work and turn on the TV." />
<br />
<SayIt text="At school she would always sit quietly and pay attention." />

**2. Things that are — or were — always the case:**

<SayIt text="Cold weather will kill certain plants." />
<br />
<SayIt text="During the war, people would eat all kinds of things that we don't eat now." />

<Callout type="info" title="This isn't the future 'will'">
  Here <em>will</em> doesn't point forward in time — it generalises about what typically happens or what is always true. (For <em>will</em> talking about the future, see <strong>Unit 9</strong>.)
</Callout>

We **don't** use **will** or **would** in this way to talk about a *particular* occasion. Compare a repeated habit with a one-off event:

<div className="not-prose my-6 space-y-3">
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-emerald-700">repeated — will / would</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-emerald-900">Each time I gave him a problem he <strong>would solve</strong> it for me.</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-rose-200 bg-rose-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-rose-700">one occasion — past simple</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-rose-900">Last night I gave him a problem and he <strong>solved</strong> it for me. <span className="font-normal text-rose-500">(not <em>he would solve it</em>)</span></p>
  </div>
</div>

<Callout type="tip" title="The exception: won't / wouldn't">
  The negatives <strong>won't</strong> and <strong>wouldn't</strong> work in <em>either</em> case — for a characteristic behaviour <em>and</em> for a single occasion. Compare: <em>He <strong>wouldn't</strong> walk the five miles to his place of work</em> (characteristic) and <em>She <strong>wouldn't</strong> say what was wrong when I asked her</em> (one particular occasion).
</Callout>

## B — Stressing will and would to criticise

In speech, we can **stress** *will* and *would* to criticise someone's characteristic behaviour or habits. Say the modal with heavy emphasis and it turns into a complaint:

<SayIt text="She just won't do the washing-up when I ask her." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Stressed <strong>WON'T</strong> — an exasperated criticism of what she always does.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I was happy when Ryan left. He would talk about people behind their backs." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Stressed <strong>WOULD</strong> — disapproval of a past habit.)</p>

We can also use a stressed **will** to express disapproval of something someone has done — the sense is "you insist on doing it, so don't be surprised at the result":

<SayIt text="You feel sick? Well, if you will eat so much, I'm not surprised!" />

<Callout type="warning" title="It's the stress that does the work">
  On the page these look like ordinary sentences — the criticism lives in the <em>emphasis</em>. In writing you signal it with context or italics: <em>He <strong>would</strong> leave the door open, wouldn't he?</em>
</Callout>

## C — Drawing conclusions and stating assumptions

We use **will** (for the present) and **would** (for the past) to **draw conclusions** or **state assumptions** about things we believe are the case now. This is a confident guess, not a fact you have checked (see also **Unit 98**):

<SayIt text="Martina will be at home by now. Let's go and see her." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= I'm confident she's home — I'm assuming from what I know.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="You will know that Ewan and Lucy are engaged." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= I assume you already know.)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="Present, not future">
  <em>Martina will be at home</em> says nothing about later — it's a deduction about <em>right now</em>. This is the same "logical" <em>will</em> as in <em>That'll be the postman at the door</em>.
</Callout>

## D — Would and used to

When we talk about **repeated events in the past that don't happen now**, we can use either **would** or **used to + infinitive**. But there is a catch: we can use **would** *only if the time reference is clear*. **Used to** carries no such condition — it can stand on its own. Compare:

<SayIt text="We used to play in the garden." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(not <em>We would play …</em> — no past time frame has been set up.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Whenever we went to my uncle's house, we would play in the garden." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>would</em> or <em>used to</em> — the clause <em>whenever we went …</em> supplies the time frame.)</p>

We can use **used to** — but **not would** — when we talk about **past states that have changed**:

<SayIt text="The factory used to be over there." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Didn't you use to have red hair?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(In questions and negatives the form is <em>use to</em>, without the -d: <em>didn't you <strong>use to</strong> …?</em>)</p>

We use **neither** *used to* **nor** *would* when we say **exactly how many times** something happened in total, **how long** something took, or that a **single event** happened at a given past time. Here the plain past simple is the only option:

<SayIt text="We visited Switzerland four times during the 1990s." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(not <em>We would / used to visit …</em> — a stated number of times.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="She went to Jamaica last month." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(not <em>She would / used to go …</em> — a single, dated event.)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="Two traps in one unit">
  <strong>1.</strong> Don't reach for <em>would</em> to describe a past <em>state</em>: ❌ <em>I would have long hair as a child</em> → ✅ <em>I <strong>used to have</strong> long hair as a child</em>. <br />
  <strong>2.</strong> Don't use either form for a counted or dated event: ❌ <em>He used to phone me twice yesterday</em> → ✅ <em>He <strong>phoned</strong> me twice yesterday</em>.
</Callout>

## E — Would have and will have + past participle

To talk about an **unreal** past situation — something imaginary, or something that *might* have happened but **didn't** — we use **would have + past participle**:

<SayIt text="I would have been happy to see him, but I didn't have time." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(I <em>wasn't</em> able to see him — the meeting is unreal.)</p>

But to say that we **think a past situation actually happened** — a confident assumption about the past — we use **will have + past participle**:

<SayIt text="As it was cloudy, few people will have seen last night's lunar eclipse." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(rather than <em>… would have seen …</em> — we assume it really did happen that way.)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="Two questions to tell them apart">
  Ask yourself: <em>did it happen?</em> <br />
  • <strong>will have + p.p.</strong> = "I'm confident it happened / was the case." (the past twin of the assumption <em>will</em> in section C) <br />
  • <strong>would have + p.p.</strong> = "it didn't happen — I'm imagining it." (the past twin of the conditional <em>would</em>)
</Callout>
