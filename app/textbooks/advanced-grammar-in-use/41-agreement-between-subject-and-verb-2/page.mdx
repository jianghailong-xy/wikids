**Agreement** (or *concord*) is the rule that a singular subject takes a singular verb and a plural subject takes a plural verb. Easy enough — until the subject is a quantifying phrase like *a number of*, *each of*, *neither of* or *one of those who*, where the head word and the noun seem to pull in opposite directions. This unit sorts out which of these expressions count as singular, which count as plural, and which genuinely allow **either** verb.

## A — Quantifying subjects: singular verb, plural verb, or a choice

**With *any of*, *each of*, *either of*, *neither of* or *none of* + a plural noun or pronoun** we can use a singular **or** a plural verb. The singular is preferred in careful, formal written English; the plural is common and natural in speech.

<SayIt text="I don't think any of them knows where the money is hidden. (or … know …)" />
<br />
<SayIt text="Neither of the French athletes has won this year. (or … have …)" />

**With *a* / *the majority of*, *a number of*, *a lot of*, *all (of)* or *some (of)* + a plural noun or pronoun** we use a **plural** verb. But note the trap below.

<SayIt text="A number of refugees have been turned back at the border." />

<Callout type="warning" title="a number of (plural) vs the number of (singular)">
  <strong>A number of</strong> + plural noun is itself plural — the head idea is the people or things. But <strong>the number of</strong> + plural noun is <strong>singular</strong>, because now the head idea is the <em>number</em>: <em>The number of books in the library <strong>has</strong> risen to over five million.</em>
</Callout>

**After *one of* + a plural noun or pronoun** we use a **singular** verb. However, after **one of + a plural noun + *who*** we can often use either a singular or a plural verb (the plural is considered more grammatical, agreeing with the plural noun).

<SayIt text="One of the reasons I took the job was that I could work from home." />
<br />
<SayIt text="He's one of those teachers who insist on pupils sitting silently in class. (or … insists …)" />

**With *any of*, *none of*, *the majority of*, *a lot of*, *plenty of*, *all (of)* or *some (of)* + an *uncountable* noun** we use a **singular** verb.

<SayIt text="All the furniture was destroyed in the fire." />
<br />
<SayIt text="None of the equipment appears to be damaged." />

**With *every* or *each* + a singular noun (or a coordinated *x and y*)** we use a **singular** verb. *(For *each of*, see the first rule above.)*

<SayIt text="Every room looks over the harbour." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Every boy and girl takes part in the activity." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Each child has drawn a picture. (or The children each have drawn a picture.)" />

**With *everyone*, *everybody*, *everything*** — and similar words beginning *any-*, *some-* and *no-* — we use a **singular** verb.

<SayIt text="Practically everyone thinks that Phil should be given the job." />

## B — Two or more items joined by *and*

When a subject is two or more items joined by **and**, we usually use a **plural** verb:

<SayIt text="Ingrid and Tobias are moving back to Australia." />

However, phrases connected by *and* can also be followed by a **singular** verb if we think of them as making up a **single item**:

<SayIt text="The lorry, its cargo and passengers weighs around 35 tonnes. (or … weigh …)" />

## C — Items joined by *(either) … or …* and *(neither) … nor …*

When a subject is two or more items joined by **(either) … or …** or **(neither) … nor …**, the verb agrees with the **last** item: a **singular** verb if the last item is singular (though a plural verb is sometimes used in informal English), and a **plural** verb if the last item is plural.

<SayIt text="The station or the cinema is a good place to meet. (or … are … in informal English)" />
<br />
<SayIt text="The President or his representatives are to attend the meeting." />

When the subject's final item is singular but the previous item is plural, we can use **either** a singular or a plural verb:

<SayIt text="Either the teachers or the principal is to blame for the accident. (or … are to blame …)" />

## D — *there + be* / *there + have*

In **there + be** or **there + have** (see Unit 95), we choose a singular form before a singular or uncountable noun, and a plural form before plural nouns. However, in **informal speech** we often use a shortened singular form (*There's*, *There was*) even with plural nouns.

<SayIt text="Over the last few years there have been many improvements in car safety." />
<br />
<SayIt text="There's been lots of good films on lately. (or There've been …)" />

<Callout type="tip" title="Take it further">
  Keep a small two-column list as you read this week. Every time you meet a quantifying subject — <em>a number of, the majority of, neither of, one of those who, all of, there's</em> — note whether the writer chose a singular or a plural verb, and decide whether you'd have chosen the same. The cases that genuinely allow <strong>either</strong> (the formal-singular / informal-plural ones) are the ones worth saying both ways out loud until the choice feels deliberate rather than accidental.
</Callout>
