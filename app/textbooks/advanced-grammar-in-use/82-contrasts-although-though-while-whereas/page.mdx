## A — Although and though

We use **although** or — less formally — **though** to signal a *surprising contrast* between what happens in the **main clause** and what happens in the **adverbial clause**:

<SayIt text="Although Reid failed to score himself, he helped Jones to score two goals." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Though Reid failed to score himself, he helped Jones to score two goals." />

With a similar meaning, we can reach for the heavier **despite / in spite of** family. Notice what each one is followed by:

<div className="not-prose my-6 space-y-3">
  <div className="rounded-xl border border-slate-200 bg-white p-4 shadow-sm">
    <p className="font-semibold text-slate-800"><span className="text-sky-700">despite / in spite of the fact that</span> + clause</p>
    <p className="text-sm text-slate-600">Despite the fact that Reid failed to score, he helped Jones to score twice.</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border border-slate-200 bg-white p-4 shadow-sm">
    <p className="font-semibold text-slate-800"><span className="text-sky-700">despite / in spite of</span> + <strong>-ing</strong></p>
    <p className="text-sm text-slate-600">Despite failing to score, Reid helped Jones to score twice.</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border border-slate-200 bg-white p-4 shadow-sm">
    <p className="font-semibold text-slate-800"><span className="text-sky-700">despite / in spite of his / her</span> + <strong>noun</strong></p>
    <p className="text-sm text-slate-600">Despite his failure to score, Reid helped Jones to score twice.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<Callout type="warning" title="Never 'despite of'">
  It is <strong>despite</strong> + noun/-ing, but <strong>in spite of</strong> + noun/-ing. <br />
  ✅ <em>despite the rain</em> &nbsp;·&nbsp; ✅ <em>in spite of the rain</em> &nbsp;·&nbsp; ❌ <s>despite of the rain</s>
</Callout>

**End-position *though*.** We can use **though**, but **not** *although*, at the *end* of a clause — a very common spoken move:

<SayIt text="I eat most dairy products. I'm not keen on yogurt, though." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(❌ <s>… I'm not keen on yogurt, although.</s>)</p>

**Fronting the adjective for emphasis.** We can give special emphasis to an adjective by putting it *before* **though**, in the pattern **adjective + though + subject + verb**. The verb is usually a linking verb — *appear, be, become, feel, look, seem, sound, prove*, etc. In this structure **as** (but **not** *although*) can replace *though*:

<SayIt text="Hot though the night air was, they slept soundly." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Hot as the night air was, they slept soundly." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">= the plainer <em>Although / Though the night air was hot, they slept soundly.</em></p>

<Callout type="tip" title="Mind the word order">
  The fronted adjective comes first, then <em>though / as</em>, then the subject, then the verb: <br />
  ✅ <strong>Experienced though the climbers were</strong>, … &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ <s>Experienced though were the climbers</s> …
</Callout>

## B — Even though and even if

These two look like twins but mean opposite things.

- **even though** (never *even although*) = **'despite the fact that'** — the speaker treats the situation as a **known fact**.
- **even if** = **'whether or not'** — the speaker is talking about a **possibility**, not a fact.

<div className="not-prose my-6 overflow-hidden rounded-2xl border-2 border-violet-200 shadow-sm">
  <div className="grid grid-cols-1 text-sm sm:grid-cols-3">
    <div className="bg-violet-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-violet-900">Sentence</div>
    <div className="bg-violet-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-violet-900 sm:border-l sm:border-violet-200">Means</div>
    <div className="bg-violet-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-violet-900 sm:border-l sm:border-violet-200">What the speaker knows</div>

    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>Even though</strong> Matt doesn't speak Spanish, I think he should still visit Madrid.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700 sm:border-l sm:border-violet-100">= Despite the fact that he doesn't speak Spanish</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700 sm:border-l sm:border-violet-100">The speaker <strong>knows</strong> Matt doesn't speak Spanish.</div>

    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 bg-violet-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>Even if</strong> Matt doesn't speak Spanish, I think he should still visit Madrid.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 bg-violet-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700 sm:border-l sm:border-violet-100">= Whether or not he speaks Spanish</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 bg-violet-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700 sm:border-l sm:border-violet-100">The speaker <strong>doesn't know</strong> whether Matt speaks Spanish or not.</div>
  </div>
</div>

<Callout type="info" title="The quick test">
  Could you swap in <em>whether or not</em>? Then it's <strong>even if</strong>. Could you swap in <em>despite the fact that</em>? Then it's <strong>even though</strong>.
</Callout>

## C — While, whilst and whereas

In **formal** contexts, **while** or **whilst** can carry a meaning close to *'although'* — introducing something that *qualifies* the main clause or *seems to conflict* with it. With this concessive meaning, the **while / whilst** clause comes **before or within** the main clause, but **not after** it:

<SayIt text="While there is no evidence that Rob cheated, we were all astonished that he passed the exam." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(❌ <s>We were all astonished that he passed the exam, while there is no evidence …</s>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The diesel model of the car, while more expensive, is better value for money." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Here the <em>while</em> clause sits <em>within</em> the main clause.)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="A note on 'whilst'">
  <em>Whilst</em> means exactly the same as <em>while</em> but is a rather literary, old-fashioned word — many writers avoid it. Safe in formal prose; out of place in conversation.
</Callout>

**Whereas for direct contrast.** We use **while** or **whereas** (or, less often, *whilst*) to say that one thing simply **contrasts** with another. Here the **while / whereas** clause may come **before or after** the main clause:

<SayIt text="Juan gets lots of homework from school, whereas Mia gets very little." />
<br />
<SayIt text="While I always feel I would pass the exam, I never thought I would get an A grade." />

<Callout type="warning" title="The whereas trap">
  Do <strong>not</strong> use <em>whereas</em> when the subordinate clause makes the main clause <em>unexpected</em> (i.e. a real concession). Use <em>although / while</em> there instead: <br />
  ✅ <strong>Although / While</strong> Sophie's father is from Spain, she doesn't speak Spanish. <br />
  ❌ <s>Whereas Sophie's father is from Spain, she doesn't speak Spanish.</s>
</Callout>

After **although, though, while** and **whilst**, we can also use **-ing** and **past participle (-ed)** clauses, and clauses with the subject and verb left out — *Although tired, they kept walking* / *While waiting, I read the paper* (see Unit 59D).
