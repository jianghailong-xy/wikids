Units 22 and 23 dealt with how passives are *built*. This unit is about the more interesting question: **why a writer reaches for one in the first place.** A passive is rarely forced on you by grammar — it is a choice, and at C1 the skill is knowing what that choice buys you.

## A — Why choose a passive? Mostly, to drop the doer

The headline reason is that a passive lets us **omit the agent** (= the subject of the matching active sentence) simply by leaving out the *by*-phrase. We prefer the passive when the agent is:

<div className="not-prose my-6 overflow-hidden rounded-2xl border-2 border-emerald-200 shadow-sm">
  <div className="grid grid-cols-[auto_1fr] text-sm">
    <div className="bg-emerald-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-emerald-900">the agent is …</div>
    <div className="bg-sky-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-sky-900">example</div>
    <div className="border-t border-emerald-100 px-4 py-3 font-semibold text-emerald-800">not known</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">My office <strong>was broken into</strong> when I was on holiday.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-emerald-100 bg-emerald-50/50 px-4 py-3 font-semibold text-emerald-800">'people in general'</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 bg-sky-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">An order form <strong>can be found</strong> on page 2.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-emerald-100 px-4 py-3 font-semibold text-emerald-800">unimportant</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">He <strong>is thought</strong> to be somewhere in Russia.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-emerald-100 bg-emerald-50/50 px-4 py-3 font-semibold text-emerald-800">obvious</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 bg-sky-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">She <strong>is being treated</strong> in hospital. <span className="text-slate-400">(the agent is clearly 'medical staff')</span></div>
  </div>
</div>

<SayIt text="My office was broken into when I was on holiday." />
<br />
<SayIt text="An order form can be found on page 2." />

In **factual writing** — especially when describing procedures or processes — the doer is so often irrelevant that passives pile up naturally:

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-2xl border-2 border-amber-200 bg-amber-50 p-5 text-sm leading-relaxed text-slate-700 shadow-sm">
  <p className="mb-2 text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-amber-700">describing a process — the agent disappears</p>
  Nuclear waste will still be radioactive even after 20,000 years, so it <strong>must be disposed of</strong> very carefully. It <strong>can be stored</strong> as a liquid in stainless-steel containers which <strong>are encased</strong> in concrete. The most dangerous nuclear waste <strong>can be turned</strong> into glass, which <strong>will be stored</strong> in deep underground mines.
</div>

<Callout type="info" title="Informal active vs formal passive">
  In conversation we happily use an active with a vague subject — <em>people, somebody, something, they, we, you</em> — even when we don't actually know who the agent is. In formal contexts we prefer the passive so we can avoid mentioning an agent at all. Compare:
  <br />· <em>They're installing the new computer system next month.</em> <span className="text-slate-400">(spoken)</span>
  <br />· <em>The new computer system <strong>is being installed</strong> next month.</em> <span className="text-slate-400">(more formal)</span>
</Callout>

## B — Turn the verb into a noun, then passivise

Many verbs have a **related noun** that carries the same meaning (*install → installation*, *consider → consideration*). That noun can become the subject of a passive sentence, with a **new passive verb** introduced to do the work. It pushes the action even further into the background:

<SayIt text="The installation of the new computer system will be completed by next month." />

<div className="not-prose my-6 grid gap-3 sm:grid-cols-2">
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-violet-200 bg-violet-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-violet-700">verb → noun</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-violet-900">install → installation · consider → consideration · appoint → appointment · demolish → demolition · present → presentation · resist → resistance</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-sky-200 bg-sky-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-sky-700">verbs that 'carry' the noun</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-sky-900">be given · be made · be completed · be expected … <span className="font-normal text-sky-700">Consideration will be <strong>given</strong> to … · The appointment will be <strong>made</strong> …</span></p>
  </div>
</div>

## C — Keep the topic first, the heavy part last

English prefers to put the **topic** (what is already being talked about) at the **start** of a clause and the **comment** on it at the **end**. The passive is one of the main tools for arranging information this way. Watch where the topic — *the valves* — lands in the second sentence of each pair:

<div className="not-prose my-6 space-y-3">
  <div className="rounded-xl border border-slate-200 bg-white p-4 shadow-sm">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-rose-600">active — topic buried at the end</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm text-slate-700">The three machines tested for the report contained different types of safety valve. The Boron Group in Germany manufactured <em>all the valves</em>.</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border border-slate-200 bg-white p-4 shadow-sm">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-emerald-600">passive — topic kept up front</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm text-slate-700"><em>All the valves</em> <strong>were manufactured</strong> by the Boron Group in Germany.</p>
  </div>
</div>

The same principle handles **long, heavy subjects**. We'd rather not open a sentence with a great clause-shaped lump, so the passive lets us shift it to the end:

<SayIt text="I was surprised by Dev's decision to give up his job and move to Sydney." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">More natural than <em>'Dev's decision to give up his job and move to Sydney surprised me'</em> — though the choice can depend on style and context.</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="The end-weight test">
  If the 'doer' of an active sentence is long and the 'done-to' is short, flip it: make the short thing the subject and let the passive carry the long agent (or drop it) to the end. <em>Given / new</em> and <em>light / heavy</em> almost always want the same order.
</Callout>

## D — *It is believed that …*, not *That … was believed*

You **can** make a *that*-clause the subject of a passive, but it sounds clumsy. The normal move is to start with an empty **it** and push the *that*-clause to the end (more on this in **Unit 25**):

<div className="not-prose my-6 space-y-2">
  <div className="rounded-xl border border-slate-200 bg-white p-3 text-sm text-slate-700 shadow-sm">Everybody <strong>believed</strong> (that) the plan would fail. <span className="text-slate-400">— active</span></div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50 p-3 text-sm font-semibold text-emerald-900 shadow-sm">✓ <strong>It was believed</strong> that the plan would fail. <span className="font-normal text-emerald-700">— natural passive</span></div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-rose-200 bg-rose-50 p-3 text-sm text-slate-700 shadow-sm">✗ <em>That the plan would fail was believed by everybody.</em> <span className="text-rose-400">— grammatical but awkward</span></div>
</div>

<SayIt text="It was believed that the plan would fail." />

<Callout type="warning" title="Two traps with passives">
  ✅ The accident <strong>happened</strong> last night. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ The accident was happened last night. <span className="text-slate-500">(intransitive verbs — happen, disappear, rise, arrive — have <strong>no passive</strong>: there is no object to promote)</span><br />
  ✅ <strong>It is thought</strong> that he is in Russia. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ That he is in Russia is thought. <span className="text-slate-500">(start with <em>it</em>, not the that-clause)</span>
</Callout>
