English hates repeating a whole verb group. Once an auxiliary has been said, we can leave out everything that would otherwise follow it, and the listener reconstructs the missing words from the previous clause. This unit gathers the trickier cases: when there is more than one auxiliary, when there is **no** auxiliary at all, and the special behaviour of **be** and **have**.

## A — Leaving out everything after the auxiliary

To avoid repeating words from a previous clause or sentence, we use an auxiliary verb (**be, have, can, will, would**, etc.) on its own — instead of a whole verb group, or a verb and what follows it:

<SayIt text="She says she's finished, but I don't think she has." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(instead of <em>... I don't think she has finished.</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="'Would any of you like to go to Paris?' 'I would.'" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(instead of <em>I would like to go to Paris.</em>)</p>

If there is **more than one auxiliary** in the previous clause or sentence, we keep only the **first** and leave out the rest — or we can keep two or three of them:

<SayIt text="'They could have been delayed by the snow.' 'Yes, they could.'" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>... could have</em>, or <em>... could have been</em> — all natural.)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="Keep just the first auxiliary — or as far down the chain as you like">
  From <em>could have been delayed</em> you can stop at any auxiliary: <strong>could</strong> · <strong>could have</strong> · <strong>could have been</strong>. The shorter forms are more common in speech; the longer ones add a little emphasis. What you can't do is strand a contracted form — <em>Yes, I'<strong>m</strong></em>. and <em>she'<strong>s</strong></em>. are wrong; say the full <em>Yes, I am.</em> / <em>she has.</em>
</Callout>

## B — No auxiliary? Use a form of *do*, *be*, or *have*

If there is **no** auxiliary verb in the previous clause or sentence — or there is a form of **do** — we use a form of **do** instead of repeating the main verb. We use **do** when the main verb is present simple and **did** when it is past simple:

<SayIt text="Mona plays golf on Saturdays, and I do too." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(instead of <em>... and I play golf on Saturdays too.</em>; <em>... and so do I</em> is also possible.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="'I didn't steal the money.' 'No one thinks that you did.'" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(instead of <em>... that you stole it.</em>; <em>No one thinks so</em> is also possible.)</p>

If **be** is the main verb in the previous clause or sentence, we repeat a form of the verb **be**:

<SayIt text="'The children are noisy again.' 'They always are.'" />

If **have** or **have got** is the main verb, we can usually use a form of **either do or have**:

<SayIt text="'Do you think I have a chance of winning?' 'Yes, I think you have.'" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>... you do</em>; <em>Yes, I think so</em> is also possible.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Even if he hasn't got a great car himself, he may know someone who has." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>... someone who does.</em>)</p>

However, if we use **have + noun** to talk about an **action** (*have a shower, have a shave, have a good time*, etc.), we prefer **do**:

<SayIt text="I wasn't expecting to have a good time at the party, but I did." />

<Callout type="warning" title="have + noun = an action → use do, not have">
  ✅ I didn't expect to have a good time, but I <strong>did</strong>. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ ... but I <s>had</s>. <br />
  ✅ Did you have a shave this morning? — Yes, I <strong>did</strong>. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ ... Yes, I <s>had</s>.
</Callout>

<Callout type="info" title="When do, be and have all work">
  Sometimes more than one substitute fits, with a slightly different meaning: <br />
  <em>I asked Clara to tidy her room, and she <strong>has</strong> / <strong>did</strong>.</em> — <em>has</em> stands for <em>has tidied her room</em>; <em>did</em> stands for <em>tidied her room</em>.
</Callout>

## C — *have ... done* and *do* after a modal

If we use **have** as an auxiliary verb, we can often follow it with **done** instead of repeating the main verb. This happens particularly in spoken English:

<SayIt text="'She's never made a mistake before.' 'Well, she has (done) this time.'" />

This is usually **not** possible when the verb being talked about is **intransitive**:

<SayIt text="'They've already gone.' 'I don't think Daniel has.'" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(not <em>... I don't think Daniel has <s>done</s>.</em> — <em>go</em> is intransitive.)</p>

Similarly, after a **modal** (*can, could, may, might, must, ought to, shall, should, will, would*) we can add **do**, again especially in speech:

<SayIt text="'Will you be seeing David today?' 'I might (do).'" />

Sometimes a form of **do** carries a similar meaning to the original (see also **D**):

<SayIt text="'Will you be seeing Felix today?' 'I might (do / be).'" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>do</em> stands for <em>see Felix today</em>; <em>be</em> stands for <em>be seeing Felix today</em>.)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="The intransitive block">
  You can echo a transitive verb with <em>have done</em> (<em>she has done</em> = <em>she has made a mistake</em>), but not an intransitive one: <em>arrive, go, come, fall, happen</em> take a bare <em>has / have</em>. So <em>'Has the train left?' 'It has.'</em> — not <em>It has done.</em>
</Callout>

## D — *be* after a modal

If we use **be** as an **auxiliary** verb in the previous clause or sentence, we can use **be** after a modal:

<SayIt text="'Is Ella staying for lunch?' 'Yes, I think she will (be).'" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>... she will do.</em>)</p>

However, if **be** is used as a **main verb**, or as an auxiliary verb within a **passive**, we can usually leave out **be** after a modal **only in informal contexts**. Compare:

<SayIt text="'Lina's late again.' 'I thought she might (be).'" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(informal — <em>be</em> is the main verb here, so dropping it is casual.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="It has been found that the comet is made entirely of gas, and it was predicted that it would be." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(formal / written — <em>be</em> is part of a passive, so we keep it.)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="Auxiliary be vs main / passive be">
  After a modal, you can always drop a continuous-aspect <em>be</em> (<em>she will (be)</em> ← <em>is staying</em>). But when <em>be</em> is the linking verb (<em>Lina is late</em>) or the passive auxiliary (<em>the comet is made of gas</em>), keeping <em>be</em> is the safe, neutral choice — leave it out only in relaxed speech.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Take it further">
  Listen for ellipsis in real conversation. Almost every short answer — <em>Yes, I have. / No, it isn't. / I think so. / She might (be).</em> — is a clause with everything after the auxiliary deleted. Try answering questions today using the <strong>shortest</strong> form that's still grammatical: it's one of the clearest signals of a fluent, natural speaker.
