Unit 53 dealt with the core relative pronouns — *who, whom, which, that* and the zero relative. This unit gathers the **other relative words** that do the same connecting job but carry extra meaning of their own: **whose** (possession or association), **when / whereby / where / why** (time, method, place, reason), and the *wh-* words **what, whatever, whoever** and **whichever**, which can stand in for a whole noun phrase. Many of these belong to a more formal, written register, and several have a tidy *preposition + which* alternative that is worth knowing.

## A — Clauses with *whose*

We use a relative clause beginning with the relative pronoun **whose + noun**, particularly in **written English**, when we talk about something *belonging to* or *associated with* a person, animal or plant:

<SayIt text="Stevenson is an architect whose designs have won international praise." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Suzy was taking care of a dog whose ears were badly damaged in a fight with a cat." />

We can use **whose** in both **defining** and **non-defining** relative clauses.

<Callout type="warning" title="Avoid whose for an ordinary thing">
  We generally <strong>avoid</strong> using <strong>whose</strong> to talk about something belonging to or associated with a <em>thing</em>. Rephrase with <em>its</em> in a separate clause instead:<br /><br />
  <span className="text-emerald-700">More natural:</span> I received a letter, and <em>its</em> poor spelling made me think it was written by a child.<br />
  <span className="text-rose-600">Less natural:</span> I received a letter, <em>whose</em> poor spelling made me think&nbsp;…
</Callout>

However, we **sometimes** do use **whose** when we talk about **towns, countries or organisations**:

<SayIt text="The film was made in Botswana, whose wildlife parks are larger than those in Kenya." />
<br />
<SayIt text="We need to learn from companies whose trading is healthier than our own." />

And in **academic writing**, **whose** is used to talk about a much wider variety of 'belonging to' relationships, even with abstract things:

<SayIt text="Students have to solve problems whose solutions require a knowledge of calculus." />

<Callout type="info" title="Reminder → J1–J5">
  For the full picture of defining vs non-defining clauses, commas, and when the relative pronoun can be left out, see the relative-clause sections (J1–J5) and Unit 53.
</Callout>

## B — Clauses with *when, whereby, where* and *why*

We can begin relative and other clauses with **when** (referring to *time*), **whereby** (*method* or *means*; used mainly in **formal** contexts), and **where** (*location*). In formal English in particular, a phrase with **preposition + which** can often be used instead of these:

<SayIt text="The camera records the time when the photo is taken." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or … the time <em>at which</em> the photo is taken.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Do you know the date when we have to hand in the essay?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or … the date <em>on / by which</em> we have to hand in the essay?)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="We need to develop a system whereby workers and management can communicate more effectively." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or … a system <em>by / with which</em> workers and management can communicate …)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="This was the place where we first met." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or … the place <em>at / in which</em> we first met.)</p>

In **academic English**, we can also use **where** to refer to features *other than location*, particularly after words such as **case, condition, example, situation, system**:

<SayIt text="Later in this chapter we will discuss cases where consumer complaints have resulted in changes in the law." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or, more formally, … cases <em>in which</em> consumer complaints have resulted …)</p>

We can also use **why**, or **a / the reason that**, or just **a / the reason**, to give a reason:

<SayIt text="I didn't get a pay rise, but this wasn't the reason why I left." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or … the reason <em>(that)</em> I left.)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="The formal swap">
  Whenever <em>when / where / whereby</em> feels too plain for an academic or legal register, try <em>preposition + which</em>: <em>the day on which</em>, <em>the means by which</em>, <em>the point at which</em>. The choice signals register, not a change in meaning.
</Callout>

## C — Clauses with *who* and *what*; *whatever, whoever* and *whichever*

Some clauses beginning with a **wh-word** are used like a **noun phrase** in a sentence. These are sometimes called **nominal relative clauses**:

<SayIt text="Can you give me a list of who's been invited?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= a list of <em>the people who have been invited</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I didn't know what I should do next." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= <em>the thing that</em> I should do next)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't use what after a noun">
  We can't use <strong>what</strong> in this way <em>after a noun</em> — use <strong>that</strong> (or <em>which</em>) instead:<br /><br />
  <span className="text-emerald-700">✓</span> I managed to get all the <em>books</em> <strong>that</strong> you asked for.<br />
  <span className="text-rose-600">✗</span> I managed to get all the books <strong>what</strong> you asked for.
</Callout>

We use clauses beginning with **whatever** (= *anything*, or *it doesn't matter what*), **whoever** (= *the person / group who*, or *any person / group who*), or **whichever** (= *one thing or person from a limited number*) to talk about things or people that are **indefinite or unknown**:

<SayIt text="I'm sure I'll enjoy eating whatever you cook." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Whoever wins will go on to play Barcelona in the final." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Whichever one of you broke the window will have to pay for it." />
