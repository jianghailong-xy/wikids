English gives you several ways to express **purpose** (why you do something) and **result** (what happens because of it). The simplest is a bare *to*-infinitive — *I went to the shop to buy milk* — but formal writing and careful speech draw on a wider toolkit: *in order to*, *so as to*, *so that*, *in order that*, *such that*, and *in such a way that / as to*. This unit maps exactly when to reach for each one, how the negative forms work, and where the line runs between purpose and result.

## A — In order / so as + to-infinitive

To talk about the **purpose** of an action we can use **in order / so as + to-infinitive**:

<SayIt text="He took the course in order to get a better job." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Trees are being planted by the roadside so as to reduce traffic noise." />

In spoken English in particular it is much more common simply to use a **to-infinitive** without *in order* or *so as* to express the same meaning:

<SayIt text="He took the course to get a better job." />

<Callout type="warning" title="Negative purpose: not just 'not to'">
  We <strong>rarely</strong> use just <em>not + to-infinitive</em> for negative purpose. Instead, use <strong>so as not to</strong> or <strong>in order not to</strong>:<br /><br />
  ✓ <em>He kept the speech vague <strong>in order not to</strong> commit himself to one side or the other.</em><br />
  ✓ <em>I wrote down her name <strong>so as not to</strong> forget it.</em><br />
  ✗ <em>He kept the speech vague <strong>not to</strong> commit himself ...</em>
</Callout>

However, in **contrastive** sentences we can use *not + to-infinitive, but + to-infinitive*:

<SayIt text="I came to see you not to complain, but to apologise." />

We can also put *in order / so as* before the to-infinitives in sentences like this:

<SayIt text="I came to see you not in order to complain, but in order to apologise." />

## B — In order that and so that

We also use **in order that** and **so that** to talk about a purpose. Compare:

<SayIt text="She stayed at work late in order to complete the report." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(+ to-infinitive — no separate subject)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="She stayed at work late in order that she could complete the report." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(+ clause with its own subject — <em>she</em>)</p>

**So that** is more common than *in order that*, and is used in less formal situations. Informally we can leave out *that* after *so*, but we always include it after *in order*:

<SayIt text="I hid the presents so Marianna wouldn't find them." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(informal, <em>that</em> omitted)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="Tense agreement with in order that / so that">
  A <strong>present</strong> tense verb in the main clause is usually followed by a present tense verb (or a modal with present or future reference — <em>can, will</em>, etc.) in the purpose clause. A <strong>past</strong> tense verb in the main clause is usually followed by a past tense verb (or a modal with past reference — <em>could, would</em>, etc.). Modal verbs are very often used after <em>in order that / so that</em>:<br /><br />
  <em>Regular checks are made <strong>in order that</strong> safety standards <strong>are</strong> maintained.</em><br />
  <em>Advice is given <strong>in order that</strong> students <strong>can</strong> choose the best course.</em><br />
  <em>Did you give up your job <strong>so that</strong> you <strong>could</strong> take care of your mother?</em><br />
  <em>I hid the presents <strong>so that</strong> Marianna <strong>wouldn't</strong> find them.</em>
</Callout>

## C — Such that, in such a way that / as to; such ... that

In **formal** contexts, such as academic writing, we can use **such that** to introduce a **result**:

<SayIt text="The model was designed such that the value of x could be calculated." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= in a way that has the result that ... ; or = in order that ... ; or = so that ...)</p>

Less formally we can also use **in such a way that** or **in such a way as + to-infinitive** with a similar meaning:

<SayIt text="The advertisement is printed in such a way that two very different pictures can be seen depending on how you look at it." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Our business is managed in such a way as to minimise its environmental impact." />

We can also use **such + noun phrase + that** to introduce a result:

<SayIt text="It is such a popular play that all the performances were sold out after the first day." />

<Callout type="tip" title="such ... that vs so ... that">
  Use <strong>such + noun phrase + that</strong> for a result involving a noun:<br />
  <em>It was <strong>such a</strong> hot day <strong>that</strong> we stayed indoors.</em><br /><br />
  Use <strong>so + adjective/adverb + that</strong> for a result involving a quality or manner (see Unit 73):<br />
  <em>It was <strong>so</strong> hot <strong>that</strong> we stayed indoors.</em>
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="The rule at a glance">
  **Positive purpose**: <em>to + infinitive</em> ✓, <em>in order to</em> ✓, <em>so as to</em> ✓ — all three work.<br />
  **Negative purpose**: only <em>so as not to</em> ✓ and <em>in order not to</em> ✓ — bare <em>not to</em> ✗.<br />
