A relative clause often needs a preposition — *the man **to** whom I spoke*, *the rate **at** which it heats up*. The advanced question is **where the preposition goes**: fronted before the relative pronoun for a formal, written style, or left at the end of the clause for a more natural, spoken one. This unit also covers the formal **noun + of which** pattern and the awkward case of phrasal verbs.

## A — Formal style: noun + of which / of whose

In formal styles, **noun + of which** is often preferred to two less formal alternatives.

It is preferred to **whose + noun**:

<SayIt text="A huge amount of oil was spilled, the effects of which are still being felt." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or ... <em>whose effects</em> are still being felt.)</p>

It is also preferred to a **that / which ... + preposition** clause, where the preposition sits at the end:

<SayIt text="The school of which she is head is closing." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(less formally: The school <strong>(that / which)</strong> she is head <strong>of</strong> is closing.)</p>

After **both** we can use **of which** and **of whose**, but not usually **which** or **whose** on their own:

<SayIt text="Lotta was able to switch between German and Russian, both of which she spoke fluently." />

<Callout type="warning" title="Keep the 'of'">
  ✅ ... both <strong>of which</strong> she spoke fluently. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ ... both <em>which</em> she spoke fluently.
</Callout>

<Callout type="info" title="The same 'of which / of whose' pattern follows many quantifiers">
  Not just <strong>both</strong>, but also <strong>all, each, many, most, neither, none, part, some, a number</strong> — plus numbers (<em>one, two</em> ...), ordinals (<em>the first, the second</em> ...), fractions (<em>half, a third</em> ...) and <strong>superlatives</strong> (<em>the best, the biggest</em> ...): <em>I have three sisters, <strong>all of whom</strong> are doctors</em>; <em>a castle, <strong>part of which</strong> dates back to 1466</em>.
</Callout>

## B — A preposition before whose

In formal, mainly written, English a preposition can come **before whose** in a relative clause. Putting the preposition at the end of the clause is more natural in informal and spoken English:

<SayIt text="I now turn to Freud, from whose work the following quotation is taken." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(less formally: ... Freud, <strong>whose</strong> work the following quotation is taken <strong>from</strong>.)</p>

## C — Putting the preposition before which / whom

When a preposition is needed with the relative pronouns **which** and **whom**, we usually put it **before** the pronoun in formal styles:

<SayIt text="The rate at which a material heats up depends on its chemical composition." />
<br />
<SayIt text="There are 80 teachers in the Physics Department, among whom are 24 professors." />

After a preposition we use **whom** rather than **who** in formal styles, and we do **not** use **that** or a zero (dropped) pronoun:

<SayIt text="Is it right that politicians make decisions without consulting the public to whom they are accountable?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(rather than ... the public <em>to who</em> they are accountable.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The valley in which the town lies is heavily polluted." />

<Callout type="warning" title="When the preposition is fronted">
  ✅ The valley <strong>in which</strong> the town lies ... <br />
  ❌ The valley <em>in that</em> the town lies ... &nbsp;(no <em>that</em> after a preposition) <br />
  ❌ The valley <em>in</em> the town lies ... &nbsp;(you can't drop the pronoun)
</Callout>

In **less formal** English we usually put the preposition **later** in the clause, and we prefer **who** (or **that**, or no pronoun) to **whom**:

<SayIt text="The office that Juan took us to was filled with books." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(rather than the formal: The office <strong>to which</strong> Juan took us ...)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The playground wasn't used by the children who it was built for." />

## D — Phrasal (two- and three-word) verbs

If the verb in the relative clause is a **two-word verb** (e.g. *come across, fill in, look after, take on*), we do **not** usually put the preposition before the relative pronoun. The particle stays with its verb:

<SayIt text="The Roman coins, which a local farmer came across in a field, are now on display in the National Museum." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(not ... coins, <em>across which</em> the local farmer came, are ...)</p>

With **three-word verbs** (*look up to, look forward to, do away with* ...), we only front the preposition in a very formal or literary style, and many people avoid the pattern altogether:

<SayIt text="She is one of the few people to whom I look up." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or, less formally: ... <strong>who</strong> I <strong>look up to</strong>.)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="Quick test">
  If the preposition belongs to a phrasal verb, keep it at the end: <em>the form <strong>which</strong> she filled <strong>in</strong></em>, not <em>the form <strong>in which</strong> she filled</em>. Fronting only sounds natural when the preposition is independent of the verb (<em>the desk <strong>on which</strong> she filled in the form</em>).
</Callout>
