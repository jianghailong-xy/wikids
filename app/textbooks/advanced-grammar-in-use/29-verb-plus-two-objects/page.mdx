When a verb takes two objects, English gives you a choice of word order — and that choice is not free. This unit sorts the verbs into four groups: those that swap their objects freely (putting **to** or **for** in front of the person), those where **to** and **for** carry a **different meaning**, those that **never** reverse, and those that can only reach a second object **through a preposition**. Knowing which group a verb belongs to is what stops *She described me the situation* and *He fixed me the tap* before they leave your mouth.

## A — Indirect object + direct object, and the reorder

Some verbs can be followed by **two objects**. Usually the first object — the **indirect object (IO)** — is a person or group of people, and the second — the **direct object (DO)** — is a thing:

<SayIt text="Can you bring me some milk from the shops?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>me</em> = IO, a person &middot; <em>some milk</em> = DO, a thing.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="He made himself a cup of coffee." />

Many verbs that can have two objects may also be used with a **DO only** (e.g. *I read a story*). With many of them, you can **reverse the order** of the objects if you put **for** or **to** before the IO — which then becomes a **prepositional object**. Compare:

<SayIt text="I built my daughter a doll's house." />
<SayIt text="I built a doll's house for my daughter." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Can you pass me that bandage?" />
<SayIt text="Can you pass that bandage to me?" />

<div className="not-prose my-6 grid gap-4 sm:grid-cols-2">
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-emerald-700">Reorder with <em>for</em> + object</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-emerald-900">book &middot; buy &middot; catch &middot; choose &middot; cook &middot; fetch &middot; find &middot; get &middot; make &middot; order &middot; pour &middot; save</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-sky-200 bg-sky-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-sky-700">Reorder with <em>to</em> + object</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-sky-900">award &middot; give &middot; hand &middot; lend &middot; offer &middot; owe &middot; show &middot; teach &middot; tell &middot; throw</p>
  </div>
</div>

We often use the **preposition** pattern to focus particular attention on the object after **for / to**. We also use it when the **IO is a lot longer than the DO**:

<SayIt text="Jasmin taught music to a large number of children at the school." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>Jasmin taught a large number of children at the school music.</em> — the long IO would strand <em>music</em> awkwardly at the end.)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="If the direct object is a pronoun, use DO + preposition + IO">
  When the DO is a pronoun (<em>it, them, one</em>), the natural pattern is <strong>DO + preposition + IO</strong>. The double-object version is avoided as bad style:
  <br /><br />
  ✅ I gave them <strong>to</strong> Isa. &nbsp; <span className="text-rose-600">rather than</span> &nbsp; ❌ I gave Isa them. / I gave them Isa.<br />
  ✅ We bought it <strong>for</strong> them. &nbsp; <span className="text-rose-600">rather than</span> &nbsp; ❌ We bought them it.
</Callout>

## B — *to* vs *for*: transfer or benefit

Some verbs work with **either** preposition, and often the choice changes the meaning: **to** suggests a **transfer** of something to someone; **for** suggests that someone **benefits** from what you do. Compare:

<SayIt text="I hadn't got time to visit Mira, so I wrote a letter to her." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<strong>to</strong> = the letter travels to her — she is the addressee.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Mira had broken her wrist, so I wrote a letter for her." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<strong>for</strong> = I did the writing in her place, for her benefit — she may not even be the addressee.)</p>

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-amber-200 bg-amber-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-amber-700">These verbs take either <em>to</em> or <em>for</em> — mind the meaning</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-amber-900">bring &middot; leave &middot; pay &middot; play &middot; post &middot; read &middot; sell &middot; send &middot; sing &middot; take</p>
</div>

Sometimes, though, the two are **very close in meaning**:

<SayIt text="He played the piece to me." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>to</em> or <em>for</em> — barely any difference.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Can you sing that song again to us?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>to</em> or <em>for</em> both work.)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="What the double-object version means">
  When you use <strong>object + object</strong> after these verbs, it normally matches the <strong>object + to + object</strong> reading, not the <em>for</em> one:
  <br /><br />
  <em>I sold him the car.</em> means <em>I sold the car <strong>to</strong> him</em> — <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>I sold the car <strong>for</strong> him</em> (on his behalf).
</Callout>

## C — Verbs that never reverse

Some verbs take two objects but **cannot** move the person into a **for / to** phrase at all. The double-object order is the only one available:

<SayIt text="We all envied him his lifestyle." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">but not</span> <em>We all envied his lifestyle for / to him.</em>)</p>

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-rose-200 bg-rose-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-rose-700">No <em>to / for</em> reorder — keep IO + DO together</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-rose-900">allow &middot; ask &middot; cost &middot; deny &middot; envy &middot; forgive &middot; guarantee &middot; permit &middot; refuse</p>
</div>

## D — Verbs that need a preposition to reach a second object

Some verbs — like **describe** and **fix** — are the mirror image of Section C: they can **only** take a second object if it is a **prepositional object**. They never take a bare indirect object.

**With *to*** (see also Unit 22A). Compare a verb that resists an IO with one that allows it:

<SayIt text="She described the situation to me." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">but not</span> <em>She described me the situation.</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="She told this joke to me." />
<SayIt text="She told me this joke." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>tell</em> is flexible — both orders are fine; <em>describe</em> is not.)</p>

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-sky-200 bg-sky-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-sky-700">Second object only as a prepositional object with <em>to</em></p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-sky-900">admit &middot; announce &middot; demonstrate &middot; describe &middot; explain &middot; introduce &middot; mention &middot; point out &middot; prove &middot; report &middot; say &middot; suggest</p>
</div>

**With *for***. The same restriction, but the preposition is **for**:

<SayIt text="He fixed the tap for me." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">but not</span> <em>He fixed me the tap.</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I booked a room for her." />
<SayIt text="I booked her a room." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>book</em> allows both orders; <em>fix / mend / repair</em> need the <em>for</em> phrase.)</p>

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-emerald-700">Second object only as a prepositional object with <em>for</em></p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-emerald-900">collect &middot; fix &middot; mend &middot; repair</p>
</div>

## The four patterns at a glance

| Pattern | Example verbs | What you can say |
|---|---|---|
| **Reorder with *to*** | give, lend, offer, show, teach | *gave him the glass* / *gave the glass **to** him* |
| **Reorder with *for*** | buy, cook, pour, save, make | *poured Ben a drink* / *poured a drink **for** Ben* |
| **Never reverses** | envy, allow, cost, ask, permit | *envied him his lifestyle* (only — no *to / for*) |
| **Needs a preposition** | describe, explain (*to*); fix, mend (*for*) | *described the situation **to** me* (no bare IO) |
