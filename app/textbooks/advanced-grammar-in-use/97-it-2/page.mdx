Unit 96 dealt with **it** as a *preparatory subject* — *It's no use crying*, *It seems that ...*. This unit looks at the other half of the picture: **it** as a **preparatory object** that points **forward** to a later clause, the *it + adjective/noun + clause* and *it + as* frames that let us comment on a situation, and the fixed pair **It is / was no ...** vs **There is / was no ...**. The recurring trap is knowing exactly **when** the little word **it** is required, optional, or wrong.

## A — `it` as the object pointing forward to a clause

We can use a pattern with **it** as the *object* of a verb, where **it** refers **forward** to a clause that comes later. After **can't bear, can't stand, hate, like, love** and **resent**, **it** can be followed directly by a **that-**, **if-** or **when-clause**; after **dislike, enjoy, prefer** and **understand**, by an **if-** or **when-clause**:

<SayIt text="I hate it that you can swim so well and I can't." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>not</em> I hate <span className="text-rose-600">that</span> you can swim so well ...)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="We always enjoy it when they stay with us." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>not</em> We always enjoy <span className="text-rose-600">when</span> they stay with us ...)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="Verbs that take it OR drop it">
  A second group can be followed by <strong>it</strong> + a <strong>that-clause</strong> (especially in spoken English), <em>or</em> directly by a <strong>that-clause</strong> with <strong>no it</strong> — including <strong>accept, admit, deny, guarantee</strong> and <strong>mention</strong>:<br /><br />
  <em>You've just got to <strong>accept (it)</strong> that Emil's gone and won't be coming back.</em>
</Callout>

But **many other verbs** that take a that-clause or wh-clause are **not** used with **it** in this way — among them **argue, discover, emphasise, notice, predict** and **remember**:

<SayIt text="I can't remember when I last saw her." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>not</em> I can't remember <span className="text-rose-600">it</span> when I last saw her.)</p>

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-rose-200 bg-rose-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-rose-700">No <em>it</em> here</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-rose-900">argue · discover · emphasise · notice · predict · remember</p>
  <p className="mt-2 text-sm text-rose-800">These take the bare clause: <em>I predict <strong>that</strong> she'll withdraw</em>, <em>I noticed <strong>that</strong> the door was open</em> — never with a forward-pointing <em>it</em>.</p>
</div>

## B — `it + adjective / noun phrase + clause`: how we see a situation

With other verbs we use to say **how we see** a particular event or situation, **it** is followed **first** by an **adjective or noun phrase** and **then** by a **that-clause**, a **to-infinitive clause**, or a clause beginning **when**. Verbs in this group include **believe, consider, feel, find** (= discover from experience) and **think**:

<SayIt text="Officials said they believe it unlikely that any lasting damage to the environment has been done." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>... believe it <strong>is</strong> unlikely that ...</em>; <em>not</em> <span className="text-rose-600">believe unlikely that ...</span>)</p>

With **leave** and **owe** (= have a responsibility to), the same **it** sets up a **to somebody + to-infinitive** pattern:

<SayIt text="Don't bother to arrange anything — just leave it to me to sort out." />
<br />
<SayIt text="She owed it to her parents to do well at college." />

<Callout type="tip" title="Why the it?">
  Here <strong>it</strong> is a placeholder: it fills the object slot so the heavy clause can move to the end, where English prefers its weight. <em>I find it hard to believe that he lied</em> reads far better than <em>I find to believe that he lied hard</em>.
</Callout>

## C — `accept / regard / see / take / view + it + as + noun (or adjective) + clause`

With the verbs **accept, regard, see, take** (= interpret something in a particular way) and **view**, we use **it + as + noun (or adjective) + clause**:

<SayIt text="We see it as an insult to have received no reply to our letter." />
<br />
<SayIt text="I take it as encouraging when students attend all my lectures." />

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-emerald-700">The frame</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-base font-semibold text-emerald-900">verb + <span className="rounded bg-white px-1">it</span> + <span className="rounded bg-white px-1">as</span> + noun / adjective + clause</p>
  <p className="mt-2 text-sm text-emerald-800"><em>I <strong>regard it as</strong> unacceptable <strong>for</strong> a modern company <strong>to</strong> exclude its workforce.</em> · <em>I <strong>take it as</strong> a great honour <strong>to</strong> be asked.</em></p>
</div>

## D — `It is / was no ...` vs `There is / was no ...`

A large set of fixed expressions begins with one of these two openers. They are **not** interchangeable — each noun keeps its own preposition or complement pattern.

<div className="not-prose my-6 overflow-hidden rounded-2xl border-2 border-violet-200 shadow-sm">
  <div className="grid grid-cols-2 text-sm">
    <div className="bg-violet-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-violet-900">It is / was no ...</div>
    <div className="bg-sky-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-sky-900">There is / was no ...</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>It's no secret</strong> that he wants a new job.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>There's no denying</strong> that he's intelligent.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 bg-violet-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>It's no use</strong> telling me now — I needed to know a week ago.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 bg-sky-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>There's no point in</strong> buying one unless you'll use it a lot.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>It's no good</strong> getting angry; that won't help.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>There's no need</strong> to explain how it works.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 bg-violet-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>It's no coincidence</strong> (or <strong>accident</strong>) that they left together.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 bg-sky-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>There's no question of</strong> agreeing to his demands.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>It's no longer</strong> necessary to have a visa to visit.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>There's no alternative</strong> (or <strong>choice</strong>) but to ask her to leave.</div>
  </div>
</div>

Other common members of the second family: **there's no hope of** getting more money, **there's no reason to** be pessimistic, **there's no chance of** finding a cure if we don't fund research.

<Callout type="warning" title="Fronting works for one family only">
  An <strong>It is / was no ...</strong> sentence can put its clause at the <strong>front</strong>:<br />
  <em>It's no secret that he wants a new job.</em> → <em>That he wants a new job is no secret.</em><br /><br />
  A <strong>There is / was no ...</strong> sentence <strong>cannot</strong>:<br />
  <em>There's no denying that he's intelligent.</em> → <span className="text-rose-600"><em>not</em> That he's intelligent there's no denying.</span>
</Callout>
