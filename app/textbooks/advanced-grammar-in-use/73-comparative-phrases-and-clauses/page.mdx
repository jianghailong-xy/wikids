Unit 72 gave you the *forms* — *bigger*, *most interesting*, *better*. This unit is about the **phrases and clauses** those forms live inside: the *as ... as* frame and its negatives, the odd-looking word order in *as normal a life as possible*, the quantity signals *as few as* and *as much as*, and the three ways English links a degree to a result — **enough**, **too** and **so**. Much of the difficulty here is not meaning but **word order**, so watch where the article *a / an* lands.

## A — as + adjective / adverb + as

We use **as + adjective / adverb + as** to say that something or someone is like something or someone else, or how they are similar:

<SayIt text="Was the film as funny as his last one?" />
<br />
<SayIt text="I came round as quickly as I could." />

Negative forms of sentences like this can use either **not as** or **not so**. In formal speech and writing it is more common to use **less + adjective + than**:

<SayIt text="The gap between the sides is not as wide as it was." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Also: <em>is not so wide as it was</em> — or, more formally, <em>is less wide than it was</em>.)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="not as / not so — both are available">
  In the <strong>negative</strong>, <em>not as ... as</em> and <em>not so ... as</em> are interchangeable. In the <strong>affirmative</strong>, only <em>as ... as</em> works: say <em>as wide as it was</em> — <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>so wide as it was</em>.
</Callout>

## B — as + adjective + a / an + noun + as

If we put a **singular countable noun** between an adjective and the second **as**, we use **a / an** in front of the noun:

<SayIt text="Despite his disability, he tried to lead as normal a life as possible." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>... as normal life as ...</em>)</p>

The negative form of sentences like this can use either **not as** or sometimes **not such**:

<SayIt text="It's not as quiet a place as it used to be." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Also: <em>It's not such a quiet place as it used to be.</em>)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="The article moves — this is the trap">
  The two patterns put <em>a / an</em> in <strong>different places</strong>:<br /><br />
  <strong>not as + adjective + a / an + noun</strong> → <em>not as quiet <u>a place</u></em><br />
  <strong>not such + a / an + adjective + noun</strong> → <em>not such <u>a quiet place</u></em><br /><br />
  So <em>not such a good player</em> and <em>not so good a player</em> are both fine — but <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>not such good player</em>.
</Callout>

## C — as much / many as, as little / few as

We also use **as much / many as** or **as little / few as** to say that a quantity or amount is **larger or smaller than expected**. **Many** and **few** are preferred before **numbers**; **much** and **little** are preferred with **amounts** (e.g. £5, 20%) and **distances** (e.g. 3 metres):

<SayIt text="There are a small number of people involved, possibly as few as twenty." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Prices have increased by as much as 300%." />

We can use **so, too** and **how** followed by an adjective in a similar way:

<SayIt text="It's not quite so straightforward a problem as it might at first seem." />
<br />
<SayIt text="How big a piece do you want?" />

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-sky-200 bg-sky-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-sky-700">Which word before which quantity?</p>
  <p className="mt-2 text-sm text-sky-900"><strong>as many as / as few as</strong> → countable numbers of things: <em>as many as 5,000 people</em>, <em>as few as twenty</em></p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm text-sky-900"><strong>as much as / as little as</strong> → sums, percentages, distances, periods: <em>as much as 25%</em>, <em>as little as £40</em>, <em>as much as 50 metres</em>, <em>as little as two weeks</em></p>
</div>

## D — not + adjective / adverb + enough + to-infinitive

We use **not + adjective / adverb + enough + to-infinitive** to mean that there isn't as much as is necessary to do something:

<SayIt text="I'm not tall enough to reach." />
<br />
<SayIt text="He didn't speak loudly enough to be heard." />

We can use **sufficiently** before adjectives to express a similar meaning to **enough**. **Sufficiently** is often preferred in more formal contexts:

<SayIt text="She didn't play sufficiently well to qualify." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Or: <em>... well enough to qualify.</em>)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="enough follows, sufficiently precedes — and never both">
  <strong>enough</strong> goes <em>after</em> the adjective or adverb (<em>bad enough</em>, <em>loudly enough</em>) — <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>enough bad</em>.<br />
  <strong>sufficiently</strong> goes <em>before</em> it (<em>sufficiently well</em>).<br /><br />
  They do the same job, so you use <strong>one or the other</strong>: <em>sufficiently well</em> <span className="text-rose-600">or</span> <em>well enough</em> — <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>sufficiently well enough</em>.
</Callout>

## E — too + adjective / adverb + to-infinitive

We use **too + adjective / adverb + to-infinitive** to mean 'more than necessary, possible, etc.' to do something:

<SayIt text="They arrived too late to get seats." />
<br />
<SayIt text="It moved too fast to see it clearly." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The suitcase was too small for him to get all his clothes in." />

In rather formal English we can use **too + adjective + a / an + noun**:

<SayIt text="I hope you don't have too tiring a day." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>... a too tiring day</em>. In a less formal style we might say <em>I hope your day hasn't been too tiring.</em>)</p>

## F — so + adjective / adverb + that-clause

We use **so + adjective / adverb + that-clause** to say that something existed or happened to such a degree that a **specified result** occurred (see also Unit 31):

<SayIt text="It's so simple that even I can do it." />
<br />
<SayIt text="He came in so quietly that I didn't hear him." />

Less often we can use **so + adjective / adverb + as + to-infinitive** with a similar meaning. Compare:

<SayIt text="The difference was so small that it wasn't worth arguing about." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The difference was so small as to not be worth arguing about." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= Because the difference was so small, it wasn't worth arguing about.)</p>

We can use **go so far as / as far as + to-infinitive** to talk about actions that are **surprising or extreme**:

<SayIt text="One furious woman went so far as to throw tomatoes at the minister." />

<Callout type="tip" title="go so far as TO — the to is not optional">
  The fixed phrase is <strong>go so far as to + verb</strong> (or <em>go as far as to + verb</em>): <em>I wouldn't go so far as to say he's dishonest.</em> Dropping the <em>to</em> — <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>go so far to say</em> — is one of the most common slips with this pattern.
</Callout>
