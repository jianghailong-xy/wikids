## A — Must / must not: rules, arrangements, and reminders

At advanced level the interesting thing about **must** is how much of its work has nothing to do with personal obligation. Three uses stand out.

**1. Formal rules, regulations, and warnings.** This is where written **must** and **must not** live — notices, contracts, official instructions:

<SayIt text="Bookings must be made at least seven days before departure." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The government must not be allowed to appoint judges." />

**2. Proposing a future arrangement (spoken).** In conversation we often use **must** and **mustn't** to float a vague plan — a meeting, a get-together — *without* committing to details:

<SayIt text="We must get together more often." />
<br />
<SayIt text="We mustn't leave it so long next time." />

**3. Reminding ourselves.** **I must …** is the note we leave for ourselves:

<SayIt text="I must charge my phone. I meant to do it yesterday, but I forgot." />

<Callout type="info" title="must vs have to — the speaker test">
  <strong>Must</strong> usually carries the speaker's own feeling or authority — a rule the speaker states, a plan the speaker proposes, a reminder to the speaker's own self. <strong>Have to</strong> tends to report an obligation that comes from <em>outside</em> the speaker (the law, a timetable, someone else). That difference is why <em>I must phone home</em> (I feel I should) ≠ <em>I have to phone home</em> (someone is making me).
</Callout>

## B — Drawing conclusions: the logical "must"

The biggest advanced use of **must** is **deduction** — saying *this is surely the case* because of the evidence. The form changes with the time and aspect of what you're concluding about.

**A completed action in the past** → **must + have + past participle:**

<SayIt text="That's not Clara's car. She must have borrowed it from her parents." />

**Something happening at or around the time of speaking** → **must be + -ing:**

<SayIt text="I can't hear a noise. You must be imagining things." />

**Something likely to happen in the future** → **must be going to** or **must be + -ing:**

<SayIt text="'What are all those workmen doing?' 'I think they must be going to dig up the road.'" />
<br />
<SayIt text="I was wrong about the meeting being today. It must be happening next Friday." />

**A present situation / state** → **must be**, or **have (got) to be** in informal speech:

<SayIt text="Their goalkeeper has got to be at least two metres tall! (or … must be …)" />

We can also conclude about **necessity**. Use **must have to** for a necessity you infer about the *present*, and **must have had to** for one you infer about the *past*:

<SayIt text="I can't access the database. You must have to put in a password." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= a password is surely necessary — a present necessity, inferred.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Matt wasn't at home when I went round. He must have had to go out unexpectedly." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= I conclude he was obliged to go out — a past necessity, inferred.)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="The contraction you can't make">
  You <strong>cannot</strong> say <em>must've (got) to</em> — there's no spoken contraction for the present-necessity conclusion. But <em>must've had to</em> <strong>is</strong> fine: <em>He must've had to go out.</em>
</Callout>

## C — Questions: have (got) to vs must

When a question **hopes for or expects a "no"**, we prefer **have (got) to**. **Must** is possible but feels formal:

<SayIt text="Do we have to answer all the questions? (or Have we got to …?; formally Must we …?)" />

We also use **have to** in questions that **imply a criticism** — a complaint dressed as a question. **Must** works too, though to some ears it sounds old-fashioned. We usually **stress** *have* and *must* here:

<SayIt text="Do you have to play your trumpet here? It's deafening! (formally Must you play …?)" />

<Callout type="tip" title="Reading the question's attitude">
  <em>Do we <strong>have to</strong> …?</em> leans towards <em>please say we don't</em>. <em><strong>Must</strong> you …?</em> with stress is the classic exasperated complaint. The grammar is carrying the speaker's mood, not just the information.
</Callout>

## D — have to vs have got to: when each one is required

Often **have to** and **have got to** are interchangeable — but four situations force your hand.

**1. With frequency adverbs** (always, never, normally, rarely, sometimes …) → use **have to:**

<SayIt text="I often have to work at the weekend to get everything done." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Not <em>I often have got to work …</em>)</p>

**2. In the past simple** — especially in questions and negatives → use **had to:**

<SayIt text="When did you have to give it back?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Not <em>When had you got to give it back?</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="We didn't have to wait too long for an answer." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Not <em>We hadn't got to wait too long …</em>)</p>

**3. If have is contracted** (I've, He's, It'd …) → you **must** include **got:**

<SayIt text="I need a new pair of shoes, and they've got to be blue." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Not <em>… they've to be blue.</em>)</p>

**4. With another modal verb** → use **have to**, never **have got to:**

<SayIt text="Motorists will have to wait until next year to use the bridge." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Not <em>Motorists will have got to wait …</em>)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="Register note">
  Outside those four constraints, <strong>have got to</strong> is often the more natural, informal choice in speech, while <strong>have to</strong> is the neutral default that fits anywhere. When in doubt — or in writing — <em>have to</em> never lets you down.
</Callout>

  Take one piece of evidence — a closed shop, an empty fridge, a missing colleague — and conclude about it in <em>three</em> times: past (<em>must have …</em>), around now (<em>must be …-ing</em>), and necessity (<em>must have to …</em>).
