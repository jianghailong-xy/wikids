Prepositions of time do two distinct jobs: they pin an event **inside** a period (*during the summer*), and they mark the **boundary** where something stops or must happen (*until 2:30 / by 2:30*). This unit is about getting those two jobs right — and about the traps that catch even advanced learners, like *until now* vs *so far*.

## A — During and in

We use **during** or **in** to talk about a period of time within which an event or activity occurs. The activity may continue for the **whole** of the period:

<SayIt text="I stayed at home during the summer." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or … in the summer.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The population of the city has actually fallen during the last decade." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or … in the last decade.)</p>

Or the event may happen **at some time**, or be repeated a number of times, **within** the period:

<SayIt text="We went to Turkey during the summer." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or … in the summer — the trip happened at some point, not all summer.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="He suffered a number of injuries during his career as a jockey." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or … in his career — repeated events across the period.)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="During, not in — before certain nouns">
  We use <strong>during</strong>, rather than <strong>in</strong>, to mean "at some time in the period of" before nouns such as <strong>illness, holiday, meal, stay, treatment</strong> and <strong>visit</strong>, when we refer to an event which lasts some time:<br /><br />
  ✅ <em>The President made the speech <strong>during</strong> a visit to Madrid.</em><br />
  ❌ <em>… <strong>in</strong> a visit to Madrid.</em>
</Callout>

We also use **during** with the phrase **the whole (of)**, emphasising the duration of an entire period:

<SayIt text="No one was allowed to leave the ship during the whole of its time in port." />

## B — Throughout, over vs during

We can use **throughout** to emphasise that something happens over the **whole** of a period of time:

<SayIt text="We had enough firewood to keep us warm throughout the winter." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or … warm during / through the winter.)</p>

We can use **over** or **during** when we talk about something that goes on **for a length of time** within a period, either for some of that period or for the whole of it:

<SayIt text="Weather conditions have been improving over the past few days." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or … during the past few days.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I hit my head and can't remember anything that happened over the next hour or so." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or … during the next hour or so.)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="Short events → during, not over">
  If we talk about a <strong>short event</strong> that happens within a period of time, we prefer <strong>during</strong>:<br /><br />
  ✅ <em>She sneezed <strong>during</strong> the performance.</em> (= once or a few times)<br />
  ❌ <em>… <strong>over</strong> the performance.</em><br /><br />
  ✅ <em><strong>During</strong> a pause in the conversation, she left the room.</em><br />
  ❌ <em><strong>Over</strong> a pause in the conversation.</em>
</Callout>

  </div>
</div>

## C — Until and by

There are a number of ways of saying when something that has continued for some time **stops**. We use **until** (or informally **till**) to say that something continued or will continue **to** a particular time, and **by** to say that something happened or will happen either **before** a particular time or **at that time at the latest**. Compare:

<SayIt text="We have to be at home until 2:30." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= We must not leave home before 2:30 — the staying continues up to 2:30.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="We have to be at home by 2:30." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= We must arrive home either before or at 2:30 — 2:30 is the deadline.)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="The core distinction">
  <strong>Until</strong> answers "how long does it last?" — it looks at the <em>duration</em> leading up to the point.<br />
  <strong>By</strong> answers "when is the deadline?" — it looks at the <em>point</em> itself as a limit.
</Callout>

## D — Up to, until now vs so far / to date

In informal contexts we can use **up to** or **up till** instead of *until*. We commonly use **up to / up till** with **now** and with **then**:

<SayIt text="I've just bought a tablet computer. I've always used a laptop up to now." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or … up till now.)</p>

We can use **until now** to talk about a situation that will **not continue beyond** now:

<SayIt text="Supermarkets say that until now there has been little consumer interest in buying organic produce." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= The situation has changed or is going to change.)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="The until now trap">
  We do <strong>NOT</strong> use <em>until now</em> for a situation that will or may continue into the future. Instead we use <strong>so far</strong> or, in formal contexts, <strong>to date</strong>:<br /><br />
  ✅ <em>It was certainly the best match of the football season <strong>so far</strong>.</em><br />
  ❌ <em>… <strong>until now</strong>.</em> (The season isn't over — more matches are coming.)<br /><br />
  ✅ <em>When the contract is signed it will be the building company's biggest order <strong>to date</strong>.</em>
</Callout>

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-amber-200 bg-amber-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-amber-700">Quick test</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm text-amber-900">Ask yourself: <strong>Has the situation definitely changed?</strong> If yes → <em>until now</em> is fine. If the situation is still ongoing → use <em>so far</em> or <em>to date</em>.</p>
</div>
