When we report what people think or what they have said, we often give the information using our own words. We do this with sentences that have a **reporting clause** and a **reported clause** (see also Units 33–39). This unit lays out the three essential patterns: how to structure reported statements, where to place the negative, and how to turn direct questions into reported ones — all without losing the speaker's original meaning.

## A — Quoting and reporting in our own words

The basic architecture of a reported sentence is two clauses: a reporting clause (who said it) and a reported clause (what they said):

<SayIt text="She explained (that) she couldn't take the job until January." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">Reporting clause <strong>+</strong> reported clause — <em>that</em> is optional here.</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="He didn't tell me where to put the boxes." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">The reported clause can also be a <em>wh-</em> or <em>to</em>-infinitive clause.</p>

If the **exact words** are important, we might report the actual words someone said. In writing this is done in a **quotation**:

<SayIt text="'I suppose you've heard the latest news,' she said to me." />
<br />
<SayIt text="'Of course,' Carter replied, 'you'll have to pay him to do the job.'" />

The reporting clause can come **before, within, or at the end** of the quotation. In the English used in stories and novels, the reporting verb (e.g. *ask, continue*) is often placed **before the subject** when the reporting clause comes **after** the quotation — except when the subject is a pronoun:

<SayIt text="'When will you be back?' asked Jimin." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Or: …<em>Jimin asked.</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="'And after that I moved to Italy,' she continued." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Not: …<em>continued she.</em> — pronoun subjects stay before the verb.)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="Inversion after quotations">
  Inversion (<em>asked Jimin</em>) is a feature of narrative prose — it lends a literary feel. In everyday speech and email, stick to the normal <em>Jimin asked</em> order. And with pronoun subjects (<em>he, she, it</em>), inversion is ungrammatical: <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>said she</em> but <em>she said</em>.
</Callout>

## B — Negatives in reporting

To report what somebody **didn't** say or think, we make the **reporting verb** negative:

<SayIt text="He didn't tell me how he would get to London." />

If we want to report a **negative sentence**, then we usually report this in the **reported clause**:

<SayIt text="'You're right, it isn't a good idea.' → He agreed that it wasn't a good idea." />

However, it may be reported in the **reporting clause** — depending on meaning:

<SayIt text="'I disagree. It's not a good idea at all.' → He didn't agree that it was a good idea." />

<Callout type="warning" title="Neg-raising: when the negative climbs up">
  With a specific set of verbs — <strong>believe, expect, feel, intend, plan, propose, suppose, think</strong> and <strong>want</strong> — to report a negative sentence we <strong>usually</strong> make the verb in the <strong>reporting clause</strong> negative, even when the original speaker negated the reported clause. This is called <em>neg-raising</em>:
  <br /><br />
  <em>'I expect he won't come.'</em> / <em>'I don't expect he will come.'</em> → <strong>She didn't expect him to come.</strong>
  <br /><br />
  The negative migrates upward to the reporting verb — and native speakers do this without thinking.
</Callout>

## C — Reporting questions

To report a **wh-question** we use a reporting clause and a clause with a **wh-word**:

<SayIt text="She asked me what the problem was." />
<br />
<SayIt text="I asked him where to go next." />

When we report a **yes / no question** we use a reporting clause followed by a clause beginning with either **if** or **whether**:

<SayIt text="Liz wanted to know if / whether we had any photos of our holiday." />

<Callout type="warning" title="if + to-infinitive is forbidden">
  You can say <em>I wondered whether to go</em>, but <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>I wondered if to go</em>. After <strong>if</strong> you need a full clause: <em>I wondered if I should go.</em>
</Callout>

The usual word order in a *wh-*, *if-*, or *whether*-clause is the one we would use in a **statement** — not a question:

<SayIt text="'Have you seen Paul recently?' → She wanted to know if I had seen Paul recently." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> …<em>if had I seen Paul recently.</em>)</p>

However, if the original question begins **what, which, or who** followed by **be + complement**, we can put the complement **before or after** *be* in the report:

<SayIt text="'Who was the winner?' → I asked who the winner was." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Or: …<em>who was the winner.</em>)</p>

Note that we **don't use a form of *do*** in the *wh-*, *if-*, or *whether*-clause:

<SayIt text="She asked me where I found it." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> …<em>where did I find it.</em> / <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> …<em>where I did find it.</em>)</p>

However, if we are reporting a **negative question**, we **can** use a negative form of *do*:

<SayIt text="He asked (me) why I didn't want anything to eat." />
