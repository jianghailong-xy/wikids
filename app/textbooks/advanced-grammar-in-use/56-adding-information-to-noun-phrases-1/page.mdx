A relative clause (*the writer who lives next door*) is only one way of packing extra information into a noun phrase. This unit gathers the **other** ways — placing a second noun phrase beside the first (*apposition*), joining descriptions with *and* / *or*, sharpening a vague phrase with *namely* and *that is*, and trimming a relative clause down to a bare **participle** or **to-infinitive** clause. Used well, they let you say more in fewer words — which is exactly why newspapers lean on them so heavily.

## A — A second noun phrase (apposition)

We sometimes add information about a person or thing referred to in one noun phrase by talking about the same person or thing in a different way in a following noun phrase:

<SayIt text="A hooded cobra, one of the world's most dangerous snakes, has escaped from Dudley Zoo." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Dr Alex Parr, director of the State Museum, is to become the government's arts adviser." />

In **writing**, the two items are usually separated by a **comma** (one on each side), and in **speech** they are separated by a pause or other intonation break.

<Callout type="warning" title="When the second item is a name — no comma">
  When the second noun phrase acts like a <strong>defining</strong> relative clause — picking out <em>which</em> one, and usually a name — there is normally <strong>no</strong> punctuation in writing and no intonation break in speech:
  <br /><br />
  ✅ <em>My friend Mia has moved to Sweden.</em> &nbsp;(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>My friend, Mia, ...</em>)<br />
  ✅ <em>The current champion is expected to survive her first-round match with the Italian Silvia Farina.</em> &nbsp;(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>... the Italian, Silvia Farina.</em>)
</Callout>

## B — Joining the information with *and* or *or*

We can add information to a noun phrase with a conjunction such as **and** or **or**:

<SayIt text="Kurt Svensson, her teacher and well-known concert pianist, thinks that she has great talent." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= Her teacher is <em>also</em> a well-known concert pianist — one person, two descriptions joined by <em>and</em>.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Phonetics, or the study of speech sounds, is a common component on courses in teaching English as a foreign language." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>or</em> introduces another name for the same thing — *X, or Y, ...*)</p>

## C — Spelling it out with *namely* and *that is*

The adverb **namely** and the phrase **that is** are used to add specific details about a noun phrase — they take a general phrase and pin it down to the exact item(s) meant:

<SayIt text="This side effect of the treatment, namely weight gain, is counteracted with other drugs." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The main cause of global warming, that is the burning of fossil fuels, is the focus of negotiations at the international conference." />

<Callout type="tip" title="namely vs that is">
  Both fence off the detail with commas. Use <strong>namely</strong> to name the specific member(s) of a category (<em>the two countries having borders with the USA, namely Mexico and Canada</em>); use <strong>that is</strong> (often written <em>i.e.</em>) to restate or clarify (<em>... that is, the burning of fossil fuels</em>).
</Callout>

## D — Participle and to-infinitive clauses

We can also add information to a noun phrase using a **participle clause** beginning with an **-ing**, **-ed** or **being + -ed** form. These are often similar to **defining** relative clauses:

<SayIt text="The people living next door come from Italy." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= The people <em>who are living</em> next door ...)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The weapon used in the murder has now been found." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= The weapon <em>that was used</em> ...)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The prisoners being released are all women." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= The prisoners <em>who are being released</em> ...)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="The -ing / -ed split is active vs passive">
  <strong>-ing</strong> participle clauses correspond to relative clauses with an <strong>active</strong> verb (<em>rivers <u>flowing</u> = which flow</em>).<br />
  <strong>-ed</strong> and <strong>being + -ed</strong> correspond to relative clauses with a <strong>passive</strong> verb (<em>the deer <u>introduced</u> = which were introduced</em>; <em>booklets <u>being printed</u> = which are being printed</em>).
</Callout>

We can also use a **to-infinitive clause**:

<SayIt text="Have you brought a book to read?" />
<br />
<SayIt text="My decision to resign from the company was made after a great deal of thought." />
<br />
<SayIt text="I thought that the management's offer, to increase staff holidays, was a good one." />

## E — -ing / -ed clauses for *non-defining* relative clauses

In written English, particularly in **newspapers**, **-ing** and **-ed** clauses are also used instead of **non-defining** relative clauses. These are usually written between **commas** or **dashes (–)**:

<SayIt text="The men, wearing anoraks and hats, made off in a stolen Volvo estate." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= The men, <em>who were wearing</em> anoraks and hats, ...)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The proposals – expected to be agreed by ministers – are less radical than many employers had feared." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= The proposals, <em>which are expected</em> to be agreed ...)</p>
