Most adjectives lead a double life: they sit happily **before** a noun (*a **high** price* — the *attributive* position) and equally happily **after** a linking verb (*the price is **high*** — the *predicative* position). This unit is about the adjectives that don't. Some refuse to stand before a noun, some refuse to follow a linking verb, and a small set change their meaning depending on which side of the noun they land. Knowing where each type can go is a large part of sounding natural in advanced English.

## A — Before the noun, or after a linking verb

Many adjectives can be used **either** before the noun they describe **or** following linking verbs such as **appear, be, become, feel, get** and **seem** (see Unit 21). Compare:

<SayIt text="The high price surprised him." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>high</em> before the noun <em>price</em> — attributive.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The price seemed high." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>high</em> after the linking verb <em>seemed</em> — predicative.)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="The two positions have names">
  <strong>Attributive</strong> = before the noun (<em>a <strong>high</strong> price</em>). <strong>Predicative</strong> = after a linking verb (<em>the price <strong>seemed</strong> high</em>). The rest of this unit is a tour of the adjectives that are allowed only one of these positions.
</Callout>

## B — Adjectives seldom or never used before the noun

Some adjectives are **seldom or never** used before the noun they describe.

**Some 'a-' adjectives.** The horse was **alone** — but *not* <span className="text-rose-600">the alone horse</span>.

<SayIt text="The horse was alone in the field." />

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-emerald-700">Also, predicative only</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-emerald-900">afraid · alike · alive · ashamed · asleep · awake · aware</p>
</div>

Several of these have a **related adjective** that *can* go before a noun. Reach for that one when you need the attributive position:

<SayIt text="The animal was alive. / A living animal. / A live animal." />

<div className="not-prose my-6 overflow-hidden rounded-2xl border-2 border-emerald-200 shadow-sm">
  <div className="grid grid-cols-2 text-sm">
    <div className="bg-emerald-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-emerald-900">Predicative only</div>
    <div className="bg-teal-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-teal-900">Use this one before a noun</div>
    <div className="border-t border-emerald-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">afraid</div>
    <div className="border-t border-teal-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">frightened</div>
    <div className="border-t border-emerald-100 bg-emerald-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">alike</div>
    <div className="border-t border-teal-100 bg-teal-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">similar</div>
    <div className="border-t border-emerald-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">alone</div>
    <div className="border-t border-teal-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">lone</div>
    <div className="border-t border-emerald-100 bg-emerald-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">asleep</div>
    <div className="border-t border-teal-100 bg-teal-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">sleeping</div>
  </div>
</div>

**Some adjectives describing health and feelings.** My son felt **unwell** — but *not* <span className="text-rose-600">my unwell son</span>.

<SayIt text="My son felt unwell." />

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-emerald-700">Also, predicative only</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-emerald-900">content · fine · glad · ill · sorry · (un)sure · upset · well</p>
</div>

<Callout type="warning" title="Watch the fixed exceptions">
  A few of these words <em>do</em> appear before a noun — but in set phrases or with a shifted meaning: <em>ill health</em>, an <em>upset stomach</em>, <em>He's really not a <strong>well</strong> man</em>. And a predicative-only adjective can slip in between an <strong>adverb and a noun</strong>: <em>a terminally <strong>ill</strong> patient</em>, <em>a seriously <strong>ill</strong> child</em>.
</Callout>

## C — Emphasising, classifying and qualitative adjectives

**Emphasising adjectives** stress how you feel about something. They go before the noun and are seldom or never used after a linking verb:

<SayIt text="I felt a complete fool." />
<br />
<SayIt text="It was a total failure." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not usually</span> <em>The failure was total.</em>)</p>

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-violet-200 bg-violet-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-violet-700">Emphasising — before the noun only</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-violet-900">absolute · complete · entire · mere · sheer · total · utter</p>
</div>

**Classifying adjectives** say that something is of a particular *type*. Like emphasising adjectives, they are seldom or never used after a linking verb:

<SayIt text="a nuclear explosion" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not usually</span> <em>The explosion was nuclear</em> — unless you are deliberately contrasting it with other kinds of explosion.)</p>

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-violet-200 bg-violet-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-violet-700">Classifying — before the noun only</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-violet-900">atomic · chemical · digital · domestic · environmental · medical · general · occasional · northern (etc.) · maximum · minimum · underlying</p>
</div>

**Qualitative adjectives** give a quality that a thing or person has. These are the flexible ones — use them **either** before a noun **or** after a linking verb:

<SayIt text="a beautiful sunset" />
<br />
<SayIt text="The sunset was beautiful." />

<Callout type="tip" title="Some classifiers moonlight as qualitatives">
  A number of classifying adjectives take on a <strong>different, qualitative meaning</strong> when moved after a linking verb. Compare the country's <strong>economic</strong> reforms (classifying = to do with the economy) with <em>The process isn't <strong>economic</strong></em> (qualitative = not profitable). The same double life is led by <strong>academic, conscious, educational, (il)legal</strong> and <strong>scientific</strong>: <em>a <strong>legal</strong> argument</em> (about the law) vs <em>It's completely <strong>legal</strong></em> (= allowed).
</Callout>

## D — Adjectives immediately after the noun

Many adjectives can also stand **immediately after** a noun, at the start of a *reduced relative clause* (see Unit 69B).

**Before a to-infinitive or a prepositional phrase** that completes the adjective phrase:

<SayIt text="It was a speech calculated to appeal to the unions." />
<br />
<SayIt text="He is a manager capable of making difficult decisions." />

**Some -ible and -able adjectives** — *available, imaginable, possible, suitable*. These follow the noun **only** when the noun is preceded by **the**, or is made definite by what comes after it:

<SayIt text="This was the most difficult decision imaginable." />
<br />
<SayIt text="It is a treatment suitable for all children with asthma." />

**The adjectives *concerned, involved, opposite, present, proper, responsible*.** These carry **different meanings** before a noun and immediately after it:

<SayIt text="All the people present approved of the decision." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>present</em> after the noun = who were there.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I was asked for my present address." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>present</em> before the noun = current, my address now.)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="Meaning flips with position">
  <em>the people <strong>concerned</strong></em> = the people involved &nbsp;·&nbsp; <em><strong>concerned</strong> parents</em> = worried parents<br />
  <em>the minister <strong>responsible</strong></em> = in charge &nbsp;·&nbsp; <em>a <strong>responsible</strong> adult</em> = sensible, trustworthy<br />
  <em>the town <strong>proper</strong></em> = the town itself &nbsp;·&nbsp; <em>the <strong>proper</strong> procedure</em> = the correct one
</Callout>
