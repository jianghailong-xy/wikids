Some verbs lock onto a particular preposition, and swapping that preposition changes what the sentence means — sometimes slightly, sometimes completely. *I agree with him*, *we agreed on a time* and *the government agreed to the scheme* are three different claims, not three ways of saying one thing. This unit takes seven very common verbs — **agree, argue, ask, care, know, learn, talk** — and sorts out which prepositions each takes, and what each combination actually says.

## B — What each preposition contributes

### *about* — concerning a particular thing

**about** usually means 'concerning a particular thing':

<SayIt text="They began to learn about nutrition when they were at primary school." />

We use **care about** to talk about something we are (not) concerned about:

<SayIt text="He doesn't seem to care about the effect a poor diet has on him." />

### *for* — what people want, and looking after

**for** is used with **ask** to talk about what people want:

<SayIt text="He finished the drink quickly and asked for another." />

with **argue** when we talk about giving reasons why something is true or right:

<SayIt text="Many people are arguing for a big tax cut." />

and with **care** to talk about doing the necessary things for someone or something in order to keep them in good health or condition:

<SayIt text="Jo cared for her disabled mother until her death last year." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>Jo took care of</em> her disabled mother …)</p>

**care for** also means 'like', particularly in negative sentences, and 'want' in offers. Both of these uses are rather formal:

<SayIt text="I don't care for the theatre much." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Would you care for a cup of coffee?" />

<Callout type="warning" title="care about vs care for">
  <strong>care about</strong> = be concerned about something (<em>Does the government care about nursery education?</em>).<br />
  <strong>care for</strong> = look after someone, or — formally — like / want (<em>She cared for her mother</em>; <em>I don't care for opera</em>).<br /><br />
  And note that <em>look after</em> takes <strong>after</strong>, but <em>care</em> never does: <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>I had to care after my parents</em>.
</Callout>

### *of* — discussing, having or getting information

**of** is used with **talk**, **know** and **learn** to talk about discussing, having or getting information:

<SayIt text="Mira went recently to Laos and can talk of nothing else." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or less formally … <em>talk about</em> …)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The whole country knew of Churchill's love of cigars." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or less formally … <em>knew about</em> …)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I have just learnt of the death of Dr Ramirez." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or less formally … <em>learnt about</em> …)</p>

We use **ask of** when we make or talk about requests:

<SayIt text="I have a favour to ask of you and your sister." />

<Callout type="tip" title="of is the formal twin of about">
  With <em>talk, know</em> and <em>learn</em>, <strong>of</strong> and <strong>about</strong> are usually both possible — <strong>of</strong> is simply the more formal choice. So in a gap-fill, both may be right. <em>ask of</em>, however, is not interchangeable: it belongs only to requests (<em>a lot to ask of you</em>).
</Callout>

### *on* — concerned with a particular topic

**on** is used with **talk** and **agree** to mean 'concerned with a particular topic':

<SayIt text="I was asked to talk on my research." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or … <em>to talk about</em> …)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="We agreed on a time to meet." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Usually there has been previous discussion or disagreement.)</p>

Note that we use **agree to** to say that someone allows something to happen:

<SayIt text="Once the government agreed to the scheme it went ahead without delay." />

### *with* — the person involved, and having the same opinion

**with** is used with **argue** and **talk** when we go on to mention the person involved:

<SayIt text="I used to argue / talk with Pedro for hours." />

We use **agree with** to say that two people have the same opinion:

<SayIt text="Adam thinks we should accept the offer, and I agree with him." />

and to say that we approve of a particular idea or action:

<SayIt text="I agree with letting children choose the clothes they wear." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>I agree about / on</em> …)</p>

or to say that two descriptions are the same:

<SayIt text="Stefan's story agreed with that of his son." />

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-violet-200 bg-violet-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-violet-700">The four faces of <em>agree</em></p>
  <p className="mt-2 text-sm text-violet-900"><strong>agree with</strong> someone — you share their opinion · <strong>agree with</strong> an idea — you approve of it · <strong>agree on</strong> something — you reach a decision after discussion · <strong>agree to</strong> something — you permit it to go ahead.</p>
</div>

Pick any three verbs and ask, for each preposition it <em>doesn't</em> take, what an English speaker says instead — <em>care</em> has no <em>of</em>, but <em>take care of</em> covers it; <em>ask</em> has no <em>with</em>, but <em>talk with</em> does the job. Keeping a two-column list of the pattern and its meaning beats memorising the grid: <em>agree on</em> and <em>agree to</em> are only a letter apart and a decision apart.
</Callout>
