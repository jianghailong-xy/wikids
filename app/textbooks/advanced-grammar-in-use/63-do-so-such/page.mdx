## A — Do so: standing in for a whole verb phrase

We use **do so** (and its other forms — **does so, did so, doing so, done so**) instead of repeating a verb phrase — the verb together with whatever follows it to complete its meaning — when the context already makes clear what we are talking about.

<SayIt text="She won in 2012 and seems likely to do so again this year." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>do so</em> = win)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Dr Lawson said, 'Sit down.' Katia did so, and explained her problem." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>did so</em> = sat down)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The climbers will try again today to reach the summit. Their chances of doing so are better than last week." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>doing so</em> = reaching the summit — common after a preposition)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="When he was asked to check the figures, he claimed that he had already done so." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>done so</em> = checked the figures)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="Do so is formal — do it / do that are the everyday versions">
  <strong>Do so</strong> belongs mainly to formal spoken and written English. In informal English we usually prefer <strong>do it</strong> or <strong>do that</strong>: <br />
  • Mrs Chen waved as she walked past. She <strong>does so / does it / does that</strong> every morning. <br />
  • Ricardo told me to put in a new battery. I <strong>did so / did it / did that</strong>, but the radio still doesn't work.
</Callout>

In less formal English — especially after a modal or a perfect tense — we can drop **so** and leave **do** on its own:

<SayIt text="'Will this program work on my computer?' 'It should do.'" />
<br />
<SayIt text="I told you that I'd finish the work by today, and I have done." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>have</em> is stressed here)</p>

## B — Do so for actions; plain do for states and habits

We can use **do so** to replace verbs that describe **actions**. But we **avoid do so** with verbs that describe **states** or **habitual** behaviour — there we use **do** (or **does / did**) on its own. Compare:

<div className="not-prose my-6 overflow-hidden rounded-2xl border-2 border-violet-200 shadow-sm">
  <div className="grid grid-cols-2 text-sm">
    <div className="bg-violet-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-violet-900">Action → do so is fine</div>
    <div className="bg-sky-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-sky-900">State / habit → plain do only</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">65% voted for Katie Brown this time, whereas 84% <strong>did so</strong> last year.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">Lars doesn't like Facebook, but Emma <strong>does</strong>. <span className="text-slate-400">(not <em>does so</em> — like is a state)</span></div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 bg-violet-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">He confessed, although he only <strong>did so</strong> after witnesses identified him.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 bg-sky-50/50 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">He earned a lot more than I <strong>did</strong>. <span className="text-slate-400">(not <em>did so</em>)</span></div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">I gave her the medicine, and I take full responsibility for <strong>doing so</strong>.</div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700">I don't have time to go swimming every day, but I usually <strong>do</strong>. <span className="text-slate-400">(habit)</span></div>
  </div>
</div>

<Callout type="warning" title="The classic slip">
  ✅ Lars doesn't like Facebook, but Emma <strong>does</strong>. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ … but Emma does so. <br />
  ✅ He earned a lot more than I <strong>did</strong>. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ … than I did so. <br />
  ✅ I usually <strong>do</strong>. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ I usually do so. <br />
  The test: is the original verb a one-off <em>action</em> (vote, confess, check) or a settled <em>state / habit</em> (like, earn more, usually swim)? Only actions take <em>do so</em>.
</Callout>

## C — Such and such a / an: pointing back to 'this kind'

We use **such (+ a / an) + noun** to refer back to something mentioned before, with the meaning **'of this / that kind'**. The choice depends on the noun:

<div className="not-prose my-6 grid gap-3 sm:grid-cols-2">
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-emerald-700">uncountable or plural noun</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-base font-semibold text-emerald-900">such + noun</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm text-emerald-800">such behaviour · such plans · such research</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-sky-200 bg-sky-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-sky-700">countable, singular noun</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-base font-semibold text-sky-900">such + a / an + noun</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm text-sky-800">such a person · such a scheme · such a device</p>
  </div>
</div>

<SayIt text="The students criticise teachers for badly organised lessons. Such behaviour is unacceptable in most schools." />
<br />
<SayIt text="There were rumours of job cuts, but the spokeswoman said, 'I know of no such plans.'" />
<br />
<SayIt text="They needed an excellent administrator and manager. Such a person was not easy to find." />
<br />
<SayIt text="We let parents take time off to look after children. We were the first department to introduce such a scheme." />

<Callout type="tip" title="Such is formal — 'like this' is the everyday equivalent">
  This pointing-back <strong>such</strong> belongs to formal speech and writing. In informal English we usually say <strong>… like this / like that</strong> or <strong>… of this / that kind</strong> instead: <br />
  • Such behaviour is unacceptable. → more informally: <em>Behaviour like this is unacceptable.</em> <br />
  • I know of no such plans. → more informally: <em>I know of no plans of this kind.</em> <br />
  • Such a person was not easy to find. → more informally: <em>A person like this was not easy to find.</em>
</Callout>

<Callout type="info" title="Don't confuse the two 'such' patterns">
  This unit is about <strong>such = of this kind</strong> (pointing back). That is different from the <strong>such … that</strong> of result (<em>It was such a long film that we left early</em>), which belongs with <em>so … that</em> in Unit 81. Same word, different job.
</Callout>
