Two small families of adverbs do delicate jobs. **Degree adverbs** turn the dial — they tell us *how* extreme, complete or intense something is (*extremely happy, almost always, very much admired*). **Focus adverbs** aim a spotlight — they pick out the part of the sentence that matters most, either sharpening it (*especially, particularly*) or fencing it off (*only, even, just*). This unit is mostly about the fiddly bits: where they sit, and the famous traps around **much / very much**, **very / too**, and **even / only**.

## A — What degree and focus adverbs do (and where they sit)

**Degree adverbs** go before adjectives, verbs or other adverbs to give information about the **extent or level** of something:

<SayIt text="They're extremely happy." />
<br />
<SayIt text="I really hate coffee." />
<br />
<SayIt text="He almost always arrived late." />

Their position depends on the adverb. Some — **almost, largely, really, virtually** — usually go **before** the main verb; others — **altogether, enormously, somewhat** — usually go **after** it. Degree adverbs are rarely used in front position.

  <div className="grid grid-cols-2 text-sm">
    <div className="bg-violet-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-violet-900">Usually before the main verb</div>
    <div className="bg-sky-100 px-4 py-2 font-bold text-sky-900">Usually after the main verb</div>
    <div className="border-t border-violet-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>almost · largely · really · virtually</strong><br /><em>I really hate coffee.</em></div>
    <div className="border-t border-sky-100 px-4 py-3 text-slate-700"><strong>altogether · enormously · somewhat</strong><br /><em>The result puzzled me somewhat.</em></div>
  </div>
</div>

**Focus adverbs** draw attention to the most important part of what we are talking about. Some make what we say **more specific**; others **limit** it to one thing or person:

<SayIt text="There is likely to be snow today, particularly in the north." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>especially, even, mainly, mostly, particularly, specifically</em> sharpen the focus.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Many people offered to help me invest the money, but I only trusted Rick." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>alone, just, only, simply, solely</em> limit it to one thing or person.)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="Two kinds of focus">
  <strong>Sharpening</strong> adverbs (<em>especially, particularly, mainly</em>) say <em>this part above all</em>. <strong>Limiting</strong> adverbs (<em>only, just, solely, alone</em>) say <em>this part and nothing more</em>. Both are about where you point the listener's attention — Sections B–D below are the precision controls.
</Callout>

## B — much and very much

In affirmative sentences in **formal** contexts, **much** can be a degree adverb before the verbs **admire, appreciate, enjoy, prefer** and **regret**, to emphasise how we feel:

<SayIt text="I much enjoyed having you stay with us." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Their music is much admired." />

This **much** is used particularly after **I** and **we**, and we don't normally use it in questions (*not* <em>Did you much enjoy …?</em>).

We can use **very much** in a similar way before those verbs, and also before **agree, doubt, fear, hope, like** and **want**. Crucially, we **do not** use **much** alone before this second group:

<SayIt text="I much prefer seeing films at the cinema than on DVD." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>or</em> I very much prefer … — <em>prefer</em> takes either.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="We very much agree with the decision." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>or</em> We agree very much …; <span className="text-rose-600">but not</span> … much agree … — <em>agree</em> takes only <strong>very much</strong>.)</p>

We can also use **much** or **very much** before a **past participle that is part of a passive**:

<SayIt text="The new by-pass was very much needed." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>or</em> The new by-pass was much needed.)</p>

But before a **past participle adjective** we use **(very) much** and **not much** on its own; and before a **present participle adjective** we use **neither**:

<SayIt text="I was very much surprised by her news." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>or</em> I was surprised …; <span className="text-rose-600">but not</span> I was <em>much</em> surprised … — past participle adjective.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The hotel was very welcoming." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">but not</span> The hotel was (very) <em>much</em> welcoming — present participle adjective.)</p>

In **negative** sentences in informal contexts we can use **(very) much** before verbs such as **appreciate, enjoy, like** and **look forward to** to stress a negative feeling:

<SayIt text="I didn't very much enjoy the film." />

<Callout type="warning" title="The much / very much decision tree">
  <strong>much</strong> alone → only the formal affirmative group (<em>admire, appreciate, enjoy, prefer, regret</em>) and passive past participles (<em>much needed</em>).<br /><br />
  <strong>very much</strong> → both groups of verbs and passive past participles, plus past-participle <em>adjectives</em> (<em>very much surprised</em>).<br /><br />
  <strong>neither much nor very much</strong> → before a <em>present-participle</em> adjective: use plain <strong>very</strong> (<em>very welcoming</em>, <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>very much welcoming</em>).
</Callout>

## C — very and too

Before an adjective or another adverb, use **very** when you mean **'to a high degree'**, and **too** when you mean **'more than enough'** or **'more than is wanted or needed'**. They are not interchangeable:

<SayIt text="The weather was very hot in Majorca — perfect for swimming." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> … too hot … — the heat was welcome.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="It's too hot to stay in this room — let's find somewhere cooler." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> … very hot … — the heat is a problem.)</p>

In negative sentences in informal spoken English we can use **not too** to mean **'not very'**:

<SayIt text="I'm not too bothered about who wins." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>or</em> I'm not very bothered …)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="The follow-on test">
  <strong>too</strong> very often leads into a consequence — <em>too … to do something</em> or <em>too … for somebody</em>: <em>too weak to walk, too narrow for the coach</em>. If a <em>to-</em>infinitive or <em>for-</em>phrase of result is coming, you almost certainly want <strong>too</strong>, not <strong>very</strong>.
</Callout>

## D — even and only

**Even** and **only** usually go in **mid position**, but if they refer to the **subject** they come **before** it. The position changes the meaning, so it has to be chosen carefully:

<SayIt text="My mother has only brought some food." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= she hasn't brought anything else — <em>only</em> focuses on <em>some food</em>.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Only my mother has brought some food." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= my mother and nobody else — <em>only</em> focuses on the subject.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Aya can even speak French." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= in addition to everything else she can do.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Even Aya can speak French." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= you might not expect her to — focus on the subject. Rather than <em>Aya even can speak French</em>.)</p>
