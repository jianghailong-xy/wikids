Unit 83 introduced the standard real and unreal conditional patterns with **if**. This unit drills deeper — four advanced patterns that give your conditionals a more formal, precise or vivid edge: **inversion** (dropping *if*), **if … will** (where it is actually allowed), **if … happen to / should** (for remote possibilities), and **as if / as though** (for comparisons that explain or pretend).

## A — If … should / were / had: inversion

When the first verb in a conditional *if*-clause is **should**, **were** or **had**, we can leave out *if* and put that verb at the start of the clause. This is particularly common in **formal or literary English**, and only in **hypothetical conditionals** — the kind that answer "What would happen if …?":

<SayIt text="Should any of this cost you anything, send me the bill." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= If any of this should cost you anything …)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="It would be embarrassing, were she to find out the truth." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= … if she were to find out the truth.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Had they not rushed Jo to hospital, she would have died." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= If they hadn't rushed Jo to hospital …)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="Only hypothetical conditionals">
  This inversion is <strong>only</strong> for unreal / hypothetical conditionals. You would <strong>not</strong> invert a real conditional like <em>If you see John, tell him I called</em> — <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>Should you see John, tell him I called</em> (that would sound odd because it suggests the meeting is very unlikely). The inverted <em>should</em> pattern IS possible in real conditionals when the speaker wants to stress that the condition is remote or formal — <em>Should you have any questions, please do not hesitate to contact us</em> — but this is a specific formal-register use.
</Callout>

## B — If … will

We **don't usually** use *if … will* in conditional clauses. However, there are several specific situations where *if … will* is natural and correct:

**① Result in the main clause.** Use *if … will* when the *if*-clause describes a **result** of the main clause, not a condition for it:

<SayIt text="Open a window if it will help you to sleep." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or … if it helps you to sleep; 'helping you to sleep' is the result of opening the window)</p>

Compare this with a normal conditional where *will* belongs in the main clause, not the *if*-clause:

<SayIt text="I will be angry if it turns out that you are wrong." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(not … if it will turn out …; 'turning out that you are wrong' is not the result of being angry)</p>

**② Requests / willingness.** Use *if … will* (or *if … would* for more politeness) with the meaning 'if you are willing to':

<SayIt text="If you will / would take your seats, ladies and gentlemen, we can begin the meeting." />

**③ Disapproval (stressed *will*).** In real conditionals, use *if … will* to show that you **disapprove** of something. In speech, *will* is stressed:

<SayIt text="A: I'm tired.  B: Well, if you will go to bed so late, I'm not surprised." />

**④ Refusal: *if … won't*.** Use *if … won't* when you talk about a refusal to do something:

<SayIt text="There's no point in trying to teach the class if they won't pay attention." />

<Callout type="warning" title="The quick test">
  If you can replace <em>if … will</em> with <em>if … are willing to</em> or the <em>if</em>-clause is a result (not a precondition), then <em>will</em> is correct. Otherwise, drop it. <em>If you <strong>will arrive</strong> late, you'll miss the start</em> → ❌ (it's a precondition, not willingness or result). <em>If you <strong>arrive</strong> late, you'll miss the start</em> → ✅.
</Callout>

## C — If … happen to, if … should, if … should happen to

In a **real** conditional sentence, we use **if … happen to**, **if … should**, or **if … should happen to** to talk about something which **may be possible, but is not very likely**. *If … happen to* is the most common form in spoken English:

<SayIt text="If you happen to be in our area, drop in and see us." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or If you should [happen to] be in our area …)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="Not for the impossible">
  We do <strong>not</strong> use this pattern in <strong>unreal</strong> conditionals where the <em>if</em>-clause describes a state or event the speaker sees as highly unlikely or impossible:<br /><br />
  ✅ <em>If the North Sea froze in winter, you could walk from London to Oslo.</em><br />
  ❌ <em>If the North Sea happened to freeze / should happen to freeze in winter …</em><br /><br />
  The <em>happen to</em> pattern belongs to the real world — things that genuinely might occur, however remote the chance.
</Callout>

## D — As if / as though / like

In comparison clauses, we can use **as if** followed by a noun phrase, *-ing* clause, past participle (*-ed*) clause, or *to*-infinitive to introduce a comparison with a situation described in the main clause. We do this to give an **explanation** or to say that something **appears to be the case but is not**:

<SayIt text="Magnus walked in as if nothing had happened." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(noun clause — he acted like everything was normal)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="His hands made a circular motion, as if steering a bus through a sharp bend." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(-ing clause — the movement resembled steering)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="When he caught the ball, Lee fell to the floor as if hit by a bullet." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(past participle clause — he collapsed dramatically)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="As if to convince herself that Luis was really there, she gently touched his cheek." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(to-infinitive — her action was meant to confirm his presence)</p>

**As though** is a direct equivalent of *as if* and can be used in all the same patterns. In **informal speech**, some people use **like** with the same meaning:

<SayIt text="The crowd reacted as though they were watching a boxing match." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or … as if they were watching …)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="He walked into the room like nothing had happened." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or … as if nothing had happened; informal)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="Formality cline">
  <strong>as if</strong> → neutral / slightly formal<br />
  <strong>as though</strong> → neutral / slightly formal (interchangeable with as if)<br />
  <strong>like</strong> → informal / spoken (avoid in formal academic writing)
</Callout>
