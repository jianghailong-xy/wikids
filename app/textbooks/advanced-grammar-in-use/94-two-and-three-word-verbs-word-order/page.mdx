A verb plus a particle often means something you could never work out from the two words separately: *go over* is not *go* + *over*, it is **summarise**. That much you know. The hard part at this level is not the meaning but the **word order** — whether the verb takes an object at all, and if it does, whether that object goes *before* the particle, *after* it, or is locked into one position. This unit sorts those verbs into the patterns that decide it.

## A — Two-word and three-word verbs

The meaning of some verbs commonly used with a particular **preposition** or **adverb** (or *particle*) is often different from the meaning of their separate parts. We can call these **two-word verbs**:

<SayIt text="I'll quickly go over the main points of the report again." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>go over</em> = summarise)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="She had to let her dress out because she'd put on weight." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>let out</em> = make it larger)</p>

Other **three-word verbs** are commonly used with an **adverb + preposition**:

<SayIt text="Do you think he's really likely to go through with his threat?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>go through with</em> = do it)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The team has failed to live up to earlier expectations." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>live up to</em> = achieve what was expected)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="A note on the name">
  These two- and three-word verbs are sometimes also called <strong>phrasal verbs</strong>. The label matters less than the pattern: what you have to learn for each one is whether it takes an object, and <em>where that object goes</em>.
</Callout>

## B — Two-word verbs: with an object or without

Many two-word verbs are usually **intransitive** — they take no object at all:

<SayIt text="He grew up on a farm." />
<br />
<SayIt text="When she came to she found herself in hospital." />

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-emerald-700">Also — no object</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-emerald-900">crop up · fall through · get up · move off · shop around · splash out</p>
</div>

However, some two-word verbs can be used **transitively or intransitively with the same meaning**:

<SayIt text="I'll call back later." />
<br />
<SayIt text="I'll call you back when I get home." />

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-sky-200 bg-sky-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-sky-700">Also — object optional, meaning unchanged</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-sky-900">answer back · clear away · cover up · help out · take over · tidy up · wash up</p>
</div>

And other two-word verbs can be used transitively or intransitively **with a different meaning**:

<SayIt text="The engine cut out and the car came to a stop." />
<br />
<SayIt text="I cut the picture out and kept it." />

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-amber-200 bg-amber-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-amber-700">Also — object changes the meaning</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-amber-900">break in · hold out · look out · look up · pick up · split up · turn in · wind up</p>
</div>

## C — Object before *or* after the adverb

With many **transitive** two-word verbs, the object can come **before or after** the adverb:

<SayIt text="I want to try out the local food." />
<br />
<SayIt text="I want to try the local food out." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Both orders are natural.)</p>

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-violet-200 bg-violet-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-violet-700">Also — either order</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-violet-900">bring about · clean up · count out · drink up · gather up · get down · leave out · make up · mess up · shoot down · sort out · throw away · use up</p>
</div>

<Callout type="warning" title="Two constraints that override the free choice">
  <strong>1. A pronoun object must come between the verb and the adverb.</strong><br />
  <em>I won't be able to go to the party. You'll have to <strong>count me out</strong>.</em> — <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>count out me</em>.<br /><br />
  <strong>2. We prefer the object after the adverb when the object is long.</strong> Compare:<br />
  <em>She had to <strong>clean the kitchen up</strong>.</em> (or <em>clean up the kitchen</em>) — a short object, so either order.<br />
  <em>She had to <strong>clean up the mess in the kitchen</strong>.</em> — rather than <em>clean the mess in the kitchen up</em>, which strands the particle too far from its verb.
</Callout>

## D — Object *must* come between the verb and the adverb

With some transitive two-word verbs, the object comes **between** the verb and the adverb — there is no choice:

<SayIt text="I just couldn't tell the twins apart." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>tell apart the twins</em>)</p>

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-rose-200 bg-rose-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-rose-700">Also — object always in the middle</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-rose-900">catch out · hear out · order about · pull to · push to · shut up (= to silence) · stand up</p>
</div>

## E — Object follows the preposition

With some transitive two-word verbs, the object **follows the preposition**. The verb and its particle are never separated:

<SayIt text="She takes after her mother." />
<br />
<SayIt text="I flicked through a magazine while I was waiting." />

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-teal-200 bg-teal-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-teal-700">Also — object after the preposition</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-teal-900">account for · act on · approve of · call on · check into · look after · provide for · result from · run into · take against</p>
</div>

<Callout type="tip" title="Why these can't split">
  In sections C and D the particle is an <strong>adverb</strong>, so it can move. Here it is a <strong>preposition</strong>, and a preposition has to stay next to the noun it governs — including when that noun is a pronoun: <em>She takes after <strong>her</strong></em>, <span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>She takes her after</em>.
</Callout>

## F — Three-word verbs

With **most** three-word verbs, the object goes **after the preposition**:

<SayIt text="He really looks up to his older brother." />

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-indigo-200 bg-indigo-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-indigo-700">Also — object after the preposition</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-indigo-900">come in for · come up against · cut back on · look down on · put up with</p>
</div>

However, a few three-word verbs usually have the object **immediately after the verb**. A second noun or noun phrase then goes after the preposition:

<SayIt text="She tried to talk me out of the plan." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>me</em> straight after the verb; <em>the plan</em> after the preposition.)</p>

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-xl border-2 border-fuchsia-200 bg-fuchsia-50 p-4">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-fuchsia-700">Also — object straight after the verb</p>
  <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-fuchsia-900">do out of · help on with · let in on · put down as · put up to · take up on</p>
</div>

<div className="not-prose my-6 overflow-hidden rounded-2xl border-2 border-slate-200 shadow-sm">

<Callout type="tip" title="Take it further">
  When you meet a new phrasal verb, don't just record its meaning — record its <strong>frame</strong>.
