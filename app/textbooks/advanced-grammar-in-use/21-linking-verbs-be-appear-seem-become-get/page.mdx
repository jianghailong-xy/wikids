## A — Linking verbs and complements

When an adjective or noun phrase is used after a verb to **describe the subject**, or to **say what or who the subject is**, that adjective or noun phrase is a **complement** and the verb that joins it to the subject is a **linking verb**.

<SayIt text="Clara is a doctor." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(noun-phrase complement — it tells us what Clara is)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="She seemed unable to concentrate." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(adjective complement — it describes her)</p>

`be` is the commonest linking verb, but a whole family of others does the same job. It helps to sort them by the kind of meaning they add:

<div className="not-prose my-6 grid gap-3 sm:grid-cols-3">
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-sky-200 bg-sky-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-sky-700">'being' verbs</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-sky-900">be, keep, prove, remain, stay</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-xs text-sky-700/80">the state continues or is shown to hold</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-amber-200 bg-amber-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-amber-700">'becoming' verbs</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-amber-900">become, come, end up, go, grow, turn out</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-xs text-amber-700/80">a change into a new state</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-violet-200 bg-violet-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-violet-700">'seeming' verbs</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-violet-900">appear, look, seem, sound</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-xs text-violet-700/80">how the subject strikes us</p>
  </div>
</div>

Most of these verbs can be followed by **either an adjective or a noun phrase**:

<SayIt text="It sounds like a vibration." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The plan proved impossible." />

<Callout type="warning" title="come and grow are the exception">
  When <strong>come</strong> and <strong>grow</strong> are used as linking verbs (<em>come to know someone</em>, <em>grow thoughtful</em>), they <strong>can't</strong> be followed by a noun phrase. ✅ <em>She grew anxious.</em> &nbsp; ❌ <em>She grew an anxious person.</em>
</Callout>

## B — appear / look / prove / seem / turn out (+ to be)

After the verbs **appear** (= seems true), **look** (= seem), **prove**, **seem** and **turn out** we can often **either include or leave out** *to be*:

<SayIt text="The room appears to be brighter than when I last saw it." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The room appears brighter than when I last saw it." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(both fine — the adjective <em>brighter</em> follows directly or after <em>to be</em>)</p>

But *to be* is **usually included** in two cases — before the adjectives **alive, alone, asleep** and **awake**, and before the **-ing form** of a verb:

<SayIt text="I didn't go in because she appeared to be asleep." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(not <em>... she appeared asleep</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="The television seems to be working okay now." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(-ing form → keep <em>to be</em>)</p>

Before a **noun**, the choice depends on meaning. We **include** *to be* when the noun tells us **what the subject actually is**; we often **leave it out** when we are simply **giving our opinion** of the subject. Compare:

<SayIt text="He walked into what appeared to be a cave." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(identifying what the place was — keep <em>to be</em>; not <em>what seemed a cave</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="She seems a very efficient salesperson." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(an opinion — <em>to be</em> can be left out)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="In formal writing, keep it">
  Leaving out <em>to be</em> before a noun belongs to more informal English. In formal speech and writing, <em>to be</em> is usually kept: <em>The findings proved <strong>to be</strong> highly significant.</em>
</Callout>

## C — become / get and the verbs of change

We use the linking verb **become** to describe a **process of change**. A number of other linking verbs can be used instead — **come, get, go, grow, turn (into)**.

We prefer **get** rather than **become** in **informal speech and writing**:

- before *difficult, ill, interested, pregnant, angry, unhappy* and *worried*
- in **imperatives**
- in fixed phrases such as **get changed** (clothes), **get dressed, get married / divorced**

<SayIt text="I first got suspicious when he looked into all the cars." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(more formally: <em>... became suspicious ...</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Don't get upset about it!" />
<br />
<SayIt text="Where did you live before you got married?" />

But we use **become, not get**, when there is a **noun phrase** after the linking verb:

<SayIt text="Dr Morales became an adviser to the government." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(not <em>got an adviser</em>)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="These usually take become">
  With <strong>apparent, aware, convinced, infected, irrelevant</strong> and <strong>obvious</strong> we normally choose <em>become</em> rather than <em>get</em>: <em>It became obvious that ...</em>, <em>She became convinced that ...</em>
</Callout>

## D — go / turn for colours and unwanted change

We use **go** or **turn** — not usually *get* or *become* — when we talk about **colours changing**:

<SayIt text="The traffic lights turned green and I pulled away." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The traffic lights went green and I pulled away." />

We often use **go** to talk about changes too, **particularly unwanted ones**:

<div className="not-prose my-6 grid gap-3 sm:grid-cols-3">
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-rose-200 bg-rose-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-rose-700">faculties / mind</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-rose-900">go deaf · blind · bald · mad · crazy · wild</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-orange-200 bg-orange-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-orange-700">things failing</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-orange-900">go bad · off · mouldy · rotten · bust · dead</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border-2 border-stone-300 bg-stone-50 p-4">
    <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-stone-600">other</p>
    <p className="mt-1 text-sm font-semibold text-stone-800">go missing · go wrong</p>
  </div>
</div>

<SayIt text="The company went bust and had to close." />
<br />
<SayIt text="My computer's gone wrong again." />

<Callout type="warning" title="But these take get, not go">
  A small set of unwanted changes use <strong>get</strong>: <strong>get ill, get old, get tired</strong>. ✅ <em>Some people get ill very easily.</em> &nbsp; ❌ <em>... go ill ...</em>
</Callout>

After the verbs **come, get** and **grow** (but **not** after *become*) we use a **to-infinitive**. *Come* and *grow* are often used to talk about **gradual change**:

<SayIt text="I eventually came to appreciate his work." />
<br />
<SayIt text="I eventually grew to appreciate his work." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(not <em>... became to appreciate his work</em>)</p>
