This unit is about the language of **small and shrinking quantities**. You already know the meaning gap between *few / little* ('not much, almost none — a negative idea') and *a few / a little* ('some, a small amount — a positive idea'). Here we go further: using these words as **pronouns**, sharpening them with **the few / the little** and **what few / what little**, choosing the right register in **speech versus academic writing**, and — the classic trap — keeping **less** and **fewer** apart.

<Callout type="info" title="The meaning gap, in one line">
  <strong>Few / little</strong> stresses how <em>little</em> there is (<em>Few people came</em> = almost nobody). <strong>A few / a little</strong> stresses that there is <em>some</em> (<em>A few people came</em> = some did). This unit assumes that contrast and builds on it.
</Callout>

## A — (a) few and (a) little as pronouns

We often use **(a) few** and **(a) little** with nouns. However, we can also use them on their own, as **pronouns**, when the noun is clear from the context:

<SayIt text="It is a part of the world visited by few." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= visited by few <em>people</em>.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Do you want a chocolate? There's still a few left." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= a few <em>chocolates</em>.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Little is known about the painter's early life." />
<br />
<SayIt text="'Do you know anything about car engines?' 'A little.'" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= I know a little <em>about car engines</em>.)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="quite a few = a fairly large number">
  Counter-intuitively, <strong>quite a few</strong> does <em>not</em> mean 'rather few' — it means <strong>'quite a large number'</strong>: <em>She's been away from work for <strong>quite a few</strong> weeks.</em>
</Callout>

## B — the few / the little, what few / what little, my few / these few

We can use **the few** and **the little** + a noun (or pronoun) to suggest **'not enough'** — when we talk about a group of things or people (with **few**) or part of a group or amount (with **little**):

<SayIt text="It's one of the few shops in the city centre where you can buy food." />
<br />
<SayIt text="We should use the little time we have available to discuss Jon's proposal." />

Instead of **the few / the little** we can use **what few / what little** to mean **'the small number / amount (that there is)'**:

<SayIt text="She gave what little money she had in her purse to the man." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or … <em>the little</em> money …)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="What few visitors we have are always welcome." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>The few</em> visitors …)</p>

<Callout type="info" title="Drop the noun when the context is clear">
  We can leave out the noun after <strong>what / the</strong> too: <em>She gave <strong>what / the little</strong> she had …</em> and <em><strong>What / The few</strong> we have …</em> — when it's obvious what is being referred to.
</Callout>

We can use **few** (but **rarely little**) after **possessives** (*my, her,* etc.) and after **these / those**:

<SayIt text="I learned to play golf during my few days off during the summer." />
<br />
<SayIt text="These few miles of motorway have taken over ten years to build." />

<Callout type="warning" title="few after a possessive — not little">
  ✅ <em>my <strong>few</strong> days off</em> · <em>these <strong>few</strong> miles</em> &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ <em>my little days off</em>. After <em>my / her / these / those</em> we use <strong>few</strong>, not <em>little</em>, with countable nouns.
</Callout>

## C — Small quantities: speech vs academic writing

In **speech and informal writing**, we usually avoid bare *few / little*. Instead we use **not many / not much**, or **only / just + a few / a little**, and we often prefer **a bit (of)** to *a little*:

<SayIt text="Sorry I haven't finished — I haven't had much time today." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(rather than <em>… I had little time …</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="I won't be long — I've only got a few things to get." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(rather than <em>… I've got few things …</em>)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="Want a bit of chocolate?" />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(rather than <em>… a little chocolate?</em>)</p>

In **more formal contexts**, such as academic writing, we generally prefer the plain **few** and **little**:

<SayIt text="The results take little account of personal preference." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(rather than <em>… don't take much account …</em>)</p>

  </div>
</div>

## D — less (than) and fewer (than)

We use **less** with **uncountable** nouns and **fewer** with **plural countable** nouns:

<SayIt text="You should eat less pasta." />
<br />
<SayIt text="There are fewer cars on the road today." />

<Callout type="warning" title="less cars is widely heard — and widely marked wrong">
  <strong>Less</strong> is often used with a plural countable noun in conversation (<em>… less cars …</em>), but it is traditionally treated as <strong>grammatically incorrect</strong>. In writing — and exams — use <strong>fewer</strong> with things you can count.
</Callout>

We use **less** with a noun phrase indicating an **amount**, and **fewer than** with a noun phrase referring to a **group of things or people**:

<SayIt text="I used to earn less than a pound a week when I first started work." />
<br />
<SayIt text="There were fewer than 20 students at the lecture." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or, informally, <em>… less than 20 students …</em> — but some people regard this as incorrect.)</p>

When we talk about a **distance** or a **sum of money**, we use **less than**, not *fewer than*:

<SayIt text="The beach is less than a mile away." />

To emphasise that a number is **surprisingly large**, we can use **no less than** or **no fewer than**:

<SayIt text="The team has had no fewer than ten managers in just five years." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(or <em>… no less than ten managers …</em>)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="no less than with %, time and quantities">
  With <strong>percentages, periods of time and quantities</strong> we prefer <strong>no less than</strong>: <em>Profits have increased by <strong>no less than</strong> 95% in the last year</em> (rather than <em>no fewer than</em>). The same pull towards <em>less than</em> explains <em>The journey took <strong>less than</strong> two hours</em> — a period of time, not a countable group.
</Callout>
