In **Unit 99** you met inversion after fronted negative adverbials such as *Never*, *Not until* and *Hardly*. This unit widens the picture: the full family of negative and restrictive adverbials that trigger inversion, plus a second pattern altogether — the emphatic **so + adjective**, **such + be**, and the negative-addition words **neither / nor**.

<Callout type="info" title="What 'inversion' means here">
  Inversion = the **subject and the first auxiliary swap places**, exactly as in a question. When there is no auxiliary, we supply <strong>do / does / did</strong> with the simple tense — again, just like a question: <em>they broke</em> → <em>did they break</em>. The trigger word goes first; the rest of the clause then inverts behind it.
</Callout>

## A — Inversion after negative adverbials

In formal and literary language in particular, we put a **negative adverbial at the beginning of a clause** for emphasis. The subject and first auxiliary are then inverted, and **do** is used with a simple-tense verb.

**1. Time adverbials — *never (before), rarely, seldom*; *barely / hardly / scarcely … when / before*; *no sooner … than*:**

<SayIt text="Seldom do we have goods returned to us because they are faulty." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Hardly had everybody taken their seats when Dr Lee began her lecture." />

**2. *only* + a time expression** — *only after, only later, only if, only once, only then, only when*:

<SayIt text="She bought a newspaper and some milk at the shop on the corner. Only later did she realise that she'd been given the wrong change." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Only when she apologises will I speak to her again." />

**3. *only* + other prepositional phrases** beginning *only by …, only in …, only with …*, etc.:

<SayIt text="Only by chance had Carl discovered where the birds were nesting." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Maria had to work in the evenings. Only in this way was she able to complete the report." />

**4. Expressions such as *at no time, in no way, on no account, under / in no circumstances*:**

<SayIt text="At no time did they actually break the rules of the game." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Under no circumstances are passengers permitted to open the doors themselves." />

**5. Expressions with *not …*** — *not only, not until, not since, not for one moment* — and also ***not a* + noun**:

<SayIt text="Not until August did the government order an inquiry into the accident." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Not a word had she written since the exam had started." />

**6. *little*** with a negative meaning:

<SayIt text="Little do they know how lucky they are to live in such a wonderful house." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= 'they don't know', or 'they don't realise sufficiently'.)</p>

<Callout type="warning" title="The inversion sits on the main clause">
  After <em>only after / if / when</em> and <em>not until</em>, the adverbial clause stays in normal order — it is the <strong>following main clause</strong> that inverts:
  <br />✅ <em>Only when the famine <strong>gets</strong> worse <strong>will world governments</strong> begin to act.</em>
  <br />✅ <em>Not until the train <strong>pulled</strong> into the station <strong>did Miguel</strong> find that his coat had gone.</em>
  <br />Don't invert the <em>when / until</em> clause itself — invert what comes after it.
</Callout>

## B — Inversion after *so + adjective*, *such + be*, and *neither / nor*

This is a different trigger from the negative adverbials in **A** — here we front a word to emphasise **degree or extent**, and to add a negative point.

**1. *so + adjective* at the start of a clause** emphasises the adjective. The subject and first auxiliary invert (and **do** appears with a simple tense):

<SayIt text="So successful was her business that Marie was able to retire at the age of 50." />
<br />
<SayIt text="So dangerous did weather conditions become that all mountain roads were closed." />

**2. *such + be* at the start of a clause** emphasises the extent or degree of something:

<SayIt text="Such is the popularity of the play that the theatre is likely to be full every night." />

<Callout type="tip" title="so vs such — what follows the trigger">
  <strong>So</strong> fronts an <strong>adjective</strong> (or adverb): <em>So <u>cold</u> was it that …</em>
  <br /><strong>Such</strong> fronts the verb <strong>be</strong> + a <strong>noun phrase</strong>: <em>Such <u>was the cold</u> that …</em>
  <br />Both are usually rounded off by a <strong>that</strong>-clause stating the result.
</Callout>

**3. Inversion after *neither* and *nor*** when these words begin a clause adding a **negative point** to a previous clause or sentence:

<SayIt text="For some time after the explosion Jack couldn't hear, and neither could he see." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The council never waited for the new supermarket to be built, nor did local residents." />

<Callout type="info" title="The short-response cousins">
  The same inversion drives the everyday agreement responses: <strong>Neither / Nor do I</strong>, <strong>Neither / Nor does Diane</strong> (adding a negative), and <strong>So do I</strong>, <strong>So does Maya</strong> (adding a positive). Auxiliary first, subject second — every time.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Use it deliberately, not constantly">
  Fronted inversion is a tool of <strong>emphasis and formality</strong> — a courtroom summing-up, a news report, a piece of persuasive writing. Drop one well-placed <em>Not until …</em> or <em>So great was …</em> into a paragraph and it lands; pile up five and the writing turns purple. The skill this book has built towards is exactly this: knowing the structure cold, then choosing when it earns its place.
</Callout>
