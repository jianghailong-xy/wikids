English likes to keep its heavy, complicated material at the **end** of a clause and to lead with something short and already familiar. Introductory (or *preparatory*) **it** is the device that makes this possible: it holds the subject — or object — slot at the front so that a long *to*-infinitive, *that*-clause, *wh*-clause or *-ing* clause can slide to the position where it reads most naturally. This unit lays out the main patterns, the verbs that trigger them, and the one place where introductory *it* is **not** an option.

## A — Introductory *it*: heavy last, light first

We can use an introductory **it** at the beginning of a sentence for two closely related reasons.

**First, to place long or grammatically complex elements at the end** — the usual home for them in English. Compare:

<SayIt text="To drive without a licence is illegal." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Grammatically correct, but front-heavy and stiff.)</p>
<br />
<SayIt text="It is illegal to drive without a licence." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>it</em> fills the subject slot; the infinitive moves to the end.)</p>

**Second, to focus attention on something** by saving it for the end — the usual place for new or important information in English. Compare:

<SayIt text="That she wasn't hurt is a miracle." />
<br />
<SayIt text="It's a miracle that she wasn't hurt." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(The point — <em>that she wasn't hurt</em> — now lands last, where the stress falls.)</p>

Introductory *it* is most common when the postponed subject is a **to-infinitive** or a **that-clause** (as above), but it works just as well when the subject is a **wh-clause** or an **-ing clause** (see also Unit 25):

<SayIt text="It is clear why Diego decided to leave Spain." />
<br />
<SayIt text="It is useless asking Sophie to help." />

<Callout type="tip" title="The test for introductory it">
  Ask yourself: <em>what real subject is <strong>it</strong> standing in for?</em> If you can answer with a clause or infinitive (<em>to drive…</em>, <em>that she wasn't hurt</em>, <em>why Diego left</em>, <em>asking Sophie</em>), the <strong>it</strong> pattern is available. If the only answer is a plain noun, it usually isn't — see Section&nbsp;C.
</Callout>

## B — Other patterns with introductory *it*

So far the examples have used **it + be + adjective / noun** (*it is illegal*, *it's a miracle*). But several other patterns take an introductory *it*. Here are the common ones.

<div className="not-prose my-3 rounded-xl border-2 border-emerald-200 bg-emerald-50/60 p-4">
  <p className="font-mono text-sm font-bold text-emerald-800">it + verb + to-infinitive</p>
</div>

<SayIt text="'I've got a terrible headache.' 'It helps to lie down.'" />
<br />
<SayIt text="If you want someone to help you, it doesn't do to annoy them just before you ask." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>'[not] do'</em> here = <em>[not] advisable, acceptable or enough</em>.)</p>

<div className="not-prose my-3 rounded-lg border border-emerald-200 bg-white px-4 py-2 text-sm">
  <span className="font-semibold text-emerald-700">Also:</span> <strong>hurt, pay</strong> <span className="text-slate-600">(= give an advantage or benefit) — <em>It pays to plan ahead.</em></span>
</div>

<div className="not-prose my-3 rounded-xl border-2 border-sky-200 bg-sky-50/60 p-4">
  <p className="font-mono text-sm font-bold text-sky-800">it + verb + object + to-infinitive</p>
</div>

<SayIt text="It shocked him to see her looking so ill." />
<br />
<SayIt text="It means a lot to get a place at university." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(After these verbs the object — <em>him</em> — usually refers to a person.)</p>

<div className="not-prose my-3 rounded-lg border border-sky-200 bg-white px-4 py-2 text-sm">
  <span className="font-semibold text-sky-700">Also:</span> <strong>amaze, annoy, astonish, concern, cost, frighten, hurt, scare, surprise, upset, worry</strong> <span className="text-slate-600">(most are to do with feelings)</span>
</div>

<Callout type="info" title="A special case: it + take + object + to-infinitive">
  We use <strong>it + take + object + to-infinitive</strong> to say what is or was needed for a particular activity — the time, resources or personal qualities it demands:<br /><br />
  <em>It takes a lot of effort to play the flute.</em> &nbsp;(more natural than <em>To play the flute takes a lot of effort.</em>)
</Callout>

<div className="not-prose my-3 rounded-xl border-2 border-violet-200 bg-violet-50/60 p-4">
  <p className="font-mono text-sm font-bold text-violet-800">it + verb + that-clause</p>
</div>

<SayIt text="It seems that she has lost her memory." />
<br />
<SayIt text="It emerged that he already had a criminal record." />

<div className="not-prose my-3 rounded-lg border border-violet-200 bg-white px-4 py-2 text-sm">
  <span className="font-semibold text-violet-700">Also:</span> <strong>appear, come about, follow, happen, transpire</strong>
</div>

<div className="not-prose my-3 rounded-xl border-2 border-amber-200 bg-amber-50/60 p-4">
  <p className="font-mono text-sm font-bold text-amber-800">it + verb + object + that-clause</p>
</div>

<SayIt text="It suddenly hit me that Sara wanted to borrow money." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(<em>Less likely:</em> That Sara wanted to borrow money suddenly hit me.)</p>

<div className="not-prose my-3 rounded-lg border border-amber-200 bg-white px-4 py-2 text-sm">
  <span className="font-semibold text-amber-700">Also:</span> <strong>dawn on, not bother, strike</strong> <span className="text-slate-600">(= occur to),</span> <strong>turn out</strong><span className="text-slate-600">, and the highlighted verbs above. The object again usually refers to a person.</span>
</div>

## C — *It ...* is not a substitute for a noun subject

We do **not** normally use an *it ...* pattern as an alternative to a plain **noun** subject. There is no clause or infinitive to postpone, so *it* has nothing to stand in for:

<SayIt text="Their success was unexpected." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1"><span className="text-rose-600">not</span> <em>It was unexpected their success.</em></p>

In informal contexts, however — **especially in speech** — this *does* happen, in two related ways. We may bring a short comment to the front to give **special emphasis** to the information that immediately follows *It ...*:

<SayIt text="It tastes really good, this new ice cream." />

…and we may push a **longer noun phrase to the end** to focus attention on it:

<SayIt text="It's ridiculous, all the bureaucracy involved in running a school these days." />

<Callout type="warning" title="Keep the spoken version where it belongs">
  These tag-on sentences (<em>It tastes really good, this new ice cream</em>) are natural in conversation as an emphatic afterthought, but they are <strong>not</strong> a neutral, written substitute for a noun subject. In careful writing, keep the noun in the subject slot: <em>This new ice cream tastes really good.</em>
</Callout>
