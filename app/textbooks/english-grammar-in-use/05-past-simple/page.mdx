## A — Study this example situation

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-2xl border-2 border-amber-200 bg-gradient-to-br from-amber-50 to-orange-50 p-5 shadow-sm">
  <div className="flex flex-col items-center gap-4 sm:flex-row">
    <div className="rounded-xl bg-amber-100 p-4 text-center text-4xl shadow-inner">🎼</div>
    <div className="flex-1">
      <p className="text-lg font-semibold text-amber-900">Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart</p>
      <p className="text-sm text-amber-700">Austrian composer (1756–1791)</p>
      <p className="mt-2 text-base text-amber-800">
        He <strong>lived</strong> from 1756 to 1791.
        He <strong>started</strong> composing at the age of five.
        He <strong>wrote</strong> more than 600 pieces of music.
      </p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<SayIt text="Mozart lived from 1756 to 1791. He started composing at the age of five and wrote more than 600 pieces." />

**Lived / started / composed / wrote / died** are all **past simple**.

The past simple tells us about **a finished action at a finished time**. Mozart's life is over. He composed his music a long time ago — and then he stopped. The action is **complete, not connected to now**.

<Callout type="tip" title="The core idea">
  The past simple is for <strong>finished actions at a finished time</strong>. The action started in the past and finished in the past. There's no link to the present — it's over.
</Callout>

---

Very often the past simple ends in **-ed** (regular verbs):

<SayIt text="I work in a travel agency now. Before that I worked in a department store." />
<br />
<SayIt text="We invited them to our party, but they decided not to come." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The police stopped me on my way home last night." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Laura passed her exam because she studied very hard." />

<Callout type="info" title="Time markers that signal the past simple">
  When you see these, the past simple is almost certainly the right tense: <strong>yesterday</strong>, <strong>last night/week/month/year</strong>, <strong>... ago</strong>, <strong>in 2010</strong>, <strong>when I was a child</strong>, <strong>at the age of five</strong>, <strong>for ten years</strong> (a finished period).
</Callout>

## Spelling: how to add -ed correctly

Most verbs just add **-ed**: *work → worked*, *start → started*, *play → played*, *rain → rained*. But three patterns are worth knowing:

### Rule 1 — verb ends in -e? Just add -d.

<Contraction
  caption="live → lived"
  segments={[
    { keep: "liv" },
    { swap: "e", to: "ed" },
  ]}
/>

Same: **dance → danced**, **like → liked**, **hope → hoped**, **phone → phoned**, **invite → invited**.

### Rule 2 — consonant + y? y → ied.

<Contraction
  caption="study → studied"
  segments={[
    { keep: "stud" },
    { swap: "y", to: "ied" },
  ]}
/>

Same: **try → tried**, **copy → copied**, **cry → cried**, **carry → carried**, **hurry → hurried**.

<Callout type="warning" title="Vowel + y stays put">
  Only swap when a <strong>consonant</strong> comes before the <strong>y</strong>. After a vowel, just add normal <strong>-ed</strong>: ✅ <em>play → played</em>, ✅ <em>enjoy → enjoyed</em>, ✅ <em>stay → stayed</em>.
</Callout>

### Rule 3 — short word, one vowel + one consonant? Double the consonant.

<Contraction
  caption="stop → stopped"
  segments={[
    { keep: "sto" },
    { swap: "p", to: "pped" },
  ]}
/>

Same: **plan → planned**, **shop → shopped**, **rob → robbed**, **rub → rubbed** (and **prefer → preferred**, **admit → admitted** — the stress is on the last syllable).

But no doubling in **happen → happened**, **visit → visited**, **open → opened**, **listen → listened** (the last syllable isn't stressed, or the word ends in two consonants already).

## C — Questions with did

In the past simple, we use **did** + the **base form** (infinitive without *to*) to make questions — for **all persons** (I, you, he, she, it, we, they):

| | |
|---|---|
| **Did** I / you / he / she / it / we / they | **work?** &nbsp; **go?** &nbsp; **see?** &nbsp; **have?** |

<SayIt text="Did you go to the cinema last night?" />
<br />
<SayIt text="Did she see the email I sent?" />
<br />
<SayIt text="What did you do at the weekend?" />
<br />
<SayIt text="Where did they go for their holiday?" />
<br />
<SayIt text="How did the accident happen?" />

<Callout type="warning" title="Two critical rules for did-questions">
  1. <strong>Did</strong> goes before the subject. <br />
  2. The main verb stays in the <strong>plain form</strong> — never in the past. <br /><br />
  ✅ <strong>Did</strong> you <strong>go</strong> out last night? &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ Did you went out last night? <br />
  ✅ What <strong>did</strong> you <strong>do</strong>? &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ What did you did? <br />
  ✅ Where <strong>did</strong> she <strong>buy</strong> that bag? &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ Where did she bought that bag? <br />
  <br />
  Think: <em>did</em> already carries the past — the main verb can relax in the plain form.
</Callout>

### Yes/No answers: short forms

In conversation, we don't repeat the full sentence. We use short answers:

<SayIt text="'Did you go to the cinema?' 'Yes, I did.'" />
<br />
<SayIt text="'Did she see you?' 'No, she didn't.'" />
<br />
<SayIt text="'Did they enjoy the party?' 'Yes, they did.'" />

The pattern: **Yes + subject + did** or **No + subject + didn't**. Never say "Yes, I went" or "No, I didn't go" as a short answer — it sounds unnatural.

## D — Negatives with didn't

To make a negative in the past simple, use **didn't** (= did not) + the **base form** — again, for all persons:

| | |
|---|---|
| I / you / he / she / it / we / they | **didn't** work &nbsp; **didn't** go &nbsp; **didn't** see |

<SayIt text="I didn't go out last night. I stayed at home." />
<br />
<SayIt text="She didn't enjoy the film. She thought it was boring." />
<br />
<SayIt text="They didn't have any money, so they walked home." />
<br />
<SayIt text="He didn't pass the exam because he didn't study enough." />

Listen to the contrast — a negative followed by a positive, just like in real conversation:

<SayIt text="I didn't watch TV last night. I read a book instead." />
<br />
<SayIt text="We didn't go away at the weekend. We stayed at home." />
<br />
<SayIt text="He didn't call me — he sent a text message." />

<Callout type="warning" title="Didn't + BASE form — never the past">
  Once <strong>didn't</strong> is there, the main verb goes back to its plain form: <br />
  ✅ I <strong>didn't go</strong> out. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ I didn't went out. <br />
  ✅ She <strong>didn't see</strong> him. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ She didn't saw him. <br />
  ✅ They <strong>didn't have</strong> breakfast. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ They didn't had breakfast. <br />
  <br />
  <em>Didn't</em> carries the past — the verb doesn't need to.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Take it further">
  Write a short diary entry about **yesterday** — 6 to 8 sentences. Use at least **three regular verbs** (-ed) and **three irregular verbs** (went, had, saw, ate, got, made, etc.). Include one question tag (*Did I ...?*), one negative (*I didn't ...*), and one time marker (*yesterday, last night, this morning, ... ago*). Read it out loud — your mouth needs the practice as much as your brain.
</Callout>

<Callout type="info" title="What's next?">
  Unit 5 is just the foundation. The past simple gets more interesting when it meets the past continuous in **Unit 6** — *I was doing something when something else happened*. The past simple is the **finished event**; the past continuous is the **background activity**. That contrast is one of the most useful patterns in English storytelling.
</Callout>
