## A — Study this example situation

Sarah is in her car. She is on her way to work.

<SayIt text="She's driving to work." />

**This means:** she is driving **now, at the time of speaking**. The action is not finished.

  <div className="my-3 flex items-center justify-center gap-2 text-sm font-semibold text-slate-600">

    <span className="text-sky-400">———</span>

  <p className="text-base font-semibold text-sky-800"><em>I am doing</em> = I'm in the middle of doing it</p>
</div>

**I am doing something** = I started doing it and I haven't finished; I'm in the middle of doing it.

<SayIt text="Please don't make so much noise. I'm trying to work." />
<br />
<SayIt text="'Where's Mark?' 'He's having a shower.'" />
<br />
<SayIt text="Let's go out now. It isn't raining any more." />
<br />
<SayIt text="How's your new job? Are you enjoying it?" />
<br />
<SayIt text="What's all that noise? What's going on?" />

<Callout type="info" title="Native speaker note">
  <em>What's happening?</em> and <em>What's going on?</em> both mean the same thing — and they're almost always in the continuous. You'll hear them constantly in everyday conversation.
</Callout>

## B — am/is/are + -ing

The present continuous is always **am / is / are + -ing**:

| | |
|---|---|
| **I** am (`'m`) | driving |
| **he / she / it** is (`'s`) | working |
| **we / you / they** are (`'re`) | doing etc. |

<SayIt text="I'm driving." /> &nbsp; <SayIt text="He's working." /> &nbsp; <SayIt text="We're doing our homework." />

The short forms (`I'm`, `he's`, `we're` etc.) are the normal way to speak. Use the full form (`I am`, `he is`) only in formal writing or when you want to emphasise.

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't drop the auxiliary">
  ✅ It <strong>isn't</strong> raining. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ It doesn't rain. <br />
  ✅ <strong>Are</strong> you enjoying it? &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ Do you enjoying it?
</Callout>

## C — Not happening at the time of speaking

Here is where the present continuous gets interesting. The action doesn't have to be happening at the **exact second** you speak.

**Steve is talking to a friend on the phone. He says:**

<SayIt text="I'm reading a really good book at the moment. It's about a man who ..." />

But Steve is **not** reading the book at the time of speaking — his mouth is busy talking! He means that he has **started** reading the book, but has **not finished** it yet. He is in the middle of reading it.

<Callout type="tip" title="The key insight">
  The action happens <strong>around now</strong> — this period of your life — not necessarily <strong>right this second</strong>. You've started it and you haven't finished it.
</Callout>

More examples from the book:

<SayIt text="Kate wants to work in Italy, so she's learning Italian." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(Perhaps she isn't learning Italian at the time of speaking.)</p>

<SayIt text="Some friends of mine are building their own house. They hope to finish it next summer." />

You can use the present continuous with **today / this week / this year** etc. (periods around now):

<SayIt text="You're working hard today. — Yes, I have a lot to do." />
<br />
<SayIt text="The company I work for isn't doing so well this year." />

<Callout type="info" title="Right now vs around now">
  - **Right now:** *Be quiet — I'm trying to work.* (this second) <br />
  - **Around now:** *I'm reading a really good book.* (these days — maybe not at this exact moment)
</Callout>

## D — A change that has started to happen

We use the present continuous when we talk about a change that has started to happen. We often use these verbs in this way:

<div className="not-prose my-6 grid grid-cols-2 gap-2 sm:grid-cols-4">
  {["getting", "becoming", "changing", "rising", "increasing", "starting", "beginning", "improving", "falling", "growing"].map((w) => (
    <span key={w} className="rounded-lg border-2 border-amber-200 bg-amber-50 px-3 py-2 text-center text-sm font-semibold text-amber-800">{w}</span>
  ))}
</div>

<SayIt text="Is your English getting better?" />
<br />
<SayIt text="The population of the world is increasing very fast." />
<br />
<SayIt text="At first I didn't like my job, but I'm starting to enjoy it now." />

<Callout type="tip" title="The trend test">
  If you could draw an arrow ↗ or ↘ over time, use the continuous: *prices **are rising**, the days **are getting** shorter, my cooking **is improving***.
</Callout>

## Spelling reminder: the -ing form

Most verbs just add **-ing**: *work → working*, *go → going*, *read → reading*. But three rules catch people out:

### Rule 1: one vowel + one consonant → double the consonant

<Contraction
  caption="run → running"
  segments={[
    { keep: "ru" },
    { swap: "n", to: "nning" },
  ]}
/>

Same with **stop → stopping**, **swim → swimming**, **get → getting**, **begin → beginning** (stress on the last syllable).

But **open → opening** and **listen → listening** — the stress isn't on the last syllable, so no doubling.

### Rule 2: ends in -e → drop the e

<Contraction
  caption="write → writing"
  segments={[
    { keep: "writ" },
    { swap: "e", to: "ing" },
  ]}
/>

Same with **make → making**, **dance → dancing**, **drive → driving**, **have → having**.

### Rule 3: ends in -ie → -ying

**lie → lying**, **die → dying**, **tie → tying**.

<Callout type="warning" title="Three common traps">
  ✅ They're <strong>building</strong> flats. (just add -ing — two consonants at the end) <br />
  ✅ I'm <strong>planning</strong> a trip. (plan → planning, double the n) <br />
  ❌ I'm studing. → ✅ I'm <strong>studying</strong>. (study just adds -ing — no consonant before the y)
</Callout>
