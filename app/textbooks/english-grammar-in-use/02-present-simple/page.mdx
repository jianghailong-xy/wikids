## A — Study this example situation

  <div className="flex flex-col items-center gap-4 sm:flex-row">
    <div className="rounded-xl bg-amber-100 p-4 text-center text-4xl shadow-inner">🚌</div>
    <div className="flex-1">
      <p className="text-lg font-semibold text-amber-900">Alex is a bus driver.</p>
      <p className="text-sm text-amber-700">But right now he is in bed asleep.</p>
      <p className="mt-2 text-base text-amber-800">
        So: <strong>He is not driving a bus.</strong> (He is asleep.)
      </p>
      <p className="text-base text-amber-800">
        But: <strong>He drives a bus.</strong> (He is a bus driver.)
      </p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<SayIt text="He drives a bus. → He is a bus driver. That's his job." />

The sentence **"He drives a bus"** does **not** mean he is driving right now. It means that in **general** — this is what he does for work. The action itself is not happening at this moment.

<Callout type="tip" title="The core idea">
  The present simple is for <strong>things in general</strong>: facts, habits, routines, and the way things are — not for what is happening right this second (that's what Unit 1 — present continuous — is for).
</Callout>

## B — When do we use the present simple?

We use the present simple to talk about things **in general**. We use it to say that something happens **all the time or repeatedly**, or that something is **true in general**.

Here are the main uses:

## The form: he / she / it always gets an -s

Every present simple verb has two shapes. The plain form for **I / we / you / they**, and the **-s form** for **he / she / it**.

| Subject | Verb |
|---|---|
| **I / we / you / they** | drive &nbsp; work &nbsp; do &nbsp; live &nbsp; watch |
| **He / she / it** | drive**s** &nbsp; work**s** &nbsp; doe**s** &nbsp; live**s** &nbsp; watch**es** |

<Callout type="warning" title="Never forget the third-person -s">
  ✅ My brother <strong>works</strong> in a bank. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ My brother work in a bank. <br />
  ✅ Lucy <strong>lives</strong> in London. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;❌ Lucy live in London. <br />
  ✅ The earth <strong>goes</strong> round the sun. &nbsp; ❌ The earth go round the sun.
</Callout>

Listen:

<SayIt text="I work in a shop. My brother works in a bank." />
&nbsp;
<SayIt text="Lucy lives in London. Her parents live in Scotland." />

## Spelling: how to add the -s correctly

Most verbs just add a plain **-s**: *drive → drives*, *work → works*, *live → lives*, *like → likes*. But watch out for these three patterns:

### Rule 1 — verb ends in **-s**, **-sh**, **-ch**, or **-x**? Add **-es**.

<Contraction
  caption="watch → watches"
  segments={[
    { keep: "wat" },
    { swap: "ch", to: "ches" },
  ]}
/>

Same rule for **pass → passes**, **finish → finishes**, **wash → washes**, **teach → teaches**, **fix → fixes**.

### Rule 2 — verb ends in **consonant + y**? Swap **y** → **-ies**.

<Contraction
  caption="study → studies"
  segments={[
    { keep: "stud" },
    { swap: "y", to: "ies" },
  ]}
/>

Same: **try → tries**, **fly → flies**, **cry → cries**.

<Callout type="warning" title="Vowel + y stays put">
  Only swap when a <strong>consonant</strong> comes before the <strong>y</strong>. After a vowel, just add normal <strong>-s</strong>: ✅ <em>play → plays</em>, ✅ <em>enjoy → enjoys</em>, ✅ <em>say → says</em>.
</Callout>

A few very common verbs don't follow the rules:

## C — Questions and negatives with do / does

To make questions and negative sentences in the present simple, we need the helper verbs **do** and **does**.

### Questions

| | |
|---|---|
| **Do** I / we / you / they | work? &nbsp; come? &nbsp; like? &nbsp; live? |
| **Does** he / she / it | work? &nbsp; come? &nbsp; like? &nbsp; live? |

<SayIt text="Do you like your job?" />
<br />
<SayIt text="Does she live in London?" />
<br />
<SayIt text="Where do you come from?" />
<br />
<SayIt text="What does this word mean?" />

<Callout type="warning" title="Word order trap">
  In questions, <strong>do/does</strong> goes before the subject, and the main verb stays in the plain form. <br />
  ✅ <strong>What does</strong> this word <strong>mean</strong>? &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ What means this word? <br />
  ✅ <strong>Where do</strong> your parents <strong>live</strong>? &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ Where live your parents?
</Callout>

### Negatives

| | |
|---|---|
| I / we / you / they | **don't** work &nbsp; (= do not) |
| He / she / it | **doesn't** work &nbsp; (= does not) |

<SayIt text="I don't go away very often." />
<br />
<SayIt text="Rice doesn't grow in cold climates." />
<br />
<SayIt text="He doesn't work very hard." />

<Callout type="warning" title="No -s on the main verb in negatives">
  Once <strong>doesn't</strong> is there, the main verb goes back to the plain form: <br />
  ✅ He <strong>doesn't work</strong>. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ He doesn't works. <br />
  ✅ She <strong>doesn't like</strong> tea. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ She doesn't likes tea. <br />
  Think of <em>doesn't</em> as carrying the -s so the verb doesn't need it any more.
</Callout>

## D — How often? Adverbs of frequency

When we want to say **how often** something happens, we use adverbs of frequency. These tiny words sit **before the main verb**:

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-2xl border-2 border-indigo-200 bg-gradient-to-r from-indigo-50 to-blue-50 p-5 shadow-sm">
  <p className="mb-3 text-center text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-indigo-700">
    How often? — from 100% to 0%
  </p>
  <div className="flex flex-wrap items-center justify-center gap-1 text-center sm:gap-2">
    <span className="rounded-lg bg-indigo-200 px-3 py-1 text-sm font-bold text-indigo-900">always</span>
    <span className="text-indigo-400">›</span>
    <span className="rounded-lg bg-indigo-100 px-3 py-1 text-sm font-bold text-indigo-800">usually</span>
    <span className="text-indigo-400">›</span>
    <span className="rounded-lg bg-indigo-100 px-3 py-1 text-sm font-bold text-indigo-800">often</span>
    <span className="text-indigo-400">›</span>
    <span className="rounded-lg bg-indigo-100 px-3 py-1 text-sm font-bold text-indigo-800">sometimes</span>
    <span className="text-indigo-400">›</span>
    <span className="rounded-lg bg-indigo-200 px-3 py-1 text-sm font-bold text-indigo-900">never</span>
  </div>
</div>

The word order is strict: **subject → adverb → verb**.

<Callout type="info" title="The adverb goes BEFORE the main verb">
  ✅ Sue <strong>always gets</strong> to work early. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ Sue gets always to work early. <br />
  ✅ I <strong>never eat</strong> breakfast. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ I eat never breakfast. <br />
  ✅ Robert <strong>usually goes</strong> away at weekends. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ Robert goes usually away at weekends.
</Callout>

Listen:

<SayIt text="Sue always gets to work early." />
&nbsp;
<SayIt text="I never eat breakfast." />
&nbsp;
<SayIt text="We often go away at weekends." />
&nbsp;
<SayIt text="I sometimes walk to work, but not very often." />

## E — I promise / I apologise / I suggest ...

Sometimes we use the present simple for things we **say** at the moment of speaking — promises, apologies, suggestions, and more. Even though we are speaking right now, English uses the present simple for these "speech act" verbs:

<Callout type="tip" title="Use it like a native">
  When you're in a situation that calls for a <strong>promise</strong>, an <strong>apology</strong>, or a <strong>suggestion</strong>, reach for the simple form: <em>I promise</em>, <em>I apologise</em>, <em>I suggest</em>. Saying <em>I'm promising</em> or <em>I'm apologising</em> sounds unnatural — native speakers almost never do that in conversation.
</Callout>
