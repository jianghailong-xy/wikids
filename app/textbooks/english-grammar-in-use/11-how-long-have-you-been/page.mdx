In **Unit 9** and **Unit 10** you met the present perfect continuous (*I've been doing*). This unit answers one very practical question: when a situation **began in the past and is still true now**, which tense do you reach for? The answer surprises a lot of learners — it's the **present perfect**, not the present simple.

## A — Study this example situation

Dan and Kate are married. They got married exactly 20 years ago, so today is their 20th wedding anniversary.

<div className="not-prose my-6 rounded-2xl border-2 border-rose-200 bg-gradient-to-br from-rose-50 to-pink-50 p-5 text-center shadow-sm">
  <p className="text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-wider text-rose-700">a situation that began in the past and continues now</p>
  <div className="my-3 flex items-center justify-center gap-2 text-sm font-semibold text-slate-600">
    <span>got married<br /><span className="text-xs font-normal text-slate-400">20 years ago</span></span>
    <span className="text-rose-400">———————</span>
    <span className="rounded-lg bg-rose-200 px-3 py-1 text-rose-900">NOW · still married</span>
  </div>
  <p className="text-base font-semibold text-rose-800">They <em>have been</em> married <em>for 20 years</em>.</p>
</div>

We say: They **are** married. *(present — true now)*

But we do **not** say *They are married for 20 years*. To say **how long**, we use the present perfect:

<SayIt text="They have been married for 20 years." />

<Callout type="warning" title="The classic mistake">
  ✅ How long <strong>have</strong> they <strong>been</strong> married? &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ How long <strong>are</strong> they married? <br />
  ✅ They <strong>have been</strong> married for 20 years. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ They <strong>are</strong> married for 20 years.
</Callout>

We use the **present perfect** to talk about something that began in the past and **still continues now**. Compare the present and the present perfect:

| Now (present) | How long? (present perfect) |
|---|---|
| Paul **is** in hospital. | He'**s been** in hospital **since Monday**. *(not Paul is in hospital since Monday)* |
| We **know** each other very well. | We'**ve known** each other **for a long time**. *(not We know each other for a long time)* |
| **Do** they **have** a car? | **How long have** they **had** their car? |
| She'**s waiting** for somebody. | She **hasn't been waiting** very long. |

<SayIt text="Paul has been in hospital since Monday." /> &nbsp; <SayIt text="We've known each other for a long time." /> &nbsp; <SayIt text="How long have you had that car?" />

## B — Simple (I've known) or continuous (I've been waiting)?

Two shapes of the present perfect turn up in this unit:

| Form | Looks like | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| **Present perfect simple** | I'**ve known** · I'**ve had** · I'**ve lived** | states; verbs not used in the continuous |
| **Present perfect continuous** | I'**ve been learning** · I'**ve been waiting** · it'**s been raining** | actions and 'how long' (more usual — see Unit 10) |

When we ask or say **how long**, the **continuous** is usually the natural choice:

<SayIt text="I've been learning English since January." /> &nbsp; <SayIt text="It's been raining all morning." /> &nbsp; <SayIt text="Richard has been doing the same job for 20 years." />

<Callout type="info" title="Some verbs don't take the continuous">
  Verbs like <strong>know</strong>, <strong>like</strong>, <strong>have</strong> (= possess), <strong>believe</strong> are not normally used in the continuous (see Unit 4A). With these, use the simple even for 'how long': <br />
  ✅ How long <strong>have</strong> you <strong>known</strong> Jane? &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ How long have you been knowing Jane? <br />
  ✅ I'<strong>ve had</strong> these shoes for ages. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ I've been having these shoes for ages.
</Callout>

## C — live and work: either form

With **live** and **work** you can use the continuous **or** the simple — there's no real difference in meaning:

<SayIt text="Julia has been living in this house for a long time." /> &nbsp; <SayIt text="Julia has lived in this house for a long time." />
<br />
<SayIt text="How long have you been working here?" /> &nbsp; <SayIt text="How long have you worked here?" />

**But** with **always**, we use the **simple** only:

<Callout type="warning" title="always → simple, never continuous">
  ✅ I'<strong>ve always lived</strong> in the country. &nbsp;&nbsp; ❌ I've always been living in the country.
</Callout>

## D — I haven't done ... since / for ...

To say that something has **not** happened for a period of time, use the **present perfect simple** with `since` or `for`:

<SayIt text="I haven't seen Tom since Monday." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= Monday was the last time I saw him — and I still haven't seen him.)</p>

<SayIt text="Sarah hasn't phoned for ages." />
<p className="text-sm text-slate-500 mt-1">(= the last time she phoned was a long time ago.)</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="The whole picture">
  A situation that <strong>started in the past and is still going on now</strong> lives in the <strong>present perfect</strong>, not the present simple — whether it's something that <em>is</em> happening (<em>I've been waiting</em>), something that <em>is true</em> (<em>I've known her for years</em>), or something that <em>hasn't</em> happened (<em>I haven't seen Tom since Monday</em>).
</Callout>
